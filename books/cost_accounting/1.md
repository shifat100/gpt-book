## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

1. **Define** cost accounting using the modern Horngren–Datar–Rajan formulation and **distinguish** it from cost management and strategic cost management.
2. **Explain** the six business functions in the value chain and **analyse** how cost information flows across them to support strategic cost management.
3. **Compare** financial accounting, cost accounting, and management accounting across seven dimensions: primary users, focus, rules, time horizon, behavioural implications, verification, and scope.
4. **Apply** the cost-benefit approach to evaluate whether a proposed accounting information system or report is economically justified.
5. **Illustrate** why different cost figures are required for different purposes—external reporting, operational control, strategic pricing, and short-run special-order decisions.
6. **Describe** the organisational position of the cost accountant within the corporate financial hierarchy, distinguishing line authority from staff authority.
7. **Evaluate** the professional and ethical obligations of the cost accountant under the IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice and the Bangladeshi statutory framework administered by ICMAB.

**Prerequisite Knowledge:** Elementary financial accounting (recording transactions, preparing financial statements), basic microeconomics (cost concepts, marginal analysis), and familiarity with the organisational structure of a business enterprise.

## 1.1 The Modern Definition of Cost Accounting

### 1.1.1 From Record-Keeping to Resource Measurement

The traditional view of cost accounting confined it to the ascertainment of product cost—the accumulation of material, labour, and overhead costs for inventory valuation. This view is incomplete. Horngren, Datar, and Rajan define **cost accounting** as:

> *“the process of measuring, analyzing, and reporting financial and nonfinancial information relating to the costs of acquiring or using resources in an organization.”*

Three features of this definition deserve emphasis. **First**, cost accounting measures both **financial information** (e.g., the taka cost of raw materials) and **nonfinancial information** (e.g., machine-hours consumed, defect rates, customer-response time, or kilogrammes of pharmaceutical active ingredient wasted). **Second**, it is not merely a recording function: *analyzing* is central. The cost accountant interprets cost behaviour, separates value-adding from non-value-adding costs, and identifies the drivers of cost. **Third**, the information is assembled for *use*—by managers inside the organisation—rather than solely for external attestation.

Cost accounting is therefore the **information infrastructure of managerial decision-making**. It supplies the cost data that feed into pricing, product-mix decisions, capital budgeting, performance evaluation, and cost control. Without reliable cost information, a manager is navigating without instruments.

### 1.1.2 Cost Accounting, Cost Management, and Strategic Cost Management

A distinction that Horngren and colleagues draw sharply is between **cost accounting** and **cost management**. Cost accounting is the *measurement and reporting* activity. **Cost management** describes “the approaches and activities of managers to use resources to increase value to customers and to achieve organizational goals.” Cost management is an *action* concept; cost accounting is its *informational handmaiden*. An accounting system can produce exquisite cost reports while management fails to act on them—in which case cost management has not occurred.

**Strategic cost management** narrows the focus further: it “describes cost management that specifically focuses on strategic issues.” Strategic issues include deciding which products to emphasise in the long run, whether to outsource a value-chain activity, how to position the firm against competitors on cost or differentiation, and how to configure the supply chain for resilience. Strategic cost management asks not “How much did this cost?” but “Given our strategy, *should* we be incurring this cost, and *where* in the value chain can cost be structurally reduced or value enhanced?”

### 1.1.3 The Value Chain: Six Business Functions

The **value chain** is “the sequence of business functions in which customer usefulness is added to products or services.” Horngren, Datar, and Rajan identify **six primary business functions**, to which administrative functions provide support:

| # | Business Function | Core Activity | Illustrative Cost Information Required |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Research and Development (R&D)** | Generating and experimenting with ideas for new products, services, or processes | Cost per research project; expected cost of developing a new chemical entity; R&D cost as a percentage of sales |
| 2 | **Design of Products and Processes** | Detailed planning, engineering, and testing of products and processes | Target cost versus estimated design cost; cost of design changes; value-engineering savings |
| 3 | **Production** | Procuring, transporting, storing, coordinating, and assembling resources to produce a product or deliver a service | Direct material, direct labour, overhead; conversion cost; cost per equivalent unit; scrap and rework cost |
| 4 | **Marketing** | Promoting and selling products or services to customers or prospective customers | Cost of acquiring a customer; advertising cost per taka of sales; sales-force compensation cost |
| 5 | **Distribution** | Processing orders and shipping products or services to customers | Outbound freight cost per unit; warehousing cost; order-processing cost; last-mile delivery cost |
| 6 | **Customer Service** | Providing after-sales service to customers | Warranty cost per unit; cost of field service calls; customer-retention cost; cost of returns |

**Administrative functions**—accounting, human resource management, information technology, legal, and corporate governance—support all six primary functions. The cost accountant must map costs across the *entire* value chain, not merely the production function. A decision that appears cost-effective in production (e.g., using a cheaper raw material) may impose higher costs in customer service (e.g., increased warranty claims), and value-chain costing exposes these trade-offs.

> **Strategic Insight.** Value-chain analysis shifts the cost accountant’s gaze *upstream* (towards suppliers and R&D) and *downstream* (towards distribution and after-sales service). In a pharmaceutical firm, for example, the cost of a batch failure detected at the R&D stage is vastly lower than the cost of a product recall detected after distribution. Strategic cost management uses such structural cost drivers to prevent, rather than merely record, cost.

## 1.2 Distinguishing Cost Accounting from Financial Accounting and Management Accounting

Students frequently conflate the three branches. They are related—all draw on the same transactional data—but they serve different purposes and operate under different constraints.

**Financial accounting** “focuses on reporting to external parties such as banks and suppliers. It measures and records business transactions and provides financial statements that are based on generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP).” **Management accounting** “measures, analyzes and report[s] financial and nonfinancial information that helps managers make decisions to fulfill the goals of an organization.” **Cost accounting** is the measurement and reporting of resource costs that feeds both financial accounting (for inventory valuation) and management accounting (for decision support).

### 1.2.1 Comprehensive Comparison Table

| Comparison Criterion | Financial Accounting | Cost Accounting | Management Accounting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary users** | External parties: shareholders, creditors, lenders, regulators, tax authorities | Internal managers *and* external statutory authorities (where cost audit is mandated, e.g., under Bangladesh’s Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997) | Internal managers at all levels |
| **Focus / emphasis** | Past-oriented; reliable recording of completed transactions; stewardship | Cost ascertainment, cost control, and cost reduction; product/service costing; statutory cost audit | Future-oriented; planning, decision-making, performance evaluation, strategy implementation |
| **Rules / standards** | Mandatory GAAP/IFRS; Companies Act 1994; BSEC regulations | Bangladesh Cost Accounting Standards (BCAS) issued by ICMAB where applicable; Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997 for prescribed sectors; otherwise managerial discretion | No mandatory GAAP; governed by relevance and cost-benefit judgment |
| **Time orientation** | Historical (reporting on the past period) | Historical cost recording *plus* standard costs and budgets for control | Primarily forward-looking (budgets, forecasts, expected costs) |
| **Reporting frequency** | Periodic and statutory (annual, half-yearly, quarterly as prescribed) | As required by management; monthly cost statements; statutory cost audit report annually for prescribed companies | As needed—daily, weekly, monthly, or ad hoc for decisions |
| **Aggregation level** | Highly aggregated: entity-level financial statements | Segmented by product, process, department, cost centre, or activity | Segmented and flexible: by product, customer, channel, segment, responsibility centre |
| **Verification / audit** | Mandatory external statutory audit | Cost audit by a CMA where mandated (e.g., 88 companies in sugar, fuel & power, jute, textile, pharmaceuticals, and fertilizer); internal review otherwise | No mandatory external audit; internal validation and managerial scrutiny |
| **Behavioural implications** | Influences external perceptions and contractual consequences | Can influence cost-centre behaviour through standard costing and variance reporting | Directly designed to influence managerial behaviour (motivation, accountability, incentive alignment) |

**Key takeaway:** Financial accounting asks *“What happened, and does it conform to the rules?”* Cost accounting asks *“What does it cost, and is that cost controlled and competitive?”* Management accounting asks *“What information does a manager need to make a better decision?”* The cost accountant lives at the intersection of all three questions.

## 1.3 The Cost-Benefit Approach to Decision Making

### 1.3.1 The Economic Rationale

Horngren and colleagues insist that every accounting information system—every report, every data capture, every costing model—be subjected to the **cost-benefit approach**:

> *“Resources should be spent if the expected benefits to the company exceed the expected costs.”*

This is not a slogan; it is an operational test. Suppose a firm is considering installing a real-time activity-based costing system that would cost Tk 80 lakh per annum to operate. The system is justified only if the expected benefits—better pricing decisions, reduced cross-subsidisation between products, lower overhead waste—exceed Tk 80 lakh in present-value terms. If the benefits are speculative or unquantifiable, the system may be an expensive indulgence.

The cost-benefit approach has two corollaries. **First**, *more information is not always better*. Beyond a point, additional detail imposes comprehension and processing costs on managers that outweigh the value of the extra precision. **Second**, *the design of the accounting system itself must be subjected to economic scrutiny*. The cost accountant who designs an elaborate variance report that nobody reads has violated the cost-benefit principle as surely as the production manager who wastes raw material.

### 1.3.2 Technical versus Behavioural Considerations

Implementing an accounting system is not a purely technical exercise. Horngren emphasises that **behavioural considerations** are as important as technical ones. A technically perfect system that managers distrust, game, or ignore will fail. Key behavioural issues include:

- **Goal congruence:** Does the system reward managers for actions that serve the organisation’s goals, or does it create perverse incentives? A cost centre that is evaluated solely on cost minimization may cut preventive maintenance and shift costs into future periods.
- **Motivation and fairness:** Variance reports that blame managers for uncontrollable cost overruns demotivate. Controllability must be respected in performance evaluation.
- **Participation:** Managers who participate in budget-setting are more likely to internalise the targets than those on whom budgets are imposed.
- **Resistance to change:** A new costing system may threaten established power relationships or expose inefficiencies that some managers prefer to conceal. The cost accountant must anticipate and manage such resistance.

The technically correct answer is a *necessary* condition for a sound accounting system; it is not a *sufficient* one. The behavioural dimension determines whether the system is actually used.

## 1.4 Different Costs for Different Purposes

### 1.4.1 Why a Single Cost Figure Cannot Satisfy All Needs

The most important conceptual lesson in introductory cost accounting is that **“different costs for different purposes”**. A single, universally correct product cost does not exist. Cost is a *purpose-dependent* construct. The cost figure that is appropriate for valuing inventory under IFRS is not the figure that is appropriate for deciding whether to accept a special export order at a price below the normal selling price.

Three reasons explain this multiplicity:

1. **Different decisions require different time horizons.** Long-run pricing decisions must recover all costs, including fixed costs and a normal return on capital. A short-run special order may be accepted at any price above incremental variable cost if idle capacity exists.
2. **Different purposes demand different cost inclusions.** Financial reporting requires absorption costing (fixed production overhead is inventoried). Operational control may use flexible budgets and standard variable costs. Strategic decisions may require full value-chain costing, including R&D and customer-service costs that GAAP treats as period expenses.
3. **Different cost objects require different cost allocations.** The cost of a product, a customer, a distribution channel, and a responsibility centre are all legitimate cost objects, but they require different allocation bases and different treatment of joint and common costs.

### 1.4.2 Specific Purpose Variations

| Purpose | Appropriate Cost Concept | Illustrative Application |
|---|---|---|
| **External financial reporting** | Absorption cost (direct materials + direct labour + variable and fixed production overhead); period costs expensed | Valuing closing inventory and cost of goods sold under IFRS/GAAP |
| **Routine operational control** | Standard variable cost; flexible-budget cost; controllable cost at the responsibility-centre level | Monthly variance analysis: material price and usage variances, labour efficiency variances |
| **Strategic long-run pricing** | Full value-chain cost plus a target return; life-cycle cost | Setting the launch price of a new pharmaceutical product after recovering R&D and regulatory-approval costs |
| **Short-run special-order decision** | Incremental / relevant cost (variable cost plus any incremental fixed cost); opportunity cost of using scarce capacity | Accepting an export order at a price below normal domestic price when idle capacity exists |
| **Customer profitability analysis** | Cost-to-serve, including order-processing, distribution, and after-sales service costs | Deciding whether to retain or renegotiate terms with a large but low-margin distributor |
| **Statutory cost audit (Bangladesh)** | Cost as prescribed by the Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997 and applicable BCAS, often absorption-based with detailed cost-sheet formats | Filing the annual cost audit report for a listed pharmaceutical or textile company |

> **Pedagogical Warning.** Students often ask, “Which cost is the *real* cost?” The answer is: the real cost is the one that is *fit for the decision at hand*. A cost figure that is perfectly accurate for one purpose may be dangerously misleading for another.

## 1.5 Organisation Structure and the Cost Accountant

### 1.5.1 Line Authority versus Staff Authority

Organisational authority is of two kinds. **Line authority** is “the authority to command action or give orders to subordinates.” A production manager who directs machine operators, a sales manager who directs sales representatives, and a chief executive who directs divisional heads all exercise line authority. They are directly responsible for attaining the organisation’s operating objectives.

**Staff authority** is “the authority to advise but not command.” Staff managers provide specialised expertise and support to line managers. The cost accountant—typically located within the controller’s function—exercises staff authority. The controller does not order the production manager to reduce material usage; the controller *advises*, *measures*, and *reports* material usage so that the production manager can act. As Horngren’s framework notes, the controller “does not exercise control in terms of line authority, except over his or her own department”; the controller’s influence flows through information, analysis, and persuasion.

### 1.5.2 Comparison Table: Line Authority versus Staff Authority

| Dimension | Line Authority | Staff Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Authority to command action and give orders to subordinates | Authority to advise and provide specialised services; no power to command outside one’s own department |
| **Primary responsibility** | Directly responsible for achieving operating objectives (production, sales, delivery) | Supports line managers with expertise, information, and analysis |
| **Typical holders** | CEO, production manager, sales manager, divisional head | Controller, cost accountant, HR manager, legal counsel, internal auditor |
| **Relationship to cost accountant** | The cost accountant *serves* line managers with cost information; line managers *act* on it | The cost accountant *is* a staff function; exercises line authority only within the accounting/finance department |
| **Basis of influence** | Positional power and command hierarchy | Technical competence, credibility of information, and quality of advice |

### 1.5.3 The Corporate Financial Hierarchy

The modern corporate finance function is headed by the **Chief Financial Officer (CFO)**, who typically oversees two major sub-functions: **controllership** and **treasury**. The **Controller** is the chief management accounting officer—responsible for cost accounting, budgeting, financial planning and analysis, and internal reporting. The **Treasurer** is responsible for cash management, financing, banking relationships, and capital structure. The **Internal Auditor** provides independent appraisal of internal controls and risk management, reporting functionally to the audit committee of the board. The **Cost Accountant** sits within the controller’s organisation and is the specialist responsible for cost measurement, cost analysis, and cost reporting.

The reporting lines may be depicted as follows:

**CFO** → **Controller** → **Cost Accountant**  
**CFO** → **Treasurer**  
**Audit Committee** ← **Internal Auditor** (functional reporting)

### 1.5.4 The Evolving Role of the Cost Accountant

The cost accountant’s role has undergone a profound transformation. The traditional **scorekeeping** function—recording transactions, preparing cost sheets, and computing variances after the fact—remains necessary but is increasingly automated. Enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems now capture transactional data at source, and routine cost reports can be generated without manual intervention.

The contemporary cost accountant is expected to be a **strategic business partner**: interpreting cost data, modelling the cost consequences of alternative strategies, challenging assumptions, and participating in cross-functional decision teams. The IMA’s *Management Accounting Glossary* describes the management accountant as “a professional who is involved in the management of an organisation’s resources and who contributes to the development and implementation of strategy.” In Bangladesh, ICMAB’s practising CMAs are engaged not only in statutory cost audit but also in transfer pricing, value-chain analysis, business-process re-engineering, project feasibility studies, and performance-monitoring systems.

This evolution does not diminish the importance of technical rigour. A strategic business partner who cannot construct a reliable product cost is useless. The modern cost accountant must combine **technical mastery** (costing methods, variance analysis, activity-based costing, standard costing) with **strategic perspective** (understanding competitive dynamics, value-chain economics, and the information needs of senior management).

## 1.6 Cost Accounting as a Profession and Professional Ethics

### 1.6.1 Professional Certification Landscape

Cost accounting is a recognised profession with established certification pathways. The two most globally significant are:

- **CMA (USA)**—Certified Management Accountant, administered by the **Institute of Management Accountants (IMA)**. The CMA credential emphasises management accounting, financial planning, performance management, and strategic decision support.
- **CIMA (UK)**—Chartered Institute of Management Accountants, offering the CGMA (Chartered Global Management Accountant) designation in partnership with the AICPA. CIMA’s syllabus is strongly oriented towards business strategy, risk, and enterprise performance.

In Bangladesh, the corresponding professional body is **ICMAB**.

### 1.6.2 Institutional Authority in Bangladesh: ICMAB and Statutory Cost Audit

The **Institute of Cost and Management Accountants of Bangladesh (ICMAB)** is a statutory organisation constituted under the **Cost and Management Accountants Ordinance 1977** (Ordinance No. LIII of 1977), which has since been replaced by the **Cost and Management Accountants Act 2018** (Act No. LXX of 2018). ICMAB regulates the CMA profession in Bangladesh, prescribes professional education and continuing professional development, and issues **Bangladesh Cost Accounting Standards (BCAS)** to guide cost measurement and cost audit reporting.

A distinctive feature of the Bangladeshi regulatory landscape is the **statutory mandate for cost audit** in specified sectors. Under the **Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997** (promulgated by the Ministry of Commerce, with subsequent amendments), cost audit is mandatory for prescribed companies. ICMAB’s official documentation records that cost audit covers **88 companies** across **sugar, fuel and power, jute, textile, pharmaceuticals, and fertilizer** sectors. The rules prescribe the form and content of cost accounting records and the cost audit report, and only a Cost and Management Accountant within the meaning of the governing legislation is eligible to be appointed as a cost auditor.

Cost audit in Bangladesh serves multiple public-interest objectives: it detects errors, fraud, and misappropriation; it assists the government in determining whether an industry requires tariff protection; it supports VAT authorities in determining value-added tax liability and detecting evasion; and it provides management with an independent appraisal of cost efficiency. The cost accountant in a prescribed company therefore operates under a dual obligation: to management for internal decision support, and to the statutory framework for independent attestation of cost records.

### 1.6.3 The IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice

Professional ethics is not an optional adornment of the cost accounting profession; it is constitutive of it. The cost accountant’s output influences prices, investment decisions, executive compensation, and—where statutory cost audit applies—government policy and public revenue. Ethical failure corrupts all of these.

The **IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice** establishes four **standards** that members are obligated to uphold: **Competence, Confidentiality, Integrity, and Credibility**. These are underpinned by overarching principles of **Honesty, Fairness, Objectivity, and Responsibility**.

### 1.6.4 Summary Table: The Four Ethical Standards of the IMA

| Standard | Specific Operational Obligations |
|---|---|
| **Competence** | Maintain an appropriate level of professional leadership and expertise by enhancing knowledge and skills; perform professional duties in accordance with relevant laws, regulations, and technical standards; provide decision-support information that is accurate, clear, concise, and timely; recognise and help manage risk; communicate professional limitations that would preclude responsible judgment. |
| **Confidentiality** | Keep information confidential except when disclosure is authorised or legally required; inform all relevant parties regarding appropriate use of confidential information and monitor compliance; refrain from using confidential information for unethical or illegal advantage. |
| **Integrity** | Mitigate actual conflicts of interest and regularly communicate with business associates to avoid apparent conflicts; advise all parties of potential conflicts; refrain from engaging in conduct that would prejudice carrying out duties ethically; abstain from supporting any activity that might discredit the profession; place the integrity of the profession above personal interests. |
| **Credibility** | Communicate information fairly and objectively; disclose all relevant information that could reasonably be expected to influence an intended user’s understanding; report delays or deficiencies in information, timeliness, processing, or internal controls in conformance with organisation policy and applicable law; communicate professional limitations or constraints. |

### 1.6.5 Resolving Ethical Dilemmas: The IMA Protocol

The IMA Statement does not leave the member to navigate ethical crises by intuition. It prescribes a **resolution protocol**:

1. **Follow the organisation’s established policies**, including any anonymous reporting or whistleblower mechanism.
2. **Discuss the issue with the immediate supervisor**, *unless* the supervisor is involved—in which case, present the issue to the next level of management.
3. If satisfactory resolution is not achieved, **escalate to successively higher levels of management**, ultimately to the audit committee or board if necessary.
4. **Consult IMA’s anonymous ethics helpline** or an independent, objective adviser to clarify the application of the ethical standards.
5. **Consider consulting personal legal counsel** to understand legal obligations, rights, and risks.
6. If resolution efforts fail, **consider disassociating from the organisation**—resignation may be the only remaining ethical option.

The protocol recognises that whistleblowing carries risks. The member is advised to “consider all risks involved and whether protections exist against retaliation” before escalating. Ethical courage is not recklessness; it is principled action informed by a realistic assessment of consequences.

## 1.7 Bangladesh Corporate Vignette: Cost Management and Statutory Audit at a Gazipur Composite Textile Exporter

**Background.** *Meghna Composite Textiles Ltd.* (a fictionalised composite knitwear exporter based in Gazipur) manufactures and exports knitwear to European and North American buyers. The company is a public limited company and falls within the prescribed sectors for statutory cost audit under the Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997. Its finance function is headed by a CFO, with a Controller and an ICMAB-qualified Cost Accountant, Ms. Farhana Rahman, FCMA.

**The Problem.** Meghna’s export margins had been eroding for three consecutive years. The CFO suspected that the company’s traditional absorption-costing system, which allocated all overheads on a single direct-labour-hour base, was distorting product costs. Low-volume, high-complexity knitwear styles appeared profitable on paper, while high-volume basic styles appeared marginal. Buyers were squeezing prices on the basic lines, and the company was losing market share without understanding why.

**The Cost Accountant’s Intervention.** Ms. Rahman initiated a three-part cost management programme:

1. **Value-chain mapping.** She mapped costs across the six business functions. Design and sampling costs (a value-chain function upstream of production) were being buried in production overhead and spread uniformly across all styles. In reality, complex styles consumed disproportionate design and sampling resources. Distribution costs (downstream) were similarly heterogeneous: small, frequent orders to European boutique buyers cost far more per unit to serve than large container shipments to North American retailers.
2. **Activity-based costing (ABC) pilot.** She implemented ABC for the design, production, and distribution functions, using activity drivers such as number of design changes, number of machine setups, and number of customer orders. The ABC analysis revealed that the basic high-volume lines were actually profitable and cross-subsidising the complex low-volume lines. This finding was strategically significant: it justified a rationalisation of the product portfolio and a renegotiation of pricing with buyers of complex styles.
3. **Statutory cost audit readiness.** Because Meghna is a prescribed company, Ms. Rahman simultaneously strengthened the cost accounting records to comply with the Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997 and the applicable BCAS. She ensured that the cost sheets were consistent with the ABC insights while maintaining the statutory absorption-cost format required for the cost auditor’s report.

**Outcome.** The ABC analysis led Meghna to discontinue three persistently loss-making complex styles and to negotiate a price increase on two retained styles. Export margin improved by 240 basis points within eighteen months. The statutory cost audit was completed without qualification, and the cost auditor commended the improved granularity of the cost records. Ms. Rahman’s dual role—strategic adviser to management and statutory compliance officer—illustrates the contemporary identity of the Bangladeshi cost accountant: *technically rigorous, strategically engaged, and ethically anchored*.

## 1.8 Practical Ethical Dilemma Scenario: Capitalising Advertising Costs to Meet Profit Targets

**The Situation.** You are the Cost Accountant at *Padma Consumer Products Ltd.*, a listed FMCG company in Dhaka. The company’s annual profit target, which determines management bonuses, is Tk 12 crore. At the end of the third quarter, it is clear that profit before advertising expense will be Tk 11.2 crore, but the company has incurred Tk 1.5 crore of advertising costs for a major brand relaunch. If these costs are expensed as period costs (the correct treatment under IFRS and the Companies Act 1994), profit will fall to Tk 9.7 crore—well below target. The CFO instructs you to **capitalise the advertising costs as “brand development assets”** and amortise them over five years, arguing that the relaunch will generate benefits over that period.

**Why This Is an Ethical Problem.** The instruction violates several standards simultaneously. It violates **Competence** by departing from applicable accounting standards and the technical requirements of financial reporting. It violates **Integrity** by misrepresenting the economic substance of the transaction to achieve a personal or organisational advantage (management bonuses). It violates **Credibility** by communicating financial information that is not fair and objective and by failing to disclose information that would influence users’ understanding of the company’s performance. The cost accountant is being asked to convert a period expense into an asset—a classic mechanism for profit manipulation.

**Resolution Using the IMA Protocol.**

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **1. Clarify the facts and the ethical issues** | Confirm that the advertising costs do not meet the definition of an intangible asset under IAS 38 (they are not separable, not controlled, and do not meet the recognition criteria). Confirm that the CFO’s instruction is motivated by the profit target. |
| **2. Check organisational policy** | Review the company’s accounting policy manual and whistleblower policy. If a policy exists requiring compliance with IFRS, cite it. If an anonymous ethics hotline exists, consider using it if direct discussion fails. |
| **3. Discuss with the immediate supervisor** | The immediate supervisor is the CFO—the person issuing the instruction. Because the CFO is involved, the protocol requires escalation to the next level of management: the **Chief Executive Officer**. Present the technical analysis, the accounting standards, and the consequences of misstatement. |
| **4. Escalate if unresolved** | If the CEO supports the CFO, escalate to the **Audit Committee of the Board**, which has oversight of financial reporting and internal controls. Provide written documentation of the issue and the advice given. |
| **5. Consult independent resources** | Contact the **IMA ethics helpline** for guidance on applying the standards. Consult personal legal counsel regarding potential liability under the Companies Act 1994 and BSEC regulations. |
| **6. Document everything** | Maintain a contemporaneous written record of instructions received, advice given, and escalations made. This protects the cost accountant if retaliation occurs. |
| **7. Consider disassociation** | If the misstatement proceeds and the cost accountant is required to sign or certify the cost records, disassociation (resignation with a written explanation to the audit committee) may be the only ethically defensible course. |

**Learning Point.** The IMA protocol is not a ladder to be climbed mechanically; it is a structured framework for exercising ethical judgment under pressure. The cost accountant’s obligation is to the integrity of the information system, not to the convenience of senior management.

## 1.9 End-of-Chapter Exam Preparation Pack

### 1.9.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Q1. Define cost accounting according to Horngren, Datar, and Rajan. How does it differ from cost management?**

**Model Answer.** Cost accounting is “the process of measuring, analyzing, and reporting financial and nonfinancial information relating to the costs of acquiring or using resources in an organization.” It is an *information* function: measurement, analysis, and reporting. Cost management, by contrast, describes “the approaches and activities of managers to use resources to increase value to customers and to achieve organizational goals.” Cost management is an *action* function. Cost accounting supplies the informational inputs; cost management is the managerial use of those inputs to improve resource utilisation and competitive position. An organisation may have sophisticated cost accounting without effective cost management if managers fail to act on the information.

**Q2. What are the six business functions in the value chain? Why is value-chain analysis important for strategic cost management?**

**Model Answer.** The six functions are: (1) Research and Development, (2) Design of Products and Processes, (3) Production, (4) Marketing, (5) Distribution, and (6) Customer Service. Value-chain analysis is important because it compels the cost accountant to examine costs across the *entire* sequence of value-adding activities, not merely within the factory. A cost reduction in production that increases warranty costs (customer service) is not a net benefit. Strategic cost management uses value-chain analysis to identify structural cost drivers, to locate opportunities for cost reduction or value enhancement, and to understand how costs in one function affect costs in another. It also exposes the cost of upstream activities (R&D, design) and downstream activities (distribution, service) that traditional product costing may obscure.

**Q3. Explain the cost-benefit approach. Why is it insufficient to design an accounting system on technical grounds alone?**

**Model Answer.** The cost-benefit approach states that resources should be spent on an accounting information system only if the expected benefits exceed the expected costs. A system that produces more precise cost data but costs more to operate than the value of the decisions it improves is economically irrational. Technical considerations alone are insufficient because accounting systems are used by *people*. Behavioural considerations—goal congruence, motivation, fairness, participation, and resistance to change—determine whether a technically sound system will actually be used and trusted. A system that managers perceive as threatening or unfair may be ignored or gamed, destroying its value. Therefore, the cost accountant must design systems that are both economically justified and behaviourally acceptable.

**Q4. Why do different purposes require different cost figures? Give two contrasting examples.**

**Model Answer.** Cost is purpose-dependent. Different decisions involve different time horizons, different cost objects, and different treatment of fixed and common costs. For *external financial reporting*, absorption costing is required: fixed production overhead is inventoried and charged to cost of goods sold as products are sold. For a *short-run special-order decision* when idle capacity exists, the relevant cost is incremental variable cost (plus any incremental fixed cost); fixed overhead that will be incurred regardless of the order is irrelevant. Using absorption cost for the special-order decision would wrongly reject an order that covers variable cost and contributes to fixed costs. Conversely, using variable cost for long-run pricing would underprice products and fail to recover fixed costs and capital. The cost accountant must select the cost concept that fits the decision.

**Q5. Distinguish between line authority and staff authority. Where does the cost accountant sit in this distinction?**

**Model Answer.** Line authority is the authority to command action and give orders to subordinates; line managers are directly responsible for achieving operating objectives. Staff authority is the authority to advise and provide specialised services, without the power to command outside one’s own department. The cost accountant exercises staff authority. The cost accountant advises production, marketing, and other line managers by providing cost information, analysis, and recommendations, but does not order them to change their operations. Within the accounting/finance department, the cost accountant may exercise line authority over subordinate accounting staff.

**Q6. What are the four standards of the IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice? Briefly state the core obligation under each.**

**Model Answer.** (1) **Competence:** maintain professional expertise, perform duties in accordance with laws and technical standards, and provide accurate, clear, concise, and timely decision-support information. (2) **Confidentiality:** keep information confidential except when disclosure is authorised or legally required, and refrain from using confidential information for unethical or illegal advantage. (3) **Integrity:** mitigate conflicts of interest, refrain from conduct that would prejudice ethical duties, and place the integrity of the profession above personal interests. (4) **Credibility:** communicate information fairly and objectively, disclose all relevant information, and report delays or deficiencies in information or internal controls.

### 1.9.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions with Structural Outlines

**Essay Question 1.** *“Cost accounting is not a single, monolithic system but a portfolio of purpose-specific measurements.” Critically evaluate this statement with reference to the distinctions between financial accounting, cost accounting, and management accounting, and illustrate with examples from a manufacturing enterprise.*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** State the thesis—cost is a purpose-dependent construct; no single cost figure serves all managerial and external needs.
- **Section 1: The three branches distinguished.** Primary users, regulatory constraints, time orientation, aggregation level. Use the comparison table framework.
- **Section 2: The portfolio of cost measurements.** Absorption cost for external reporting; standard variable cost for operational control; full value-chain cost for strategic pricing; incremental cost for short-run decisions; cost-to-serve for customer profitability.
- **Section 3: Manufacturing illustrations.** Pharmaceutical firm: R&D cost (strategic) vs. production cost (operational) vs. inventory cost (statutory). Textile exporter: relevant cost for a special export order vs. full cost for long-run pricing.
- **Section 4: Implications for the cost accountant.** The cost accountant must be a *methodological pluralist*, fluent in multiple cost concepts and capable of matching the measurement to the decision.
- **Conclusion:** The strength of cost accounting lies precisely in its plurality; the danger lies in using the wrong cost for the wrong purpose.

**Essay Question 2.** *Discuss the organisational position and evolving role of the cost accountant in the modern corporation. How does the shift from “scorekeeping” to “strategic business partnering” affect the ethical responsibilities of the profession?*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** The cost accountant’s role has expanded from record-keeping to strategic advisory work; this expansion intensifies ethical demands.
- **Section 1: Traditional role.** Scorekeeping, cost sheet preparation, variance computation, statutory cost audit compliance. Staff authority within the controller’s function.
- **Section 2: Contemporary role.** Strategic business partner: value-chain analysis, ABC, target costing, transfer pricing, performance-monitoring systems. ICMAB’s practising CMAs in Bangladesh engage in these areas.
- **Section 3: The ethical intensification.** Greater involvement in decision-making creates greater exposure to conflicts of interest, pressure to manipulate cost figures, and temptations to subordinate professional judgment to managerial convenience. The IMA standards (Competence, Confidentiality, Integrity, Credibility) become more, not less, important.
- **Section 4: The Bangladeshi context.** Statutory cost audit under the Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997 imposes a public-interest dimension. The cost accountant in a prescribed company serves both management and the state; ethical failure has regulatory and macroeconomic consequences.
- **Conclusion:** Strategic business partnering without ethical anchoring is dangerous; the profession must integrate technical excellence, strategic engagement, and ethical courage.

**Essay Question 3.** *Explain the value chain and its six business functions. How can value-chain analysis improve cost management in a Bangladeshi pharmaceutical company subject to statutory cost audit?*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Define value chain and strategic cost management.
- **Section 1: The six functions.** R&D, design, production, marketing, distribution, customer service; administrative support functions.
- **Section 2: Value-chain costing vs. production-only costing.** Traditional costing focuses on the factory; value-chain costing captures upstream (R&D, design) and downstream (distribution, service) costs.
- **Section 3: Application to a Bangladeshi pharmaceutical company.** R&D and regulatory-approval costs are substantial; design of processes affects production yield and quality costs; distribution to rural markets is costly; customer service (adverse-event reporting, product returns) carries significant cost. Value-chain analysis identifies cross-functional trade-offs.
- **Section 4: Interaction with statutory cost audit.** The Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997 prescribe cost-sheet formats; value-chain analysis supplements statutory compliance with strategic insight. The cost accountant must satisfy both the statutory absorption-cost requirement and the managerial need for value-chain visibility.
- **Conclusion:** Value-chain analysis is not an alternative to statutory cost audit; it is a strategic overlay that enhances the cost accountant’s contribution to competitiveness.

**Essay Question 4.** *“The cost-benefit approach is the foundational economic principle of cost accounting, but it is incomplete without behavioural considerations.” Discuss.*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** State the cost-benefit principle; argue that economic rationality must be complemented by behavioural realism.
- **Section 1: The cost-benefit principle.** Resources spent on information systems should yield expected benefits exceeding expected costs. Corollaries: more information is not always better; the system itself must be economically justified.
- **Section 2: Behavioural considerations.** Goal congruence, motivation, fairness, participation, resistance to change, and the danger of gaming performance metrics.
- **Section 3: Examples of behavioural failure despite technical success.** A standard-costing system that punishes managers for uncontrollable variances; an ABC system that exposes inefficiencies and triggers political resistance; a budget process that demoralises managers who are not consulted.
- **Section 4: Implications for design.** The cost accountant must design systems that are both economically justified and behaviourally acceptable—technically sound and organisationally legitimate.
- **Conclusion:** Cost accounting is a socio-technical discipline; economic logic and behavioural insight are both indispensable.

**MCQ 1.** According to Horngren, Datar, and Rajan, cost accounting is best defined as:

**Answer: B.** Rationale: This is the textbook definition, encompassing financial and nonfinancial information, measurement, analysis, and reporting. Option A describes financial accounting; Option C describes compliance; Option D describes pricing, which is one *use* of cost information.

A. Research and Development
B. Design of Products and Processes
C. Human Resource Management
D. Customer Service

**Answer: C.** Rationale: Human Resource Management is an *administrative* (support) function, not one of the six primary value-chain functions. The six primary functions are R&D, design, production, marketing, distribution, and customer service.

A. Is future-oriented and unregulated.
B. Focuses on external reporting and is governed by mandatory GAAP/IFRS.
C. Is concerned only with variable costs.
D. Does not require any verification or audit.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Financial accounting serves external users and is governed by mandatory standards. Management accounting is primarily internal, future-oriented, and not subject to mandatory GAAP. Option D is false—financial statements are externally audited.

**MCQ 4.** The cost-benefit approach in accounting system design implies that:
A. The most detailed and precise accounting system is always the best.
B. Resources should be spent on an accounting system only if expected benefits exceed expected costs.

D. Cost accounting information should be provided free of charge to all managers.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: This is the definition of the cost-benefit approach. Option A violates the principle; Option C contradicts Horngren’s emphasis on behavioural considerations; Option D ignores the cost of producing information.

**MCQ 5.** For a short-run special-order decision when idle capacity exists, the most relevant cost is:
A. Full absorption cost per unit.
B. Incremental variable cost per unit plus any incremental fixed cost.
C. Average total cost per unit.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Relevant costing focuses on costs that differ between alternatives. Fixed costs that will be incurred regardless of the order are irrelevant. Full absorption cost (A) and average total cost (C) include unavoidable fixed costs and may lead to wrongly rejecting a profitable special order.

**MCQ 6.** The Controller in a typical corporate structure exercises:
A. Line authority over production managers.
B. Staff authority, advising line managers but not commanding them outside the controller’s own department.
C. No authority of any kind.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The Controller is a staff function. The Controller advises and provides information to line managers but does not exercise line authority over them. The Controller does have line authority within the controllership function.

**MCQ 7.** Which of the following is **not** one of the four standards of the IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice?
A. Competence
B. Profitability
C. Integrity
D. Credibility

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The four standards are Competence, Confidentiality, Integrity, and Credibility. Profitability is not an ethical standard.

**MCQ 8.** Under the IMA ethical resolution protocol, if the immediate supervisor is involved in the unethical issue, the member should:

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The protocol states that if the supervisor appears to be involved, the issue should be presented to the next level of management. Resignation (A) is a last resort; ignoring (C) is unethical; public disclosure (D) is not part of the protocol.

### 1.9.4 Applied Managerial Case Scenarios with Model Solutions

**Case Scenario 1: The Special Export Order**

*Rahim Textiles Ltd. produces cotton fabric. Normal capacity is 1,00,000 metres per month. Due to a seasonal downturn, the company is currently operating at 70% capacity (70,000 metres). The normal selling price is Tk 250 per metre. The full absorption cost is Tk 220 per metre, comprising variable cost of Tk 160 and fixed overhead absorption of Tk 60 per metre. A foreign buyer offers to purchase 15,000 metres at Tk 185 per metre. The order would require an additional machine setup costing Tk 1,50,000, which is a fixed cost specific to this order. Should the company accept the order?*

**Model Solution.**
Relevant cost analysis:
- Incremental revenue: 15,000 × Tk 185 = **Tk 27,75,000**
- Incremental variable cost: 15,000 × Tk 160 = **Tk 24,00,000**
- Incremental fixed cost (setup): **Tk 1,50,000**
- Total incremental cost: **Tk 25,50,000**
- **Incremental profit (contribution): Tk 2,25,000**

The full absorption cost of Tk 220 includes Tk 60 of existing fixed overhead per metre that will be incurred whether or not the order is accepted. Using full absorption cost would show a loss of Tk 35 per metre (Tk 185 – Tk 220), leading to rejection. But the relevant analysis shows a positive contribution of Tk 2,25,000. Because idle capacity exists and the order does not displace normal sales, the order should be **accepted** on financial grounds. Qualitative considerations—impact on domestic pricing, future bargaining power of the buyer, and potential labour fatigue—should also be assessed before final acceptance.

**Case Scenario 2: The Ethical Pressure on Cost Classification**

*You are the Cost Accountant at a listed pharmaceutical company. The company has incurred Tk 3 crore on a failed clinical trial for a new drug. The CFO instructs you to capitalise the expenditure as “product development cost” and amortise it over five years, arguing that the knowledge gained will benefit future research. Without capitalisation, the company will report a loss for the year. Using the IMA ethical framework, outline your response.*

**Model Solution.**
1. **Identify the ethical issue:** The instruction misclassifies a revenue expense (failed research) as an asset to avoid reporting a loss. This violates **Competence** (departure from IAS 38, which prohibits capitalising failed research), **Integrity** (misrepresenting economic substance), and **Credibility** (communicating misleading financial information).
2. **Follow organisational policy:** Check the company’s accounting policy and whistleblower policy. If the policy requires compliance with IFRS, cite it in discussions.
3. **Discuss with the supervisor:** The CFO is the source of the instruction. Because the CFO is involved, escalate to the **CEO** or, if necessary, the **Audit Committee**.
4. **Document the technical analysis:** Prepare a written memo explaining why the expenditure does not meet the definition of an intangible asset under IAS 38 and the consequences of misstatement for external users, lenders, and regulators.
5. **Consult independent resources:** Contact the IMA ethics helpline and, if necessary, legal counsel regarding potential liability under the Companies Act 1994 and BSEC rules.
6. **Consider disassociation:** If the misstatement proceeds and you are required to certify the accounts, refuse to certify and consider resignation with a written explanation to the Audit Committee.

## 1.10 Chapter Summary

Cost accounting is the measurement, analysis, and reporting of financial and nonfinancial information about the costs of acquiring and using organisational resources. It is distinct from cost management (the actions managers take to use resources effectively) and from strategic cost management (cost management focused on strategic issues). The value chain—comprising R&D, design, production, marketing, distribution, and customer service—provides the map along which costs are incurred and value is added.

Cost accounting differs from financial accounting (which serves external users under mandatory GAAP) and from management accounting (which serves internal decision-makers without mandatory rules). The cost accountant operates at the intersection, supplying absorption costs for external reporting, relevant costs for decisions, and standard costs for control.

The cost-benefit approach demands that accounting information systems be economically justified: expected benefits must exceed expected costs. But economic logic is insufficient; behavioural considerations—goal congruence, motivation, fairness, and resistance to change—determine whether a technically sound system will be used.

Different costs are appropriate for different purposes: absorption cost for external reporting, standard variable cost for operational control, full value-chain cost for strategic pricing, and incremental cost for short-run special orders. No single cost figure can satisfy all needs.

The cost accountant occupies a staff position within the controller’s function, exercising advisory authority rather than line command. The role is evolving from routine scorekeeping towards strategic business partnering, but technical rigour remains indispensable.

Professional ethics is constitutive of the cost accounting profession. The IMA Statement establishes four standards—Competence, Confidentiality, Integrity, and Credibility—and a resolution protocol for ethical dilemmas. In Bangladesh, ICMAB regulates the profession under the Cost and Management Accountants Act 2018, and statutory cost audit under the Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997 applies to 88 companies in sugar, fuel and power, jute, textile, pharmaceuticals, and fertilizer.

## 1.11 Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Cost accounting** | The process of measuring, analyzing, and reporting financial and nonfinancial information relating to the costs of acquiring or using resources in an organization. |
| **Cost management** | The approaches and activities of managers to use resources to increase value to customers and to achieve organizational goals. |
| **Strategic cost management** | Cost management that specifically focuses on strategic issues such as long-run competitive positioning and value-chain configuration. |
| **Value chain** | The sequence of business functions—R&D, design, production, marketing, distribution, and customer service—in which customer usefulness is added to products or services. |
| **Cost-benefit approach** | The principle that resources should be spent on an accounting information system only if the expected benefits exceed the expected costs. |
| **Line authority** | The authority to command action and give orders to subordinates; held by managers directly responsible for operating objectives. |
| **Staff authority** | The authority to advise and provide specialised services, without the power to command outside one’s own department. |
| **Controller** | The chief management accounting officer, responsible for cost accounting, budgeting, and internal financial reporting; exercises staff authority. |
| **Treasurer** | The finance executive responsible for cash management, financing, and banking relationships. |
| **Relevant cost** | A cost that differs between decision alternatives and is therefore pertinent to a specific decision. |
| **Absorption cost** | A product cost that includes direct materials, direct labour, and both variable and fixed production overhead. |
| **Statutory cost audit** | An independent examination of cost accounting records mandated by law—in Bangladesh, under the Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997 for prescribed sectors. |
| **ICMAB** | The Institute of Cost and Management Accountants of Bangladesh, the statutory professional body regulating the CMA profession in Bangladesh. |
| **IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice** | The ethical framework establishing the standards of Competence, Confidentiality, Integrity, and Credibility for management accountants. |
