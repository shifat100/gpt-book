## 2.1 Costs and Cost Terminology

### 2.1.1 The Definition of Cost

A **cost** is “a resource sacrificed or forgone to achieve a specific objective.” It is usually measured as the monetary amount that must be paid to acquire goods or services. This definition has three elements. First, a cost is a *sacrifice*—something of value (cash, labour time, machine capacity, or an opportunity) is given up. Second, the sacrifice is directed towards a *specific objective*: producing a unit, serving a customer, operating a department, or running a promotional campaign. Third, the sacrifice is *measured in monetary terms*, which permits aggregation, comparison, and reporting.

Two related terms require clarification:

- An **actual cost** is the cost incurred (a historical cost), as distinguished from a budgeted cost. It is the recorded, verifiable amount that has been paid or is payable.
- A **budgeted cost** (or standard cost) is the planned or expected cost for a future period or for a specified level of activity. Budgeted costs are forward-looking benchmarks against which actual costs are compared in variance analysis.

The distinction matters because managerial decisions often require comparing what a cost *should* be (budgeted) with what it *actually is* (actual). A favourable or unfavourable variance is the difference between the two.

### 2.1.2 The Cost Object

A **cost object** is “anything for which a separate measurement of costs is desired.” A cost object can be a product (a specific knitwear style), a service (a customer service call), a department (the dyeing department), a customer (a European retail buyer), a project (a new pharmaceutical launch), or an activity (machine setup). The choice of cost object is fundamental: it determines which costs are direct and which are indirect, and it shapes the entire design of the costing system.

> **Key Insight.** The same cost can be direct with respect to one cost object and indirect with respect to another. The salary of a dyeing-department supervisor is a *direct* cost of the dyeing department (it can be traced to that department) but an *indirect* cost of a specific knitwear style (it must be allocated across all styles processed in the department). The direct/indirect classification is therefore *cost-object-specific*, not an inherent property of the cost itself.

### 2.1.3 Cost Accumulation and Cost Assignment

Accounting for costs proceeds in two stages.

**Cost accumulation** is “the collection of cost data in some organised way by means of an accounting system.” Costs are accumulated by natural classification (materials, labour, fuel, advertising) or by activity (order processing, machine processing). Accumulation answers the question: *What costs have been incurred?*

**Cost assignment** is the general term for assigning accumulated costs to cost objects. It encompasses two distinct processes:

- **Cost tracing** is the assignment of *direct* costs to a cost object. Because a direct cost can be traced “in an economically feasible way,” the assignment is based on a causal, observable relationship (e.g., the kilograms of cotton yarn issued to a specific production order).
- **Cost allocation** is the assignment of *indirect* costs to a cost object. Because an indirect cost cannot be traced feasibly, it must be assigned using an allocation base (e.g., allocating factory rent on the basis of machine-hours or floor area).

The relationship may be summarised as:

**Cost Accumulation → Cost Assignment → {Cost Tracing (direct costs), Cost Allocation (indirect costs)}**

### 2.1.4 Direct Costs versus Indirect Costs

**Direct costs** of a cost object are costs that are related to the particular cost object and that can be traced to it in an economically feasible (cost-effective) way. Examples include:

- Cotton yarn consumed in producing a specific batch of knitwear (direct material).
- Wages of sewing-machine operators working on a specific style (direct labour).
- The salary of the dyeing-department supervisor, if the dyeing department is the cost object.

**Indirect costs** of a cost object are costs that are related to the particular cost object but cannot be traced to it in an economically feasible way. Examples include:

- Factory electricity consumed by all production departments.
- Depreciation of the factory building.
- Salary of the factory manager, who oversees multiple production lines.
- Lubricants and minor consumables used across the plant.

### 2.1.5 Comprehensive Comparison Table: Direct Costs versus Indirect Costs

| Dimension | Direct Costs | Indirect Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Related to the cost object and traceable to it in an economically feasible way | Related to the cost object but not traceable in an economically feasible way |
| **Assignment method** | Cost tracing | Cost allocation |
| **Basis of assignment** | Observable causal relationship (e.g., material requisition, time ticket) | Allocation base (e.g., machine-hours, labour-hours, floor area, direct material cost) |
| **Examples (manufacturing)** | Direct materials, direct manufacturing labour | Manufacturing overhead: factory rent, utilities, supervision, depreciation, indirect materials |
| **Examples (non-manufacturing)** | Cost of a salesperson’s company car assigned to that salesperson | Cost of the sales manager’s office assigned across all sales territories |
| **Dependence on cost object** | Classification depends on the chosen cost object | Classification depends on the chosen cost object |
| **Precision** | Higher precision; less arbitrary | Lower precision; allocation involves judgment |

### 2.1.6 Three Factors Affecting Direct/Indirect Classification

Horngren and colleagues identify three factors that determine whether a cost is classified as direct or indirect:

1. **Materiality of the cost in question.** A cost may be technically traceable but too small to justify the record-keeping expense. A packet of sewing needles used in a garment factory could, in principle, be traced to a specific order, but the cost of the administrative effort would exceed the informational benefit. Such a cost is treated as indirect. The cost-benefit principle (Chapter 1) governs the classification.
2. **Available information-gathering technology.** Advances in technology can convert an indirect cost into a direct cost. Barcode scanning and radio-frequency identification (RFID) now allow firms to trace materials and components to individual products with minimal manual effort, making previously indirect costs directly traceable.
3. **Design of operations.** The way a facility is organised affects traceability. If a factory is dedicated to a single product line, many costs that would otherwise be indirect (e.g., the factory manager’s salary) become direct costs of that product line. If the same facility produces multiple products, those costs remain indirect. **Contractual arrangements** (e.g., a contract specifying that a particular cost is chargeable to a specific project) can also influence classification.

## 2.2 Cost-Behaviour Patterns

**Cost behaviour** describes how a cost changes as the related level of activity or volume changes. Understanding cost behaviour is essential for planning, budgeting, and decision-making.

### 2.2.1 Variable Costs

**Variable costs** are costs that change in total in proportion to changes in the related level of total activity or volume. If activity doubles, total variable cost doubles; if activity falls by 30%, total variable cost falls by 30%. The **variable cost per unit remains constant** over the relevant range.

**Example.** Metairie Bicycles buys a handlebar at $52 for each bicycle assembled. The total handlebar cost is $52,000 when 1,000 bicycles are assembled and $182,000 when 3,500 bicycles are assembled. The total cost changes, but the cost per handlebar remains $52.

### 2.2.2 Fixed Costs

**Fixed costs** are costs that do not change in total for a given time period despite wide changes in the related level of total activity or volume. A factory’s annual rent, insurance, and depreciation of the building are typically fixed. However, **fixed cost per unit declines as activity increases**, because the same total fixed cost is spread over more units. This inverse relationship between activity and fixed cost per unit is the source of the unit-cost trap discussed in Section 2.3.

### 2.2.3 Comparison Table: Variable Costs versus Fixed Costs

| Dimension | Variable Costs | Fixed Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Behaviour in total** | Change in proportion to changes in activity or volume | Do not change in total for a given time period despite wide changes in activity or volume |
| **Behaviour per unit** | Remain constant (within the relevant range) | Decline as activity increases; rise as activity decreases |
| **Mathematical form** | \( \text{Total VC} = v \times Q \) (where \( v \) = variable cost per unit, \( Q \) = quantity) | \( \text{Total FC} = F \) (a constant) |
| **Examples** | Direct materials, direct labour (if paid per unit), sales commissions, packaging | Factory rent, property taxes, depreciation (straight-line), insurance, supervisory salaries |
| **Relevance to decisions** | Generally relevant for short-run decisions because they differ between alternatives | Often irrelevant for short-run decisions because they do not differ between alternatives (unless the decision changes capacity) |
| **Time horizon** | Variable in both short run and long run | Fixed only in the short run; all costs are variable in the long run because capacity can be adjusted |

### 2.2.4 Mixed (Semi-Variable) Costs

**Mixed costs** (also called semi-variable costs) are costs that have both a fixed and a variable component. A common example is a factory’s electricity bill: a fixed standing charge (demand charge) is incurred regardless of usage, plus a variable charge per kilowatt-hour consumed. Another example is a salesperson’s compensation: a fixed salary plus a commission on sales.

The mathematical form of a mixed cost is the **linear cost function**:

\[
y = a + bx
\]

where:
- \( y \) = total cost at activity level \( x \)
- \( a \) = total fixed cost (the intercept)
- \( b \) = variable cost per unit of activity (the slope)
- \( x \) = volume of activity or cost driver

**Graphic representation.** On a graph with activity on the horizontal axis and total cost on the vertical axis, a mixed cost appears as an upward-sloping straight line that does not pass through the origin. The intercept on the vertical axis is the fixed component \( a \); the slope of the line is the variable cost per unit \( b \). The line is valid only within the **relevant range**.

### 2.2.5 The Cost Driver

A **cost driver** is any factor whose change causes a change in total cost. It is the activity or volume measure that “drives” the cost. For direct materials, the cost driver is typically units produced or kilograms of material used. For machine maintenance, the cost driver might be machine-hours. For order-processing costs, the cost driver is the number of orders processed.

The operational significance of the cost driver is threefold. First, it provides the **basis for predicting** how costs will change when activity changes. Second, it supplies the **allocation base** for assigning indirect costs to cost objects. Third, it focuses managerial attention on the **root causes** of cost—managers can control cost only by managing its driver. A poorly chosen cost driver produces distorted product costs and misleading performance signals.

### 2.2.6 The Relevant Range

The **relevant range** is the band of activity within which the assumptions of cost behaviour—specifically, constant variable cost per unit and constant total fixed cost—are valid. Outside this range, the linear cost function is not a reasonable approximation of reality.

**Why fixed costs remain fixed only within the relevant range.** A fixed cost is fixed with respect to a given *capacity configuration*. A factory with one production shift and a certain number of machines can produce between, say, 10,000 and 50,000 units per month without changing its supervisory, rent, or insurance costs. If demand exceeds 50,000 units, the firm must add a second shift, hire additional supervisors, and perhaps lease additional space—at which point total fixed costs jump to a higher level. Conversely, if demand falls below 10,000 units, management may close a section of the plant, reducing fixed costs. The relevant range is therefore the range over which the *existing* capacity configuration remains unchanged.

**Graphic representation.** On a graph, the relevant range is the horizontal segment over which the fixed-cost line is flat at a given level. At the boundary of the relevant range, the fixed-cost line steps up (or down) to a new level, reflecting a change in capacity. Within each relevant range, the cost behaviour assumptions hold; across ranges, they do not.

> **Time dimension of the relevant range.** The relevant range is defined not only by volume but also by *time period*. A cost that is fixed in the short run (e.g., a one-year factory lease) becomes variable in the long run (the lease can be renegotiated or the factory can be relocated). This is why economists say that “all costs are variable in the long run.”

## 2.3 Relationships of Types of Costs: Total Costs, Unit Costs, and the Decision-Making Trap

### 2.3.1 Total Costs and Unit (Average) Costs

The **total cost** of a cost object is the sum of all costs assigned to it. The **unit cost** (average cost) is the total cost divided by the number of units:

\[
\text{Unit cost} = \frac{\text{Total cost}}{\text{Number of units}}
\]

If total cost comprises fixed and variable components, then:

\[
\text{Total cost} = F + vQ
\]

\[
\text{Unit cost} = \frac{F}{Q} + v
\]

where \( F \) is total fixed cost, \( v \) is variable cost per unit, and \( Q \) is the number of units. This formulation reveals the mathematical source of the unit-cost trap: as \( Q \) increases, the fixed component per unit \( F/Q \) declines, so the unit cost falls even though the variable cost per unit is unchanged.

### 2.3.2 The Critical Decision-Making Trap: Unitising Fixed Costs

Managers frequently make the error of treating a fully absorbed unit cost as if it were a variable cost—that is, they assume that if a product’s unit cost is, say, Tk 220, then discontinuing the product will save Tk 220 per unit. This is the **unitising trap**: the mistaken belief that the unit cost remains constant when activity changes, and that all costs embedded in the unit cost are avoidable if the product is dropped.

**Why the trap is dangerous.** Fixed costs are incurred to provide capacity. They do not disappear simply because one product line is discontinued, unless the discontinuation actually reduces the capacity requirement (e.g., a dedicated machine is sold, a dedicated supervisor is dismissed, or a dedicated factory is closed). If the fixed costs are *common* to multiple products, they will continue to be incurred after the discontinuation and will be reallocated to the remaining products, making them appear less profitable. The decision to discontinue should be based on **avoidable costs** (costs that will be eliminated if the product is dropped) and **lost contribution margin**, not on the fully absorbed unit cost.

**Illustration.** A firm produces three products. Product C shows a loss when full fixed overhead is allocated to it. Management proposes discontinuance. But if the fixed overhead allocated to C is common to all three products and will not be reduced, discontinuance will simply shift C’s fixed overhead onto A and B, possibly turning them from profitable to loss-making. The correct analysis compares the contribution margin lost (C’s revenue minus C’s *avoidable* variable and fixed costs) with the avoidable fixed costs saved. If the lost contribution exceeds the savings, discontinuance destroys value.

> **Decision Rule.** Never base a capacity, discontinuance, or special-order decision on a fully absorbed unit cost. Identify the costs that *differ* between the alternatives (relevant costs), and ignore costs that will be incurred regardless of the decision.

## 2.4 Types of Inventory across Business Sectors

### 2.4.1 Manufacturing-Sector Companies

Manufacturing companies purchase materials and components and convert them into finished goods. They typically report **three specific inventory accounts**:

1. **Direct Materials Inventory.** The cost of materials *awaiting use* in the production process. These are raw materials that have been purchased but not yet issued to production.
2. **Work-in-Process (WIP) Inventory.** The cost of products *partially completed* but not yet finished at the end of the accounting period. WIP includes direct materials issued, direct labour performed, and manufacturing overhead applied to units still on the production floor.
3. **Finished Goods Inventory.** The cost of *completed* products awaiting sale. These units have absorbed all manufacturing costs (direct materials, direct labour, and manufacturing overhead) and are ready for delivery to customers.

### 2.4.2 Merchandising-Sector Companies

Merchandising companies purchase tangible goods in finished form and resell them without further processing. They report a single inventory category: **Merchandise Inventory** (the cost of goods purchased and held for resale). There is no WIP inventory because no production occurs, and no direct materials inventory in the manufacturing sense.

### 2.4.3 Service-Sector Companies

Service companies provide services rather than tangible products. They generally do **not** hold inventory for sale. However, they may carry **supplies inventory** (e.g., medical supplies in a hospital, stationery in a law firm) that is consumed in providing the service. The cost of services is calculated as the cost of labour, materials, and overhead consumed in delivering the service, but these costs are not stored as inventory in the way manufactured goods are.

## 2.5 Classifications of Manufacturing Costs

### 2.5.1 Inventoriable (Product) Costs versus Period Costs

**Inventoriable costs** (also called **product costs**) are “added to units of product (‘inventoried’) as they are incurred and are not treated as expenses until the units are sold.” For a manufacturing company, all manufacturing costs—direct materials, direct manufacturing labour, and manufacturing overhead—are inventoriable costs. They flow through the inventory accounts (Direct Materials → WIP → Finished Goods) and appear on the balance sheet as assets until the finished goods are sold, at which point they are expensed as Cost of Goods Sold on the income statement.

**Period costs** are “expensed in the time period in which they are incurred.” All selling and administrative costs are typically period costs. They are not attached to units of product and do not flow through inventory accounts. Examples include advertising expense, sales commissions, administrative salaries, and office rent.

The timing difference is crucial: an inventoriable cost incurred in January may not appear on the income statement until June (when the product is sold), whereas a period cost incurred in January is expensed in January.

### 2.5.2 Comparison Table: Inventoriable (Product) Costs versus Period Costs

| Dimension | Inventoriable (Product) Costs | Period Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Costs added to units of product and expensed only when the units are sold | Costs expensed in the period incurred |
| **Components (manufacturing)** | Direct materials, direct manufacturing labour, manufacturing overhead | Selling (marketing) costs, administrative costs |
| **Accounting flow** | Balance Sheet (Inventory) → Income Statement (COGS when sold) | Income Statement (expensed immediately) |
| **Timing of expense recognition** | Matched with revenue when the product is sold (accrual/matching principle) | Recognised in the period incurred |
| **Effect on profit** | Profit is deferred until sale; inventory is an asset | Profit is reduced immediately |
| **Examples** | Cotton yarn, sewing labour, factory electricity, depreciation of factory machinery | Advertising, sales salaries, office administration, delivery expenses |

### 2.5.3 Prime Costs and Conversion Costs

Two further classifications are useful for cost management:

- **Prime costs** are the sum of **direct materials** and **direct labour**. These are the “first” or primary costs of production, directly traceable to the product.

\[
\text{Prime Cost} = \text{Direct Materials} + \text{Direct Labour}
\]

- **Conversion costs** are the sum of **direct labour** and **manufacturing overhead**. These are the costs of *converting* direct materials into finished goods.

\[
\text{Conversion Cost} = \text{Direct Labour} + \text{Manufacturing Overhead}
\]

**Clarifying the overlap: Direct Labour.** Direct labour appears in *both* prime cost and conversion cost. This is not an error; the two classifications serve different analytical purposes. Prime cost measures the directly traceable input costs. Conversion cost measures the cost of transforming materials into finished products. A manager concerned with material and labour cost control focuses on prime cost; a manager concerned with the efficiency and cost of the production process (including overhead) focuses on conversion cost.

### 2.5.4 Comparison Table: Prime Costs versus Conversion Costs

| Dimension | Prime Costs | Conversion Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Formula** | Direct Materials + Direct Labour | Direct Labour + Manufacturing Overhead |
| **Components** | Direct materials, direct labour | Direct labour, manufacturing overhead |
| **Analytical focus** | Directly traceable input costs | Cost of transforming materials into finished goods |
| **Overlap** | Direct labour is included | Direct labour is included |
| **Typical use** | Material and labour cost control; pricing in labour-intensive industries | Process efficiency; overhead control; pricing in capital-intensive industries |

## 2.6 Different Meanings of Product Costs

A single product cost figure cannot serve all purposes. The cost accountant must construct **purpose-specific product costs**:

1. **Product costs for pricing and product-mix decisions.** Pricing decisions require a cost that covers all costs—variable and fixed—plus a target return. In the long run, prices must recover the full cost of the value chain (including R&D, design, production, marketing, distribution, and customer service). For product-mix decisions, the relevant cost may be the contribution margin per unit of the constrained resource (e.g., contribution per machine-hour), not the fully absorbed cost.
2. **Product costs for reimbursement under government or cost-plus contracts.** When a firm supplies goods or services under a cost-plus contract, the contract typically specifies which costs are reimbursable and the basis of allocation. The cost accountant must construct a cost that complies with the contractual definition, which may differ from the GAAP product cost.
3. **Product costs for financial statements under GAAP/IFRS.** External financial reporting requires **absorption costing**: inventoriable product cost includes direct materials, direct labour, and both variable and fixed manufacturing overhead. Period costs (selling and administrative) are expensed. This is the cost that appears in the Statement of Cost of Goods Manufactured and the Income Statement.

The key lesson is that the *same* production process can yield *different* product cost figures depending on the purpose. The cost accountant must be explicit about which cost concept is being used and why.

## 2.7 A Framework for Cost Accounting: Calculating Cost of Products, Services, and Cost Objects

### 2.7.1 The Comprehensive Cost Flow Schedule

The flow of manufacturing costs through the inventory accounts can be represented as follows:

**Direct Materials Inventory**  
Beginning Direct Materials + Purchases = Direct Materials Available for Use  
Direct Materials Available for Use − Ending Direct Materials = **Direct Materials Used**

**Work-in-Process Inventory**  
Direct Materials Used + Direct Manufacturing Labour + Manufacturing Overhead = **Total Manufacturing Costs Incurred**  
Total Manufacturing Costs Incurred + Beginning WIP − Ending WIP = **Cost of Goods Manufactured (COGM)**

**Finished Goods Inventory**  
Beginning Finished Goods + COGM = Cost of Goods Available for Sale  
Cost of Goods Available for Sale − Ending Finished Goods = **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)**

### 2.7.2 Detailed Statement of Cost of Goods Manufactured (COGM)

The Statement of Cost of Goods Manufactured reports the total manufacturing costs for the period that were added to work-in-process, and the cost of goods completed during the period.

**Step-by-step mathematical structure:**

1. **Direct Materials Used:**
\[
\text{Direct Materials Used} = \text{Beginning Direct Materials} + \text{Purchases} - \text{Ending Direct Materials}
\]
(If indirect materials are included in purchases, they must be deducted.)

2. **Total Manufacturing Costs Incurred:**
\[
\text{Total Manufacturing Costs} = \text{Direct Materials Used} + \text{Direct Manufacturing Labour} + \text{Manufacturing Overhead}
\]

3. **Cost of Goods Manufactured:**
\[
\text{COGM} = \text{Total Manufacturing Costs} + \text{Beginning WIP} - \text{Ending WIP}
\]

### 2.7.3 Statement of Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

\[
\text{COGS} = \text{Beginning Finished Goods} + \text{COGM} - \text{Ending Finished Goods}
\]

The COGS is the cost of all goods *sold* during the period. It is reported as an expense on the income statement.

### 2.7.4 Functional Income Statement

The **functional income statement** (also called the absorption-costing income statement) classifies costs by function: manufacturing (cost of goods sold) versus non-manufacturing (selling and administrative).

**Structure:**

\[
\text{Revenue} - \text{Cost of Goods Sold} = \text{Gross Margin}
\]

\[
\text{Gross Margin} - \text{Selling and Administrative Expenses} = \text{Operating Income}
\]

This format is required for external financial reporting under GAAP/IFRS. It contrasts with the **contribution-margin income statement**, which classifies costs by behaviour (variable versus fixed) and is used for internal decision-making.

## 2.8 Bangladesh Manufacturing Vignette: Cost Flow at a Composite Knitwear Exporter in Narayanganj

**Background.** *Padma Knit Composite Ltd.* (a fictionalised composite knitwear manufacturer based in Narayanganj) operates an integrated facility: yarn dyeing, knitting, fabric dyeing and finishing, cutting, sewing, and packaging. It exports T-shirts and polo shirts to European and North American buyers. The company’s cost structure reflects the typical Bangladeshi composite RMG model: fabric-related costs (yarn, knitting, dyeing, finishing) account for the largest share of industrial cost, followed by accessories and labour; non-industrial costs include marketing, selling, and distribution.

**Cost data for the year (illustrative, in Tk ’000):**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Beginning direct materials inventory (yarn, dyes, accessories) | 8,000 |
| Purchases of direct materials | 42,000 |
| Ending direct materials inventory | 6,500 |
| Direct manufacturing labour (sewing operators, cutters) | 12,000 |
| Manufacturing overhead: factory electricity, depreciation, supervision, indirect materials, dyeing chemicals | 15,000 |
| Beginning WIP inventory | 3,000 |
| Ending WIP inventory | 4,200 |
| Beginning finished goods inventory | 5,500 |
| Ending finished goods inventory | 7,000 |
| Selling and administrative expenses (marketing, export documentation, office salaries) | 9,000 |
| Sales revenue | 92,000 |

**Step 1: Direct Materials Used.**
\[
8,000 + 42,000 - 6,500 = \mathbf{43,500}
\]

**Step 2: Total Manufacturing Costs.**
\[
43,500 \text{ (DM used)} + 12,000 \text{ (DL)} + 15,000 \text{ (MOH)} = \mathbf{70,500}
\]

**Step 3: Cost of Goods Manufactured.**
\[
70,500 + 3,000 - 4,200 = \mathbf{69,300}
\]

**Step 4: Cost of Goods Sold.**
\[
5,500 + 69,300 - 7,000 = \mathbf{67,800}
\]

**Step 5: Functional Income Statement.**

| | Tk ’000 |
|---|---:|
| Sales revenue | 92,000 |
| Less: Cost of goods sold | (67,800) |
| **Gross margin** | **24,200** |
| Less: Selling and administrative expenses | (9,000) |
| **Operating income** | **15,200** |

**Interpretation.** The cost accountant at Padma Knit can now analyse the composition of COGM. Direct materials (yarn, dyes, accessories) represent 61.7% of total manufacturing cost—consistent with the fabric-dominated cost structure of Bangladeshi knitwear exports. Manufacturing overhead (factory electricity, dyeing chemicals, depreciation, supervision) is 21.3%, and direct labour is 17.0%. The gross margin ratio is 26.3%, and operating margin is 16.5%. These figures feed into pricing negotiations with buyers, capacity planning (the relevant range of the sewing floor), and the statutory cost audit records required for a prescribed company.

## 2.9 Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Problems

### Problem 1: Direct versus Indirect Cost Identification

**Problem Statement.** Rahim Manufacturing produces two products: Product A and Product B. Identify each of the following costs as direct or indirect with respect to **Product A** and with respect to the **factory as a whole**. Explain your classification.

1. Cotton yarn used exclusively in Product A: Tk 50,000.
2. Salary of the factory manager, who oversees both product lines: Tk 80,000.
3. Depreciation of a machine used only for Product A: Tk 30,000.
4. Factory rent: Tk 1,20,000.
5. Wages of workers assembling Product A: Tk 60,000.

**Solution.**

| Cost | With respect to Product A | With respect to the Factory | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cotton yarn (exclusive to A) | Direct | Direct | Traceable to A in an economically feasible way; also traceable to the factory |
| Factory manager’s salary | Indirect | Direct | Cannot be traced to A alone; can be traced to the factory as a whole |
| Depreciation of A-specific machine | Direct | Direct | Dedicated to A; traceable to A |
| Factory rent | Indirect | Direct | Common to all products; traceable to the factory as a whole |
| Assembly wages for A | Direct | Direct | Traceable to A through time tickets |

**Key learning:** The direct/indirect classification depends on the cost object. The factory manager’s salary is indirect for Product A but direct for the factory.

### Problem 2: The Unit-Cost Decision Trap

**Problem Statement.** A company produces three products. Product C has the following annual data:

- Selling price per unit: Tk 100
- Variable cost per unit: Tk 60
- Units sold: 10,000
- Allocated fixed overhead: Tk 5,00,000 (allocated on the basis of sales value; total company fixed overhead is Tk 12,00,000)

The fully absorbed unit cost of C is:
\[
60 + \frac{5,00,000}{10,000} = 60 + 50 = \text{Tk } 110
\]
Product C therefore shows a loss of Tk 10 per unit (Tk 100 − Tk 110). Management proposes discontinuance. However, the fixed overhead allocated to C is common to all three products and will **not** be reduced if C is discontinued. Should C be discontinued?

**Solution.**
- Contribution margin per unit of C: \( 100 - 60 = \text{Tk } 40 \)
- Total contribution margin of C: \( 40 \times 10,000 = \text{Tk } 4,00,000 \)
- Avoidable fixed costs if C is discontinued: **Tk 0** (the allocated fixed overhead is common and unavoidable)
- Net disadvantage of discontinuance: \( 4,00,000 - 0 = \text{Tk } 4,00,000 \) of contribution lost.

**Decision:** Product C should **not** be discontinued on financial grounds. The apparent loss is an artefact of allocating common fixed costs. Discontinuance would reduce company profit by Tk 4,00,000, and the remaining fixed costs would be reallocated to Products A and B, making them appear less profitable.

### 2.11.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Q1. Define cost object, cost accumulation, and cost assignment. Distinguish between cost tracing and cost allocation.**

**Model Answer.** A cost object is anything for which a separate measurement of costs is desired (a product, department, customer, or activity). Cost accumulation is the collection of cost data in an organised way through the accounting system. Cost assignment is the process of assigning accumulated costs to cost objects, and it takes two forms: cost tracing (assigning direct costs that can be traced in an economically feasible way) and cost allocation (assigning indirect costs that cannot be traced feasibly, using an allocation base).

**Q2. What three factors affect whether a cost is classified as direct or indirect? Explain each briefly.**

**Model Answer.** (1) **Materiality:** a cost that is technically traceable but immaterial is treated as indirect because the cost of tracing exceeds the benefit. (2) **Information-gathering technology:** barcodes, RFID, and ERP systems can make previously indirect costs directly traceable. (3) **Design of operations:** a facility dedicated to one product makes many costs direct; a multi-product facility makes them indirect. Contractual arrangements can also specify cost classification.

**Q3. Explain the difference between variable cost behaviour in total and on a per-unit basis.**

**Model Answer.** Total variable cost changes in direct proportion to changes in activity: if activity doubles, total variable cost doubles. However, variable cost *per unit* remains constant within the relevant range. This is because each unit consumes the same amount of the variable input. For example, if each bicycle requires a Tk 52 handlebar, total handlebar cost rises with output, but the cost per handlebar stays at Tk 52.

**Q4. Why do fixed costs remain fixed only within the relevant range? What happens outside this range?**

**Model Answer.** Fixed costs are fixed with respect to a given capacity configuration. Within the relevant range, the existing plant, equipment, and supervisory structure can accommodate changes in volume without altering total fixed costs. Outside the relevant range, capacity must be expanded (or contracted): additional shifts are added, more space is leased, or more supervisors are hired, causing total fixed costs to step up (or down) to a new level. The relevant range is therefore bounded by the capacity configuration and by the time period.

**Q5. Explain the unit-cost decision trap. Why is it dangerous to base a product-discontinuance decision on fully absorbed unit cost?**

**Model Answer.** The unit-cost trap is the error of assuming that a fully absorbed unit cost represents an avoidable cost per unit. Fully absorbed cost includes allocated fixed overhead that may be common to multiple products and will not be eliminated if one product is discontinued. Basing discontinuance on a fully absorbed unit cost that shows a “loss” may lead to dropping a product that makes a positive contribution to common fixed costs. The correct approach compares the contribution margin lost with the avoidable fixed costs saved.

**Q6. Distinguish between inventoriable (product) costs and period costs, and explain the timing of their recognition as expenses.**

**Model Answer.** Inventoriable costs are manufacturing costs (direct materials, direct labour, manufacturing overhead) that are attached to units of product and carried as inventory on the balance sheet until the units are sold; they are then expensed as cost of goods sold. Period costs are non-manufacturing costs (selling and administrative) that are expensed in the period incurred. The timing difference arises from the matching principle: product costs are matched with the revenue they help generate, while period costs are recognised immediately.

## 2.12 Chapter Summary

A **cost** is a resource sacrificed to achieve a specific objective, measured monetarily. An **actual cost** is a historical cost; a **budgeted cost** is a planned cost. A **cost object** is anything for which a separate cost measurement is desired. Costs are **accumulated** by natural classification and then **assigned** to cost objects through **cost tracing** (direct costs) or **cost allocation** (indirect costs). Whether a cost is direct or indirect depends on the cost object and on three factors: materiality, information-gathering technology, and design of operations.

**Cost behaviour** describes how costs change with activity. **Variable costs** change in total in proportion to activity but remain constant per unit. **Fixed costs** are constant in total within the relevant range but decline per unit as activity rises. **Mixed costs** contain both fixed and variable components, represented by \( y = a + bx \). A **cost driver** is the activity measure that causes cost. The **relevant range** is the band of activity within which cost-behaviour assumptions hold; outside it, fixed costs step up or down as capacity changes.

**Total cost** is \( F + vQ \); **unit cost** is \( F/Q + v \). The **unit-cost trap** is the error of treating a fully absorbed unit cost as an avoidable cost per unit. Product-discontinuance and special-order decisions must be based on **avoidable costs** and **contribution margin**, not fully absorbed unit cost.

Manufacturing companies hold **three inventories**: direct materials, work-in-process, and finished goods. Merchandising companies hold merchandise inventory; service companies generally hold no inventory for sale. Manufacturing costs are classified as **inventoriable (product) costs**—direct materials, direct labour, and manufacturing overhead—which flow through inventory to COGS, and **period costs**—selling and administrative expenses—which are expensed immediately. **Prime cost** = direct materials + direct labour. **Conversion cost** = direct labour + manufacturing overhead. Direct labour overlaps both classifications.

Product costs have **different meanings for different purposes**: full value-chain cost for pricing, contractual cost for reimbursement, and absorption cost for GAAP/IFRS financial reporting. The **Statement of Cost of Goods Manufactured** calculates direct materials used, total manufacturing costs, and COGM after adjusting for WIP. The **Statement of Cost of Goods Sold** adjusts COGM for finished goods inventory. The **Functional Income Statement** reports gross margin and operating income.
