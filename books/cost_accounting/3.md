## 3.1 Basic Assumptions and Examples of Cost Functions

### 3.1.1 The Linear Cost Function

A **cost function** is a mathematical description of how a cost changes with changes in the level of an activity relating to that cost. The simplest and most widely used specification is the **linear cost function**:

\[
y = a + bx
\]

where:
- \( y \) = total cost (the **dependent variable**)
- \( a \) = total fixed cost (the **intercept** or **constant**)
- \( b \) = variable cost per unit of activity (the **slope coefficient**)
- \( x \) = activity level or **cost driver** (the **independent variable**)

This formulation captures variable, fixed, and mixed cost behaviour within a single equation. If \( a = 0 \), the cost is strictly variable. If \( b = 0 \), the cost is strictly fixed. If both \( a > 0 \) and \( b > 0 \), the cost is mixed (semi-variable). The USPTO technical documentation on cost behaviour confirms that “within the relevant range, linear cost functions (in the single cost driver case) can be described by a single constant or intercept (called \(a\)) and a single slope coefficient (called \(b\))”.

### 3.1.2 Two Fundamental Assumptions

Horngren and colleagues identify two assumptions that underpin cost-behaviour estimation:

1. **Cost behaviour is approximated by a linear function within the relevant range.** Outside the relevant range, the linear approximation may fail—fixed costs may step up, variable cost per unit may change due to quantity discounts or diminishing returns, and the relationship between cost and activity may become nonlinear.
2. **Variations in the level of a single activity (the cost driver) explain the variations in the related total costs.** This is the **single cost driver** assumption. It simplifies estimation but may sacrifice explanatory power when multiple factors jointly drive a cost.

### 3.1.3 Graphic Presentation of Cost Behaviour Patterns

| Cost Pattern | Equation | Total Cost Behaviour | Per-Unit Behaviour | Graph Shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Strictly variable** | \( y = bx \) | Changes in proportion to \( x \) | Constant at \( b \) | Straight line through the origin |
| **Strictly fixed** | \( y = a \) | Constant regardless of \( x \) | Declines as \( x \) increases (\( a/x \)) | Horizontal line at \( a \) |
| **Semi-variable (mixed)** | \( y = a + bx \) | Changes with \( x \) but not proportionally | Declines towards \( b \) as \( x \) increases | Straight line with intercept \( a \) |
| **Step-variable** | \( y = a_1 \) for \( x_1 \leq x < x_2 \); \( y = a_2 \) for \( x_2 \leq x < x_3 \); etc. | Constant within narrow ranges; jumps at thresholds | Declines within each step; resets at each jump | Staircase with narrow treads |
| **Step-fixed** | \( y = a_1 \) for a broad range; \( y = a_2 \) for the next broad range | Constant over wide ranges; large jumps at capacity thresholds | Declines over a wide range; sharp drop at capacity expansion | Staircase with broad treads |

**Step-variable costs** are costs that are fixed over *narrow* ranges of activity but variable over *wider* ranges—for example, the wages of a small team of maintenance workers who are hired in fixed increments. **Step-fixed costs** are fixed over *broad* ranges of activity—for example, factory rent, which remains constant until a second facility is leased.

## 3.2 Identifying Cost Drivers

### 3.2.1 The Cause-and-Effect Criterion

A **cost driver** is any factor whose change causes a change in total cost. The selection of a cost driver must satisfy the **cause-and-effect criterion**: there must be a logical, economically plausible causal relationship between the activity and the cost. Causality can arise from:

- **Physical relationship:** The cost driver physically causes the cost. Machine-hours drive machine depreciation and maintenance; kilograms of yarn drive material cost.
- **Contractual agreement:** A contract specifies that a cost is driven by a particular activity. An equipment lease may charge a fixed monthly fee plus a per-hour usage charge.
- **Implicit knowledge:** Operational experience indicates a causal link. Managers may know from experience that setup complexity (not merely setup hours) drives setup costs.

### 3.2.2 Economic Plausibility

Before any quantitative modelling, the cost analyst must establish **economic plausibility**—a logical economic link between the activity and the cost. A high \(R^2\) from regression does not, by itself, justify using a cost driver; the relationship must make economic sense. A spurious correlation (e.g., the number of employees named “Rahim” and total factory cost) may produce a statistically significant coefficient but is economically absurd. Economic plausibility is the first line of defence against data mining and model misspecification.

## 3.3 Cost Estimation Methods

Horngren, Datar, and Rajan identify **four cost estimation methods**: industrial engineering, conference, account analysis, and quantitative analysis. Each offers a different trade-off between precision, cost, and data requirements.

### 3.3.1 Industrial Engineering Method (Work-Measurement Method)

The **industrial engineering method** estimates cost functions by analysing the *physical* relationship between inputs and outputs. Time-and-motion studies, work-measurement techniques, and engineering specifications are used to determine how much material, labour, and machine time a product or process should require. The method is highly detailed and can be applied to new products or processes for which no historical cost data exist. However, it is **expensive and time-consuming**, and it may be impractical for complex operations with many joint products or rapidly changing technologies.

### 3.3.2 Conference Method

The **conference method** estimates cost functions by pooling the expert opinions of managers and employees from different departments—purchasing, production, engineering, marketing, and accounting. Cross-functional discussion generates a consensus estimate of the cost-activity relationship. The method is **fast and relatively inexpensive**, and it draws on operational knowledge that may not be captured in accounting records. Its primary limitation is **subjectivity**: estimates depend on the judgment and potential biases of the participants, and there is no statistical measure of reliability.

### 3.3.3 Account Analysis Method

The **account analysis method** estimates cost functions by examining each general ledger account and classifying it as variable, fixed, or mixed with respect to a chosen cost driver, based on the analyst’s judgment and institutional experience. For example, the analyst may classify direct materials as variable, factory rent as fixed, and factory electricity as mixed. The method is straightforward and uses readily available accounting data, but it relies on **qualitative judgment** and may misclassify costs when account definitions are ambiguous or when costs behave differently from their accounting classification.

### 3.3.4 Quantitative Analysis Methods

**Quantitative analysis methods** use formal statistical and mathematical techniques to estimate cost functions from historical data. They include the **high-low method**, **scattergraph analysis**, and **regression analysis**. These methods provide objective estimates and statistical measures of reliability (\(R^2\), \(t\)-statistics, standard errors), but they require sufficient, reliable historical data and can be misled by outliers, inflation, or structural changes in the business.

### 3.3.5 Comparison Table: Four Cost Estimation Methods

| Method | Data Requirements | Cost to Implement | Primary Advantage | Primary Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Industrial engineering** | Detailed physical input-output data; time-and-motion studies | High (specialised expertise, time) | Precise for new products; no reliance on historical data | Very expensive; time-consuming; may not capture inefficiencies |
| **Conference** | Expert judgment from cross-functional managers | Low | Fast; integrates operational knowledge | Subjective; no statistical reliability measure |
| **Account analysis** | General ledger account balances | Moderate | Uses available accounting data; transparent | Judgment-dependent; may misclassify costs |
| **Quantitative analysis** | Historical data on cost and cost driver; sufficient observations | Moderate to high (statistical expertise) | Objective; provides reliability statistics | Requires reliable data; sensitive to outliers and structural breaks |

## 3.4 Steps in Estimating a Cost Function Using Quantitative Analysis

Horngren’s six-step protocol structures the quantitative estimation process:

**Step 1: Choose the dependent variable (\(y\)).** The dependent variable is the cost to be predicted—machine maintenance cost, electricity cost, or distribution cost. It must be clearly defined and consistently measured.

**Step 2: Identify the independent variable or cost driver (\(x\)).** The cost driver must satisfy the cause-and-effect criterion and be economically plausible. Common drivers include machine-hours, direct labour-hours, units produced, and number of setups.

**Step 3: Collect data on the dependent variable and the cost driver.** Data may be drawn from accounting records, production reports, or engineering logs. The data period must be long enough to capture variation in the cost driver, and the observations must be reliable.

**Step 4: Plot the data on a scatter graph.** The scatter graph provides a visual check for linearity, identifies outliers, and reveals the relevant range. If the plotted points do not suggest a linear relationship, a linear cost function may be inappropriate. Outliers—observations that lie far from the general pattern—must be investigated before estimation; they may represent data errors, unusual events, or structural breaks.

**Step 5: Estimate the cost function using the high-low method or regression analysis.** The high-low method uses two data points (the highest and lowest *activity levels*); regression analysis uses all available observations and provides statistical diagnostics.

**Step 6: Evaluate the cost driver and the accuracy of the estimated function.** Evaluation rests on three criteria: **economic plausibility**, **goodness of fit**, and **significance of the independent variable**.

## 3.5 The High-Low Method

### 3.5.1 Mechanics

The **high-low method** estimates the fixed and variable components of a mixed cost by using only two data points: the observation with the **highest activity level** and the observation with the **lowest activity level**. Crucially, the points are selected on the basis of *activity*, **not** cost. As one instructional source states, “the two data points that will be used are selected by finding the high and low **activity levels** (NOT the cost levels)”.

**Formulas:**

\[
b = \frac{\text{Cost at Highest Activity} - \text{Cost at Lowest Activity}}{\text{Highest Activity Level} - \text{Lowest Activity Level}} = \frac{\Delta \text{Cost}}{\Delta \text{Activity}}
\]

\[
a = \text{Total Cost at Highest Activity} - (b \times \text{Highest Activity Level})
\]

Equivalently, \( a = \text{Total Cost at Lowest Activity} - (b \times \text{Lowest Activity Level}) \). Both yield the same result if the two points are used consistently.

### 3.5.2 Merits and Fatal Flaw

The high-low method is **mathematically simple** and requires only two data points, making it easy to apply and explain. Its **fatal flaw** is extreme vulnerability to **outlier distortion**. If either the highest-activity or lowest-activity observation represents an unusual period—a plant shutdown, a strike, a spike in input prices, or a data-recording error—the estimated slope and intercept will be biased. Moreover, by using only two observations, the method **ignores all intermediate data points**, discarding potentially valuable information about cost behaviour.

> **Practical caution.** The observation with the highest *activity* may not have the highest *cost*. If the highest-cost month has moderate activity due to an equipment breakdown, selecting points by cost rather than activity produces a meaningless estimate. Always select by activity.

## 3.6 Regression Analysis Method

### 3.6.1 Simple Linear Regression and the OLS Criterion

**Simple linear regression** estimates the cost function \(y = a + bx\) using all available observations. The **ordinary least squares (OLS)** criterion chooses the intercept and slope that minimise the sum of squared vertical deviations between observed costs and predicted costs:

\[
\text{Minimise} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - a - bx_i)^2
\]

The OLS solution yields the line that has the “smallest possible sample standard error of the estimate” among all linear unbiased estimators. The slope coefficient \(b\) measures the average change in cost associated with a one-unit change in the cost driver.

### 3.6.2 Statistical Evaluation of the Estimated Function

**Coefficient of determination (\(R^2\)).** \(R^2\) measures the proportion of the total variation in the dependent variable that is explained by the independent variable. It ranges from 0 to 1. An \(R^2\) of 0.85 means that 85% of the variation in cost is explained by variation in the cost driver. A low \(R^2\) signals that the cost driver has limited explanatory power, and the estimated function should be used with caution.

**\(t\)-statistic for the slope coefficient (\(b\)).** The \(t\)-statistic tests whether the estimated slope is statistically significantly different from zero:

\[
t = \frac{b}{S_b}
\]

where \(S_b\) is the standard error of the slope coefficient. A large absolute \(t\)-value (typically \(|t| > 2\) for a 5% significance level in large samples) indicates that the cost driver has a statistically significant relationship with cost. The associated **\(p\)-value** is the probability of observing a \(t\)-statistic at least as extreme as the computed value if the true slope were zero; a small \(p\)-value (e.g., \(p < 0.05\)) indicates statistical significance.

**Standard error of the estimate (\(S_e\)).** \(S_e\) measures the average vertical deviation of observed costs from the regression line. It is expressed in the same units as the dependent variable and indicates the precision of cost predictions. A smaller \(S_e\) implies more precise predictions.

### 3.6.3 Multiple Regression: A Brief Overview

**Multiple regression** extends simple regression to include more than one cost driver:

\[
y = a + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \cdots + b_k x_k
\]

For example, machine maintenance cost may be driven by both machine-hours (\(x_1\)) and number of setups (\(x_2\)). Multiple regression estimates the separate effect of each driver while holding the others constant. It is more realistic than simple regression when costs are jointly driven by several activities, but it requires more data and is susceptible to **multicollinearity**—high correlation among independent variables that inflates standard errors and destabilises coefficient estimates.

### 3.6.4 Comparison Table: High-Low Method versus Regression Analysis

| Dimension | High-Low Method | Regression Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| **Data used** | Two observations (highest and lowest activity) | All available observations |
| **Computational complexity** | Very low | Moderate to high |
| **Objective** | Objective but arbitrary selection of two points | Objective; OLS minimises squared deviations |
| **Reliability diagnostics** | None | \(R^2\), \(t\)-statistics, \(p\)-values, \(S_e\) |
| **Sensitivity to outliers** | Extremely high | Lower (all observations influence the fit) |
| **Best use** | Quick preliminary estimate; small data sets | Reliable estimation; hypothesis testing; multiple drivers |

## 3.7 Evaluating Cost Drivers of the Estimated Cost Function

Three criteria govern the acceptability of an estimated cost function:

1. **Economic plausibility.** Does the cost driver have a logical, causal connection to the cost? A statistically significant coefficient on an implausible driver is not a basis for managerial action.
2. **Goodness of fit.** How much of the variation in cost is explained by the driver? \(R^2\) and \(S_e\) provide the quantitative evidence, but a high \(R^2\) on an implausible driver remains suspect.
3. **Significance of the independent variable.** Is the slope coefficient statistically different from zero? The \(t\)-statistic and \(p\)-value answer this question. A driver that is not statistically significant should not be used for prediction.

## 3.8 Nonlinear Cost Functions

### 3.8.1 Economies of Scale, Quantity Discounts, and Step Costs

Not all costs behave linearly. **Economies of scale** cause average cost per unit to decline as volume increases, producing a concave total cost curve. **Quantity discounts** on purchases make the effective material price decline at higher order quantities, creating a piecewise-linear or concave cost function. **Step-cost functions**—step-variable and step-fixed costs—produce a staircase pattern, as described in Section 3.1.3.

### 3.8.2 Learning Curves and Experience Curves

**Learning curves** describe the systematic decline in labour time or cost per unit as cumulative output increases. Two models dominate:

**Cumulative average-time learning model (Wright’s model).** As cumulative output doubles, the **cumulative average time per unit** declines by a constant percentage. The model is:

\[
Y = aX^b
\]

where \(Y\) is the cumulative average time per unit, \(X\) is the cumulative number of units produced, \(a\) is the time required for the first unit, and \(b = \log(\text{learning rate}) / \log 2\).

**Incremental unit-time learning model (Crawford’s model).** As cumulative output doubles, the **time to produce the last unit** declines by a constant percentage. The model uses the same power function form, but \(Y\) represents the incremental unit time of the lot-midpoint unit rather than the cumulative average. Crawford’s model predicts a higher cumulative total time than Wright’s model for the same learning rate, because the learning effect applies to each successive unit rather than to the cumulative average.

### 3.8.3 Comparison Table: Cumulative Average-Time versus Incremental Unit-Time Model

| Dimension | Cumulative Average-Time (Wright) | Incremental Unit-Time (Crawford) |
|---|---|---|
| **What declines** | Cumulative *average* time per unit | Time to produce the *last* unit |
| **Function** | \(Y = aX^b\) | \(Y = aX^b\) (applied to incremental units) |
| **\(Y\) interpretation** | Cumulative average time (or cost) per unit | Incremental unit time (or cost) of the lot-midpoint unit |
| **Cumulative total time** | Lower (learning applies to the average) | Higher (learning applies to each successive unit) |
| **Primary use** | Continuous production; long production runs | Batch production; discrete contracts |
| **Key formula element** | \(b = \log(\text{learning rate}) / \log 2\) | \(b = \log(\text{learning rate}) / \log 2\) |

## 3.9 Data Collection and Adjustment Issues

Raw accounting and operational data are rarely ready for immediate estimation. The cost analyst must confront:

- **Extreme values and outliers.** An outlier may be a data error, an unusual event (strike, shutdown, natural disaster), or a legitimate observation from a different relevant range. Outliers must be investigated, not automatically deleted. If an outlier reflects a data error, correct or exclude it; if it reflects a genuine structural break, the cost function may need to be re-specified.
- **Price changes and inflation.** Historical cost data are expressed in nominal taka of different purchasing power. If input prices have changed, costs must be **deflated** to a common price level or the cost driver must be adjusted (e.g., using quantity rather than value).
- **Mismatched accounting time periods.** Costs recorded in one period may relate to activity in another (e.g., a quarterly insurance premium covering three months). The analyst must align cost and activity on a consistent time basis.
- **Missing records.** Incomplete data reduce sample size and may introduce selection bias. Missing observations must be reconstructed where possible or the estimation period adjusted.
- **Allocated fixed costs treated as variable.** A cost that is fixed in total but allocated per unit may appear variable in the accounts. The analyst must distinguish the underlying cost behaviour from its accounting presentation.

## 3.10 Bangladesh Corporate Vignette: Separating Machine Maintenance Costs at a Tongi Industrial Packaging Plant

**Background.** *Padma Packaging Industries Ltd.* operates a corrugated-box manufacturing plant in Tongi, Gazipur. The plant’s cost accountant, an ICMAB-qualified CMA, is analysing **machine maintenance costs** for the year. The plant manager believes maintenance cost is driven by **machine-hours**, but the accounts show substantial month-to-month variation that may reflect fixed contractual maintenance charges, variable spare-parts consumption, and seasonal electricity fluctuations. The cost accountant’s task is to separate the fixed and variable components to support the annual budget and a proposed outsourcing decision.

**Data (illustrative, 12 months):**

| Month | Machine-Hours (\(x\)) | Maintenance Cost (Tk) (\(y\)) |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 1,200 | 85,000 |
| February | 1,500 | 92,000 |
| March | 1,100 | 81,000 |
| April | 1,800 | 1,05,000 |
| May | 1,600 | 95,000 |
| June | 1,400 | 90,000 |
| July | 1,300 | 88,000 |
| August | 1,700 | 99,000 |
| September | 1,000 | 78,000 |
| October | 1,900 | 1,08,000 |
| November | 1,450 | 91,000 |
| December | 1,250 | 86,000 |

**Step 1: Identify high and low activity levels.**
- Highest activity: **1,900 machine-hours** (October), cost **Tk 1,08,000**.
- Lowest activity: **1,000 machine-hours** (September), cost **Tk 78,000**.

**Step 2: Compute variable cost per machine-hour (\(b\)).**

\[
b = \frac{1,08,000 - 78,000}{1,900 - 1,000} = \frac{30,000}{900} = \mathbf{Tk\ 33.33 \text{ per machine-hour}}
\]

**Step 3: Compute total fixed cost (\(a\)).**

Using the high point:

\[
a = 1,08,000 - (33.33 \times 1,900) = 1,08,000 - 63,333 = \mathbf{Tk\ 44,667}
\]

**Step 4: Cost function.**

\[
y = 44,667 + 33.33x
\]

**Interpretation and decision use.** The plant incurs approximately **Tk 44,667 of fixed maintenance cost per month** (contractual maintenance, standby technicians, fixed service charges) and **Tk 33.33 of variable cost per machine-hour** (spare parts, lubricants, variable electricity). If the plant expects **2,000 machine-hours** next month, the predicted maintenance cost is:

\[
y = 44,667 + (33.33 \times 2,000) = \mathbf{Tk\ 1,11,327}
\]

The cost accountant should verify this estimate against a scatter graph and, ideally, a regression using all twelve observations, because the high-low estimate relies on only two months. If October or September was an outlier—for example, if October included a one-time major overhaul—the estimate would be distorted. The CMA’s professional judgment, informed by economic plausibility and operational knowledge, is essential before the estimate is used in the outsourcing decision.

## 3.11 Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Problems

### Problem 1: Scatter Diagram Interpretation and Outlier Identification

**Problem Statement.** A company plots monthly production volume (\(x\)) against total electricity cost (\(y\)). The scatter graph shows a generally upward-sloping linear pattern, but one observation lies far above the pattern at moderate volume, and another lies far below the pattern at high volume. Identify the likely nature of these outliers and explain how the cost analyst should proceed.

**Solution.** The observation far **above** the pattern at moderate volume likely represents an unusual cost spike—perhaps a equipment malfunction, a heat wave requiring excessive cooling, or a data-recording error. The observation far **below** the pattern at high volume likely represents an unusual cost reduction—perhaps a plant shutdown that reduced activity without a proportional cost decline, or a government subsidy. Before deleting either observation, the analyst must investigate its cause. If it is a **data error**, correct or exclude it. If it reflects a **genuine unusual event**, it may be excluded from estimation but must be documented; the estimated cost function applies to normal operating conditions, not to abnormal events. If it reflects a **structural break** (a permanent change in the cost relationship), the model may need to be re-specified or estimated on a post-break sample.

### Problem 2: High-Low Method with Forecasting

**Problem Statement.** Using the data below, estimate the cost function using the high-low method and predict cost at **13,000 machine-hours**.

| Month | Machine-Hours | Cost (Tk) |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 15,500 |
| February | 15,000 | 16,233 |
| March | 12,750 | 18,186 |
| April | 13,268 | 19,125 |
| May | 9,256 | 11,159 |
| June | 14,639 | 19,931 |

**Solution.**

**Step 1: Identify high and low activity levels.**
- Highest activity: **15,000 machine-hours** (February), cost **Tk 16,233**.
- Lowest activity: **9,256 machine-hours** (May), cost **Tk 11,159**.

**Step 2: Compute \(b\).**

\[
b = \frac{16,233 - 11,159}{15,000 - 9,256} = \frac{5,074}{5,744} = \mathbf{Tk\ 0.8833 \text{ per machine-hour}}
\]

**Step 3: Compute \(a\).**

Using the high point:

\[
a = 16,233 - (0.8833 \times 15,000) = 16,233 - 13,250 = \mathbf{Tk\ 2,983}
\]

**Step 4: Cost function and forecast.**

\[
y = 2,983 + 0.8833x
\]

At \(x = 13,000\):

\[
y = 2,983 + (0.8833 \times 13,000) = 2,983 + 11,483 = \mathbf{Tk\ 14,466}
\]

### Problem 3: High-Low Trap—Highest Cost Does Not Correspond to Highest Activity

**Problem Statement.** Using the data below, apply the high-low method. Note that the highest *cost* does **not** occur in the month with the highest *activity*. Explain why selecting points by cost would be erroneous.

| Month | Machine-Hours | Cost (Tk) |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 15,500 |
| February | 15,000 | 16,233 |
| March | 12,750 | 18,186 |
| April | 13,268 | 19,125 |
| May | 9,256 | 11,159 |
| June | 10,335 | 15,117 |
| July | 13,295 | 18,998 |
| August | 12,652 | 17,874 |
| September | 9,964 | 10,685 |
| October | 10,865 | 16,853 |
| November | 11,569 | 17,365 |
| December | 14,639 | 19,931 |

**Solution.**

**Correct approach—select by activity:**
- Highest activity: **15,000 machine-hours** (February), cost **Tk 16,233**.
- Lowest activity: **9,256 machine-hours** (May), cost **Tk 11,159**.

\[
b = \frac{16,233 - 11,159}{15,000 - 9,256} = \frac{5,074}{5,744} = \mathbf{Tk\ 0.8833}
\]

\[
a = 16,233 - (0.8833 \times 15,000) = \mathbf{Tk\ 2,983}
\]

**Erroneous approach—select by cost:**
- Highest cost: **Tk 19,931** (December, 14,639 machine-hours).
- Lowest cost: **Tk 10,685** (September, 9,964 machine-hours).

\[
b_{\text{erroneous}} = \frac{19,931 - 10,685}{14,639 - 9,964} = \frac{9,246}{4,675} = \text{Tk } 1.978
\]

This slope is more than double the correct estimate. The erroneous approach produces a meaningless cost function because December’s high cost may reflect an unusual event (e.g., a year-end overhaul), not the normal cost of higher activity. **Always select by activity, not by cost**.

### Problem 4: Simple Linear Regression—Normal Equations and \(R^2\)

**Problem Statement.** For a small data set of five observations:

| \(x\) (machine-hours, ’00) | \(y\) (maintenance cost, Tk ’000) |
|---:|---:|
| 2 | 10 |
| 4 | 16 |
| 6 | 22 |
| 8 | 28 |
| 10 | 34 |

Set up the normal equations, calculate the slope \(b\) and intercept \(a\), and compute \(R^2\).

**Solution.**

**Step 1: Compute sums.**

| \(x\) | \(y\) | \(xy\) | \(x^2\) | \(y^2\) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 10 | 20 | 4 | 100 |
| 4 | 16 | 64 | 16 | 256 |
| 6 | 22 | 132 | 36 | 484 |
| 8 | 28 | 224 | 64 | 784 |
| 10 | 34 | 340 | 100 | 1,156 |
| **\(\sum x = 30\)** | **\(\sum y = 110\)** | **\(\sum xy = 780\)** | **\(\sum x^2 = 220\)** | **\(\sum y^2 = 2,780\)** |

\(n = 5\).

**Step 2: Normal equations.**

\[
\sum y = na + b\sum x \quad \Rightarrow \quad 110 = 5a + 30b
\]

\[
\sum xy = a\sum x + b\sum x^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 780 = 30a + 220b
\]

**Step 3: Solve for \(b\) and \(a\).**

Multiply the first equation by 6: \(660 = 30a + 180b\). Subtract from the second:

\[
120 = 40b \quad \Rightarrow \quad b = 3
\]

Substitute into the first: \(110 = 5a + 90 \Rightarrow 5a = 20 \Rightarrow a = 4\).

**Cost function:** \(y = 4 + 3x\) (in Tk ’000; i.e., fixed cost Tk 4,000; variable cost Tk 3,000 per 100 machine-hours).

**Step 4: Compute \(R^2\).**

\[
R^2 = \frac{\left[n\sum xy - (\sum x)(\sum y)\right]^2}{\left[n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2\right]\left[n\sum y^2 - (\sum y)^2\right]}
\]

\[
R^2 = \frac{[5(780) - (30)(110)]^2}{[5(220) - 30^2][5(2,780) - 110^2]} = \frac{(3,900 - 3,300)^2}{(1,100 - 900)(13,900 - 12,100)} = \frac{600^2}{200 \times 1,800} = \frac{360,000}{360,000} = \mathbf{1.00}
\]

\(R^2 = 1.00\) means the cost driver explains 100% of the variation in cost—a perfect linear fit (expected in this constructed example).

### Problem 5: Cumulative Average-Time Learning Curve (80% Learning Rate)

**Problem Statement.** The first unit of a new product requires **100 direct labour-hours**. An **80% cumulative average-time learning curve** applies. Calculate the cumulative average time per unit and the total time required for **1, 2, 4, and 8 units**.

**Solution.**

For an 80% learning curve, \(b = \log(0.80) / \log(2) = -0.3219\).

**Using the doubling rule (simpler for exact doublings):**

| Cumulative Output (\(X\)) | Cumulative Average Time per Unit | Total Time |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | **100.00 hours** | 100.00 hours |
| 2 | \(100 \times 0.80 =\) **80.00 hours** | \(80 \times 2 =\) **160.00 hours** |
| 4 | \(80 \times 0.80 =\) **64.00 hours** | \(64 \times 4 =\) **256.00 hours** |
| 8 | \(64 \times 0.80 =\) **51.20 hours** | \(51.20 \times 8 =\) **409.60 hours** |

**Interpretation.** Each doubling of cumulative output reduces the cumulative average time per unit to 80% of its previous level. The **total** time continues to increase, but at a decreasing rate.

### Problem 6: Incremental Unit-Time Learning Curve (80% Learning Rate)

**Problem Statement.** Using the same 80% learning rate and first-unit time of 100 hours, compute the **incremental time for the 2nd, 4th, and 8th units** under Crawford’s incremental unit-time model.

**Solution.**

Under Crawford’s model, the time for the \(X\)-th unit is:

\[
T_X = aX^b
\]

where \(b = \log(0.80) / \log(2) = -0.3219\).

- **Unit 1:** \(100\) hours (given).
- **Unit 2:** \(100 \times 2^{-0.3219} = 100 \times 0.80 =\) **80.00 hours**.
- **Unit 4:** \(100 \times 4^{-0.3219} = 100 \times 0.64 =\) **64.00 hours**.
- **Unit 8:** \(100 \times 8^{-0.3219} = 100 \times 0.512 =\) **51.20 hours**.

**Key contrast with Wright’s model.** Under Wright’s cumulative average model, the *cumulative average* time after 2 units is 80 hours, so total time for 2 units is 160 hours. Under Crawford’s model, the *second unit* takes 80 hours, so total time for 2 units is \(100 + 80 = 180\) hours—**higher** than Wright’s 160 hours. Crawford’s model predicts a higher cumulative total time, consistent with the theoretical property that the unit model applies learning to each successive unit rather than to the cumulative average.

### 3.13.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Q1. State the two fundamental assumptions of linear cost-behaviour estimation and explain why each is necessary.**

**Model Answer.** The two assumptions are: (1) cost behaviour is approximated by a linear function within the relevant range, and (2) variations in a single cost driver explain variations in total cost. Linearity simplifies estimation to two parameters (\(a\) and \(b\)) and permits prediction with a straight-line equation; outside the relevant range, step costs and nonlinearities may invalidate the approximation. The single-driver assumption makes estimation tractable but may sacrifice explanatory power when multiple activities jointly drive a cost.

**Q2. Distinguish between the industrial engineering method and the conference method of cost estimation. What are the primary strengths and weaknesses of each?**

**Model Answer.** The industrial engineering method analyses physical input-output relationships through time-and-motion studies; it is precise for new products but expensive and time-consuming. The conference method pools expert opinions from cross-functional managers; it is fast and inexpensive but subjective and lacks statistical reliability. The engineering method is preferable when detailed physical data are available and precision is critical; the conference method is preferable when speed and operational knowledge matter more than statistical rigour.

**Q3. Explain why the high-low method is vulnerable to outlier distortion. How can this weakness be mitigated?**

**Model Answer.** The high-low method uses only two observations—the highest and lowest activity levels. If either observation represents an unusual period (a strike, shutdown, price spike, or data error), the estimated slope and intercept are biased. The method also ignores all intermediate observations. The weakness can be mitigated by plotting a scatter graph first, investigating outliers, and using regression analysis (which uses all observations) when reliable data are available.

**Q4. What does \(R^2\) measure? How should a cost analyst interpret an \(R^2\) of 0.35?**

**Model Answer.** \(R^2\) measures the proportion of total variation in the dependent variable (cost) explained by the independent variable (cost driver). An \(R^2\) of 0.35 means that only 35% of the variation in cost is explained by the driver; 65% is unexplained. The analyst should be cautious: the driver may be weak, important drivers may be omitted, or the relationship may be nonlinear. A low \(R^2\) does not by itself invalidate the driver, but it limits confidence in predictions.

**Q5. Explain the difference between Wright’s cumulative average-time model and Crawford’s incremental unit-time model.**

**Model Answer.** Wright’s model assumes that the *cumulative average time per unit* declines by a constant percentage as cumulative output doubles. Crawford’s model assumes that the *time to produce the last unit* declines by a constant percentage as cumulative output doubles. For the same learning rate, Crawford’s model predicts a higher cumulative total time because learning applies to each successive unit rather than to the cumulative average.

**Q6. Why is economic plausibility essential before accepting a statistically significant regression result?**

**Model Answer.** A statistically significant coefficient may arise from a spurious correlation—a relationship with no causal economic link. Economic plausibility ensures that the cost driver has a logical, causal connection to the cost. Without plausibility, a high \(R^2\) or a large \(t\)-statistic may lead managers to base decisions on a meaningless model. Plausibility is the first filter; statistical significance is the second.

### 3.13.3 Multiple-Choice Questions with Answer Key and Rationales

**MCQ 1.** In the linear cost function \(y = a + bx\), the slope coefficient \(b\) represents:
A. Total fixed cost.
B. Variable cost per unit of activity.
C. Total cost at zero activity.
D. The activity level.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: \(b\) is the change in total cost per unit change in the cost driver—the variable cost per unit. \(a\) is total fixed cost (C).

**MCQ 2.** The high-low method selects the two data points based on:
A. The highest and lowest **cost** levels.
B. The highest and lowest **activity** levels.
C. The mean and median activity levels.
D. The most recent and oldest observations.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The method selects by activity, not by cost. Selecting by cost can produce a meaningless estimate if the highest-cost month has moderate activity.

**MCQ 3.** A primary limitation of the high-low method is:
A. It requires advanced statistical software.
B. It uses too many data points.
C. It is highly sensitive to outliers.
D. It cannot estimate fixed costs.

**Answer: C.** Rationale: Because only two points are used, an outlier at either extreme biases the estimate. The method is simple (not A), uses only two points (not B), and can estimate fixed costs (not D).

**MCQ 4.** In regression analysis, \(R^2 = 0.90\) means:
A. The cost driver is economically plausible.
B. 90% of the variation in cost is explained by the cost driver.
C. The slope coefficient is statistically significant.
D. The fixed cost is 90% of total cost.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: \(R^2\) measures explanatory power—the proportion of total variation explained. It does not establish plausibility (A) or significance (C).

**MCQ 5.** The \(t\)-statistic for the slope coefficient tests:
A. Whether the intercept is zero.
B. Whether the slope is statistically significantly different from zero.
C. Whether \(R^2\) is greater than 0.5.
D. Whether the standard error is minimised.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The \(t\)-statistic tests the null hypothesis that the true slope is zero—i.e., that the cost driver has no linear effect on cost.

**MCQ 6.** Under Wright’s cumulative average-time learning model, an 80% learning curve means that:
A. The time for each unit is 80% of the previous unit’s time.
B. The cumulative average time per unit is 80% of its previous level when cumulative output doubles.
C. Total cumulative time declines by 20% when output doubles.
D. The first unit takes 80% of the theoretical time.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Wright’s model applies the learning rate to the cumulative *average*, not to each individual unit (A) or to total time (C).

**MCQ 7.** Compared with Wright’s model, Crawford’s incremental unit-time model predicts:
A. A lower cumulative total time for the same learning rate.
B. The same cumulative total time.
C. A higher cumulative total time for the same learning rate.

**Answer: C.** Rationale: Crawford’s model applies learning to each successive unit, which yields a higher cumulative total time than Wright’s cumulative-average approach.

**MCQ 8.** Economic plausibility is important because:
A. A statistically significant relationship may be spurious.
B. It guarantees a high \(R^2\).
C. It eliminates the need for regression analysis.
D. It ensures the cost driver is perfectly correlated with cost.

**Answer: A.** Rationale: Plausibility guards against spurious correlations—statistically significant but economically meaningless relationships.

**MCQ 9.** A step-fixed cost is:
A. Fixed over a narrow range of activity and variable over a wider range.
B. Fixed over a broad range of activity, with large jumps at capacity thresholds.
C. Strictly variable with a constant slope.
D. A cost that declines continuously as activity increases.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Step-fixed costs remain constant over wide activity ranges and jump when capacity is expanded (e.g., factory rent). Step-variable costs (A) are fixed over narrow ranges.

**MCQ 10.** If an outlier is identified in a data set, the cost analyst should:
A. Always delete it immediately.
B. Investigate its cause before deciding whether to retain, correct, or exclude it.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Outliers must be investigated; they may be data errors, unusual events, or structural breaks. Automatic deletion (A) or uncritical inclusion (C) can bias the estimate.

## 3.14 Chapter Summary

A **cost function** is a mathematical description of how cost changes with activity. The **linear cost function** \(y = a + bx\) assumes that cost behaviour is linear within the **relevant range** and that a **single cost driver** explains variations in total cost. Costs may be strictly variable (\(y = bx\)), strictly fixed (\(y = a\)), mixed (\(y = a + bx\)), step-variable, or step-fixed.

A **cost driver** must satisfy the **cause-and-effect criterion** and be **economically plausible**. Four **cost estimation methods** exist: industrial engineering (precise but expensive), conference (fast but subjective), account analysis (uses ledger data but judgmental), and quantitative analysis (objective but data-dependent).

The **six-step protocol** for quantitative estimation proceeds from choosing the dependent variable to evaluating the estimated function. The **high-low method** uses the highest and lowest *activity* levels; it is simple but highly vulnerable to **outliers** and ignores intermediate observations. **Regression analysis** uses all observations and the **OLS criterion** to minimise squared deviations. Statistical evaluation relies on **\(R^2\)** (explanatory power), the **\(t\)-statistic** and **\(p\)-value** (significance of the slope), and the **standard error** (precision of predictions). **Multiple regression** accommodates several cost drivers.

**Learning curves** describe systematic cost declines with cumulative output. **Wright’s cumulative average-time model** reduces the cumulative average time per unit by a constant percentage as output doubles; **Crawford’s incremental unit-time model** reduces the time for the last unit by a constant percentage. Crawford’s model predicts higher cumulative total time for the same learning rate.

Data collection is fraught with **outliers, inflation, mismatched periods, missing records, and misclassified allocated costs**. Each must be addressed before estimation.

## 3.15 Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Cost function** | A mathematical description of how a cost changes with changes in the level of an activity. |
| **Linear cost function** | \(y = a + bx\); a straight-line relationship between total cost and a single cost driver. |
| **Relevant range** | The band of activity within which cost-behaviour assumptions (linearity, constant fixed cost, constant variable cost per unit) are valid. |
| **Cost driver** | Any factor whose change causes a change in total cost. |
| **Cause-and-effect criterion** | The requirement that a cost driver have a logical, causal relationship with the cost. |
| **Economic plausibility** | The existence of a logical economic link between the activity and the cost, independent of statistical fit. |
| **Industrial engineering method** | Cost estimation through analysis of physical input-output relationships (time-and-motion studies). |
| **Conference method** | Cost estimation by pooling expert opinions from cross-functional managers. |
| **Account analysis method** | Cost estimation by classifying general ledger accounts as variable, fixed, or mixed based on judgment. |
| **High-low method** | A two-point estimation technique using the highest and lowest activity levels. |
| **Regression analysis** | A statistical method that estimates the cost function using all observations and the OLS criterion. |
| **\(R^2\) (coefficient of determination)** | The proportion of total variation in the dependent variable explained by the independent variable. |
| **\(t\)-statistic** | A test statistic for whether the slope coefficient is statistically significantly different from zero. |
| **Learning curve** | The systematic decline in labour time or cost per unit as cumulative output increases. |
| **Wright’s cumulative average-time model** | \(Y = aX^b\); cumulative average time per unit declines by a constant percentage as cumulative output doubles. |
| **Crawford’s incremental unit-time model** | The time for the last unit declines by a constant percentage as cumulative output doubles. |
| **Outlier** | An observation that lies far from the general pattern of the data; may reflect error, unusual events, or structural breaks. |
