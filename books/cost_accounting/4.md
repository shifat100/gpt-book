## 4.1 Classification of Materials

### 4.1.1 Direct Materials versus Indirect Materials

**Materials** constitute the physical substance of a manufactured product. In cost accounting, materials are classified according to their traceability to the cost object:

**Direct materials** are materials that become an integral part of the finished product and that can be traced to the product in an economically feasible way. Cotton yarn in a textile mill, active pharmaceutical ingredients in a medicine, and steel sheets in an appliance factory are direct materials. They are charged directly to the cost of the product.

**Indirect materials** are materials used in the production process that cannot be traced economically to a specific product. They are treated as part of **manufacturing overhead** and allocated across products. Examples include:

- **Store supplies:** stationery, record books, and packaging labels used in the stores department.
- **Maintenance lubricants:** oils, greases, and cleaning agents used on machinery.
- **Packaging consumables:** adhesive tape, strapping, and minor packing materials that are not uniquely identifiable with a specific product.

Basu and Das emphasise that indirect materials, though physically consumed in operations, lack a direct causal trace to individual units and are therefore absorbed into overhead.

### 4.1.2 The Strategic Importance of Material Cost Control

In most manufacturing enterprises—and especially in Bangladeshi textile, pharmaceutical, and engineering firms—materials represent the single largest component of total cost. Uncontrolled material cost erodes margins, inflates working capital requirements, and undermines competitiveness. Strategic material cost control encompasses:

- **Procurement efficiency:** sourcing at competitive prices, negotiating favourable credit terms, and minimising landed cost through effective use of letters of credit and customs procedures.
- **Inventory optimisation:** holding neither too much (which ties up capital and incurs carrying costs) nor too little (which risks stockouts and production stoppages).
- **Waste reduction:** minimising normal loss, preventing abnormal loss, and recovering value from scrap and spoilage.
- **Information integrity:** maintaining accurate stores records that support product costing, budgetary control, and statutory cost audit.

## 4.2 Procurement of Materials and Operational Workflow

### 4.2.1 The Procurement Cycle

The procurement of materials follows a disciplined sequence of documents and controls designed to ensure that materials are purchased at the right price, in the right quantity, and of the right quality. Basu and Das describe the workflow as: purchase requisition → purchase order → goods received note (GRN) → inspection → stores ledger and bin card. The full cycle in a modern manufacturing enterprise is as follows:

| Step | Document / Activity | Purpose and Control |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Purchase Requisition** | Initiated by the stores department or production planning when inventory reaches the reorder level. Specifies quantity, quality, and required delivery date. Authorisation by a responsible officer prevents unnecessary purchases. |
| **2** | **Quotation / Tender Invitation** | The purchasing department invites quotations from approved suppliers. Competitive bidding secures the best price and terms. For large or recurring purchases, formal tenders may be invited. |
| **3** | **Comparative Statement** | Quotations are tabulated and compared on price, quality, delivery, and credit terms. The purchasing manager selects the most advantageous offer, considering total landed cost rather than invoice price alone. |
| **4** | **Purchase Order (PO)** | A formal contract specifying the quantity, description, price, delivery date, and terms of purchase. The PO is the supplier’s authority to deliver and the company’s commitment to pay. |
| **5** | **Bill of Entry / Inbound Freight** | For imported materials, the **Bill of Entry** is the customs document that records the assessable value, customs duty, and other levies. Inbound freight costs are added to the material cost to arrive at **landed cost**. |
| **6** | **Goods Received Note (GRN)** | Prepared by the receiving department when goods arrive. Records the quantity received, supplier, PO reference, and date. The GRN triggers the stores ledger entry and the accounting payable. |
| **7** | **Inspection / Quality Control Report** | Materials are inspected for quality and specification compliance. Rejected materials are returned to the supplier via a **Material Returned Note**; accepted materials are transferred to stores. |
| **8** | **Material Returned Note** | Documents the return of rejected, excess, or incorrect materials to the supplier. Triggers a debit note and a reduction in the material cost recorded. |

### 4.2.2 Storage of Materials

The **storekeeper** is responsible for the physical custody of materials. Key duties include:

- Receiving materials against the GRN and inspecting quantities.
- Storing materials in appropriate conditions (temperature, humidity, security) to prevent deterioration and pilferage.
- Issuing materials only against authorised **material requisitions**.
- Maintaining **bin cards** for each item.
- Conducting periodic physical verification and reconciling discrepancies.

**Centralised stores** concentrate all inventory in a single location, offering economies of scale in storage, tighter security, and simplified control. **Decentralised stores** locate inventory closer to production points, reducing internal transport time and enabling faster response, but at the cost of duplicate record-keeping and reduced central oversight.

### 4.2.3 Materials Records: Bin Card versus Stores Ledger

Two parallel records track material movements:

| Dimension | **Bin Card** | **Stores Ledger** |
|---|---|---|
| **Maintained by** | Storekeeper (stores department) | Cost accounting department |
| **Location** | Attached to the bin or storage location | Central accounting records |
| **Content** | **Quantitative only** (receipts, issues, balance in units) | **Quantitative and monetary** (receipts, issues, and balance in both units and value) |
| **Purpose** | Physical control; immediate reference at the point of storage | Costing, valuation, and financial reporting |
| **Frequency of update** | Updated at the time of each transaction | Updated from source documents (GRN, requisition) |
| **Reconciliation** | Periodically reconciled with the stores ledger | Periodically reconciled with the bin card and physical count |

Basu and Das stress that the bin card and stores ledger serve complementary control functions: the bin card answers “how many are there?” while the stores ledger answers “how many are there and what are they worth?”.

## 4.3 Methods of Valuing Material Issues

When identical materials are purchased at different prices, the cost accountant must select a **cost flow assumption** to value issues to production and the ending inventory. The choice affects cost of goods manufactured, cost of goods sold, ending inventory valuation, and reported profit.

### 4.3.1 First-In, First-Out (FIFO)

Under **FIFO**, the assumption is that the oldest materials are issued first. Issues are priced at the earliest available cost; ending inventory reflects the most recent purchase prices.

**Perpetual system:** A running balance is maintained after each transaction. Each issue is valued at the cost of the oldest layer on hand.

**Periodic system:** Ending inventory is valued at the most recent purchase prices, and cost of goods sold is the residual.

**Behavioural impact during rising prices (inflation):** FIFO charges lower (older) costs to production and carries higher (newer) costs in ending inventory. This **inflates reported profit** relative to LIFO, because cost of goods sold is lower. The balance sheet shows inventory closer to current replacement cost, but the income statement does not reflect current input costs. In a period of rising cotton yarn prices—typical of the Bangladeshi textile sector—FIFO produces a higher gross margin and a higher tax liability than LIFO.

### 4.3.2 Last-In, First-Out (LIFO)

Under **LIFO**, the assumption is that the most recently purchased materials are issued first. Issues are priced at the latest cost; ending inventory reflects the oldest costs.

**Historical rationale:** LIFO was historically favoured in inflationary environments because it matches current replacement cost against current revenue, producing a more economically realistic measure of operating profit and reducing the artificial inflation of profit caused by inventory holding gains.

**Criticisms:** LIFO **artificially deflates net profit** during inflation, reducing tax payments but also reducing distributable earnings. It **distorts the balance sheet**, because ending inventory is valued at old, often obsolete, prices that bear no relationship to current replacement cost. Furthermore, **LIFO is not permitted under IFRS**; IAS 2 prohibits LIFO because it does not faithfully represent inventory value. Companies reporting under IFRS—as most Bangladeshi listed companies do—cannot use LIFO for external financial reporting.

### 4.3.3 Weighted Average Cost (AVCO)

The **weighted average** method smooths price fluctuations by valuing issues at an average cost computed from the costs of goods available for sale.

**Simple average:** The arithmetic average of the prices of purchases during the period, without weighting by quantity. This method is rarely used because it ignores the relative quantities purchased.

**Periodic weighted average:** Total cost of goods available for sale divided by total units available for sale. The same average cost is applied to all issues and to ending inventory.

\[
\text{Weighted Average Cost per Unit} = \frac{\text{Total Cost of Goods Available for Sale}}{\text{Total Units Available for Sale}}
\]

**Moving weighted average (perpetual):** A new average cost is computed after **each receipt**. The average is applied to issues occurring after that receipt. This method is widely used in perpetual inventory systems because it responds to each price change while avoiding the extreme layer-tracing of FIFO and LIFO.

### 4.3.4 Specific Identification and Replacement Price

**Specific identification** prices each issue at the actual cost of the specific units issued. It is feasible only when units are uniquely identifiable (e.g., numbered machines, custom-made furniture, or batches with distinct serial numbers). It is the most precise method but is administratively burdensome for homogeneous materials.

**Replacement price** values issues at the current market price required to replace the materials. It is not a historical cost method and is not acceptable for external financial reporting, but it can be useful for internal decision-making (e.g., pricing decisions in inflationary conditions).

### 4.3.5 Comparative Impact Table: FIFO, LIFO, and AVCO under Inflationary Conditions

| Dimension | FIFO | LIFO | Weighted Average (AVCO) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Assumption** | Oldest costs issued first | Newest costs issued first | Average of all costs |
| **Cost of materials issued** | Lowest (during inflation) | Highest (during inflation) | Intermediate |
| **Ending inventory valuation** | Highest (closest to replacement cost) | Lowest (oldest costs) | Intermediate |
| **Reported net profit** | Highest | Lowest (artificially deflated) | Intermediate |
| **Balance sheet relevance** | High (inventory near current cost) | Low (inventory at obsolete costs) | Moderate |
| **Income statement relevance** | Low (does not match current costs) | High (matches current replacement cost) | Moderate |
| **IFRS compliance** | Permitted | **Not permitted** | Permitted |
| **Tax effect** | Higher tax (higher profit) | Lower tax (lower profit) | Intermediate |
| **Best suited to** | Stable or falling prices; balance-sheet focus | Rising prices; income-statement focus (where permitted) | Volatile prices; administrative simplicity |

## 4.4 Planning Material Requirements and Stock Control Levels

### 4.4.1 Material Requirements Planning (MRP)

**Material Requirements Planning (MRP)** is a production planning and inventory control system that calculates the quantity and timing of material requirements based on the **Bill of Materials (BOM)** and the **master production schedule**. The BOM specifies the components and raw materials required to produce one unit of a finished product. Lead times determine when orders must be placed so that materials arrive exactly when needed, minimising both stockouts and excess inventory.

### 4.4.2 Scientific Stock Control Levels

Scientific stock control establishes predetermined levels at which action is triggered. The standard formulas, confirmed by ICAI and other professional sources, are:

**Reorder Level (ROL):**

\[
\text{ROL} = \text{Maximum Usage} \times \text{Maximum Lead Time}
\]

The ROL is the inventory level at which a new order must be placed to avoid a stockout under the worst-case combination of usage and lead time.

**Minimum Stock Level (Safety/Buffer Stock):**

\[
\text{Minimum Level} = \text{ROL} - (\text{Normal Usage} \times \text{Normal Lead Time})
\]

The minimum level is the buffer that should remain if normal usage and normal lead time prevail after an order is placed.

**Maximum Stock Level:**

\[
\text{Maximum Level} = \text{ROL} + \text{Reorder Quantity} - (\text{Minimum Usage} \times \text{Minimum Lead Time})
\]

The maximum level prevents overstocking. When an order arrives, inventory should not exceed this level under the minimum-usage, minimum-lead-time scenario.

**Danger / Emergency Stock Level:**

\[
\text{Danger Level} = \text{Normal Usage} \times \text{Emergency Delivery Lead Time}
\]

The danger level is the inventory level at which emergency action—such as an expedited purchase at premium cost—must be taken to avoid a complete stockout.

**Average Stock Level:**

\[
\text{Average Level} = \text{Minimum Level} + \frac{1}{2}(\text{Reorder Quantity})
\]
or
\[
\text{Average Level} = \frac{\text{Minimum Level} + \text{Maximum Level}}{2}
\]

## 4.5 Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) and Safety Stock

### 4.5.1 The Inventory Cost Balance

The **economic order quantity (EOQ)** is the order quantity that minimises the sum of **annual ordering costs** and **annual carrying costs**.

- **Ordering costs** include purchase requisition processing, supplier selection, purchase order preparation, receiving, inspection, and accounting. These costs are **inversely related** to order size: larger orders mean fewer orders per year and lower total ordering cost.
- **Carrying (holding) costs** include storage, insurance, obsolescence, deterioration, and the opportunity cost of capital tied up in inventory. These costs are **directly related** to order size: larger orders mean higher average inventory and higher total carrying cost.

The EOQ is the order size at which the two cost curves intersect—the point of minimum total cost.

### 4.5.2 Mathematical Derivation and Formula

Let:
- \( D \) = annual demand in units
- \( S \) = cost per order (ordering cost)
- \( H \) = annual carrying cost per unit
- \( Q \) = order quantity

**Annual ordering cost:** \( \frac{D}{Q} \times S \)

**Annual carrying cost:** \( \frac{Q}{2} \times H \)

**Total annual inventory cost (TIC):**

\[
TIC = \left(\frac{D}{Q} \times S\right) + \left(\frac{Q}{2} \times H\right)
\]

Differentiating TIC with respect to \( Q \), setting the derivative equal to zero, and solving yields the EOQ formula:

\[
\boxed{EOQ = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}}}
\]

At the EOQ, annual ordering cost equals annual carrying cost, and total cost is minimised.

### 4.5.3 Evaluating Bulk Quantity Discounts

Suppliers frequently offer **quantity discounts** for orders above a threshold. Accepting a discount reduces the purchase price but increases the order quantity above the EOQ, raising carrying costs. The decision rule is:

> **Accept the discount only if the total savings (discount on purchase price plus any reduction in ordering cost) exceeds the increase in carrying cost.**

The comparison should be made between the **total relevant cost** at the EOQ (without discount) and the **total relevant cost** at the discounted order quantity, including the purchase price itself.

### 4.5.4 Safety Stock and Reorder Point

**Safety stock** is the additional inventory held to protect against uncertainty in demand or lead time. The **reorder point (ROP)** is the inventory level at which an order is placed, incorporating both expected demand during lead time and safety stock:

\[
\boxed{ROP = (\text{Normal Usage} \times \text{Lead Time}) + \text{Safety Stock}}
\]

Safety stock balances the cost of holding extra inventory against the cost of stockouts—lost sales, production stoppages, and expediting costs. The optimal safety stock depends on the variability of demand and lead time, the desired service level, and the relative magnitudes of carrying and stockout costs.

## 4.6 Materials Control Systems: Selective Inventory Control

Not all inventory items deserve equal managerial attention. **Selective inventory control** techniques classify items so that control effort is proportional to importance.

### 4.6.1 ABC Analysis

**ABC analysis** applies the **Pareto principle** to inventory: a small proportion of items typically accounts for a large proportion of total inventory value. Items are classified as:

- **A items:** high value, low volume (typically 10–20% of items, 70–80% of value). These require tight control: frequent review, accurate records, low safety stocks, and senior management attention.
- **B items:** moderate value and volume (typically around 20% of items, 15–20% of value). Moderate control.
- **C items:** low value, high volume (typically 60–70% of items, 5–10% of value). Loose control: infrequent review, higher safety stocks, and simple recording procedures.

Empirical studies in pharmaceutical inventory management confirm this pattern: 14.8% of items (Class A) represented 79.9% of annual sales value, while 65.4% of items (Class C) represented only 5.9%.

### 4.6.2 VED Analysis

**VED analysis** classifies items by **criticality**:

- **Vital (V):** items whose absence would halt production or endanger life (e.g., life-saving drugs, critical spare parts). These must never be out of stock; high safety stocks are justified.
- **Essential (E):** items whose absence causes temporary disruption but not a complete halt. Moderate safety stocks.
- **Desirable (D):** items whose absence causes inconvenience but not operational failure. Low safety stocks.

VED analysis is particularly important for **spare parts** and **pharmaceutical** inventories, where the cost of a stockout may far exceed the carrying cost of the item.

### 4.6.3 FSN Analysis

**FSN analysis** classifies items by **movement velocity**:

- **Fast-moving (F):** high turnover; require frequent replenishment and close monitoring.
- **Slow-moving (S):** low turnover; may require periodic review and potential liquidation.
- **Non-moving (N):** obsolete or dormant items; should be reviewed for disposal or write-down.

### 4.6.4 Comparison Table: ABC, VED, and FSN

| Dimension | ABC Analysis | VED Analysis | FSN Analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Basis of classification** | Annual consumption value | Criticality / consequence of stockout | Movement velocity |
| **Categories** | A (high value), B (moderate), C (low) | Vital, Essential, Desirable | Fast-moving, Slow-moving, Non-moving |
| **Primary focus** | Value optimisation | Operational continuity | Inventory turnover and obsolescence |
| **Control implication** | Tight control of A items; loose control of C | High safety stock for V items; low for D | Frequent replenishment for F; periodic review for N |
| **Typical application** | Raw materials, components | Spare parts, pharmaceuticals | General inventory management |

## 4.7 Stock Verification and Adjustments

### 4.7.1 Physical Inventory Verification

**Periodic physical count** involves a complete count of all inventory at the end of an accounting period. It is time-consuming and may disrupt operations, but it provides a comprehensive verification.

**Continuous (perpetual) stocktaking** involves counting a small portion of inventory on a rotating basis throughout the year. It is less disruptive, allows discrepancies to be investigated promptly, and is compatible with perpetual inventory systems.

### 4.7.2 Material Discrepancies and Accounting Adjustments

| Type of Loss | Definition | Accounting Treatment | Ledger Entry |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Normal loss** | Unavoidable shrinkage, evaporation, or waste inherent in the production process | Absorbed into the cost of good units by inflating the unit cost | No separate ledger entry; the cost per unit of remaining material is increased |
| **Abnormal loss** | Loss due to negligence, theft, fire, or other controllable events | Charged directly to the Costing Profit and Loss Account | Debit Costing P&L; Credit Stores/Process Account |
| **Scrap** | Material residue with minor recovery value | Credited to the job, factory overhead, or sales revenue, depending on policy | Debit Scrap Account; Credit Job/Overhead/Revenue |
| **Spoilage** | Units that do not meet quality standards | Rework costs charged to overhead or the job; salvage value credited | Debit Rework/Overhead; Credit Stores/WIP for salvage |

Basu and Das emphasise that **normal losses are anticipated and absorbed** into the cost of good output, whereas **abnormal losses are written off separately** so that they do not distort product costs. ACCA technical guidance confirms that the scrap value of normal loss is credited to the process account and offset against process costs.

## 4.8 Bangladesh Manufacturing Vignette: Landed Cost and Perpetual Inventory at a Gazipur Spinning Mill

**Background.** *Meghna Spinning Mills Ltd.* (a fictionalised spinning mill in Gazipur) imports raw cotton through letters of credit (LC) from international suppliers. The mill produces cotton yarn for the domestic weaving and knitwear sectors. The cost accountant, an ICMAB-qualified CMA, must determine the **landed cost** of each cotton consignment and maintain perpetual stores ledgers under both FIFO and moving weighted average (AVCO) to support product costing and statutory cost audit.

**Landed Cost Calculation (Illustrative).** A consignment of cotton is imported at a CIF value of USD 1,00,000 (BDT 1,23,33,000 at an exchange rate of BDT 123.33/USD). Under the Bangladesh customs tariff for HS code 52010000 (cotton, not carded or combed), the applicable rates for FY 2026–2027 are **0% Customs Duty (CD), 0% Regulatory Duty (RD), 0% Supplementary Duty (SD), 0% VAT, 0% AIT, and 0% AT**. Raw cotton imports are therefore duty-free at the border. However, the **landed cost** includes:

- CIF value: BDT 1,23,33,000
- Clearing and forwarding charges: BDT 1,50,000
- Inland freight from Chittagong to Gazipur: BDT 1,20,000
- **Total landed cost: BDT 1,26,03,000**

The cost accountant records this landed cost—not the invoice price—as the cost of the materials received.

**Perpetual Stores Ledger Illustration.** During the month, the mill receives three consignments of cotton at different landed costs and issues cotton to production. Under **FIFO**, the earliest consignment is issued first; under **moving weighted average**, a new average cost is computed after each receipt. The following table illustrates the moving average calculation:

| Transaction | Units | Cost per Unit (BDT) | Total Cost (BDT) | Balance Units | Average Cost per Unit (BDT) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Opening balance | 10,000 | 120 | 12,00,000 | 10,000 | 120.00 |
| Receipt 1 | 15,000 | 125 | 18,75,000 | 25,000 | 123.00 |
| Issue | (12,000) | 123.00 | (14,76,000) | 13,000 | 123.00 |
| Receipt 2 | 20,000 | 130 | 26,00,000 | 33,000 | 127.24 |
| Issue | (18,000) | 127.24 | (22,90,320) | 15,000 | 127.24 |

The cost accountant uses these valuations to compute the cost of yarn produced (COGM), the cost of yarn sold (COGS), and the value of closing cotton inventory. The choice between FIFO and AVCO affects reported profit and inventory value—a decision that must be disclosed and applied consistently. The perpetual records also support the statutory cost audit, which requires accurate cost accounting records for prescribed companies under the Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997.

### Problem 3: Calculation of All Stock Levels

**Problem Statement.** From the following data, calculate the reorder level, minimum level, maximum level, danger level, and average stock level:

- Normal usage: 200 units per week
- Maximum usage: 300 units per week
- Minimum usage: 150 units per week
- Reorder quantity: 1,600 units
- Normal lead time: 3 weeks
- Maximum lead time: 5 weeks
- Minimum lead time: 2 weeks
- Emergency delivery lead time: 1 week

**Solution.**

\[
\text{ROL} = 300 \times 5 = \mathbf{1,500 \text{ units}}

\[
\text{Minimum Level} = 1,500 - (200 \times 3) = 1,500 - 600 = \mathbf{900 \text{ units}}
\]

\[
\text{Maximum Level} = 1,500 + 1,600 - (150 \times 2) = 3,100 - 300 = \mathbf{2,800 \text{ units}}
\]

\[
\text{Danger Level} = 200 \times 1 = \mathbf{200 \text{ units}}
\]

\[
\text{Average Level} = 900 + \frac{1,600}{2} = \mathbf{1,700 \text{ units}}
\]

### Problem 4: EOQ and Total Annual Inventory Cost

**Problem Statement.** A factory uses 24,000 units of a material annually. The cost of placing an order is Tk 400, and the annual carrying cost per unit is Tk 12. Calculate the EOQ and the total annual inventory cost at the EOQ.

**Solution.**

\[
EOQ = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 24,000 \times 400}{12}} = \sqrt{\frac{1,92,00,000}{12}} = \sqrt{16,00,000} = \mathbf{1,265 \text{ units (approximately)}}
\]

\[
TIC = \left(\frac{24,000}{1,265} \times 400\right) + \left(\frac{1,265}{2} \times 12\right) = (18.97 \times 400) + (632.5 \times 12) \approx 7,589 + 7,590 = \mathbf{Tk\ 15,179}
\]

### Problem 5: EOQ with Quantity Discount — Accept or Reject

**Problem Statement.** A company has annual demand of 10,000 units. The ordering cost is Tk 200 per order, and the carrying cost is Tk 10 per unit per year. The purchase price is Tk 50 per unit. The supplier offers a **2% discount** if the company orders **2,000 units or more** at a time. Should the discount be accepted?

**Solution.**

**Without discount (at EOQ):**

\[
EOQ = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 10,000 \times 200}{10}} = \sqrt{4,00,000} = 632 \text{ units}
\]

\[
TIC_{\text{EOQ}} = \left(\frac{10,000}{632} \times 200\right) + \left(\frac{632}{2} \times 10\right) = 3,165 + 3,160 = \text{Tk } 6,325
\]

Purchase cost: \( 10,000 \times 50 = \text{Tk } 5,00,000 \). Total cost: **Tk 5,06,325**.

**With discount (order quantity 2,000 units, price Tk 49):**

\[
TIC_{2,000} = \left(\frac{10,000}{2,000} \times 200\right) + \left(\frac{2,000}{2} \times 10\right) = 1,000 + 10,000 = \text{Tk } 11,000
\]

Purchase cost: \( 10,000 \times 49 = \text{Tk } 4,90,000 \). Total cost: **Tk 5,01,000**.

**Decision:** Total cost is **lower** with the discount (Tk 5,01,000 < Tk 5,06,325). The saving on purchase price (Tk 10,000) exceeds the increase in carrying cost (Tk 4,675). **Accept the discount.**

### Problem 6: Normal and Abnormal Spoilage, Scrap, and Defectives

**Problem Statement.** A job requires 1,000 kg of material at Tk 50 per kg. Normal spoilage is 5% of input; abnormal spoilage is 2% of input. Spoiled units have a scrap value of Tk 10 per kg. Defective units (3% of good output) require rework costing Tk 200 per unit. Calculate the cost of good units, the abnormal loss, and the scrap recovery.

**Solution.**

- Input: 1,000 kg; cost = Tk 50,000.
- Normal spoilage: 5% = 50 kg. Scrap value = 50 × Tk 10 = Tk 500.
- Abnormal spoilage: 2% = 20 kg. Scrap value = 20 × Tk 10 = Tk 200.
- Good output before defectives: 1,000 − 50 − 20 = 930 kg.
- Defectives: 3% of 930 = 27.9 units (approximately 28 units). Rework cost = 28 × Tk 200 = Tk 5,600.

**Cost of good units:**

\[
\text{Net material cost} = 50,000 - 500 \text{ (normal scrap)} - 200 \text{ (abnormal scrap)} = 49,300
\]

\[
\text{Cost of good output} = 49,300 + 5,600 = \mathbf{Tk\ 54,900}
\]

**Abnormal loss:** The abnormal spoilage cost (20 kg × Tk 50 = Tk 1,000, less scrap Tk 200 = Tk 800) is charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account. **Normal spoilage** (Tk 2,500 − Tk 500 scrap = Tk 2,000) is absorbed into the cost of good units.

## 4.10 Formula Reference Cheat-Sheet

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Reorder Level (ROL)** | \( \text{Maximum Usage} \times \text{Maximum Lead Time} \) |
| **Minimum Level** | \( \text{ROL} - (\text{Normal Usage} \times \text{Normal Lead Time}) \) |
| **Maximum Level** | \( \text{ROL} + \text{Reorder Quantity} - (\text{Minimum Usage} \times \text{Minimum Lead Time}) \) |
| **Danger Level** | \( \text{Normal Usage} \times \text{Emergency Lead Time} \) |
| **Average Level** | \( \text{Minimum Level} + \frac{1}{2}(\text{Reorder Quantity}) \) |
| **EOQ** | \( \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}} \) |
| **Total Annual Inventory Cost (TIC)** | \( \left(\frac{D}{Q} \times S\right) + \left(\frac{Q}{2} \times H\right) \) |
| **Reorder Point with Safety Stock** | \( (\text{Normal Usage} \times \text{Lead Time}) + \text{Safety Stock} \) |
| **Weighted Average Cost per Unit** | \( \frac{\text{Total Cost of Goods Available}}{\text{Total Units Available}} \) |
| **Landed Cost** | CIF Value + Customs Duty + Clearing + Inland Freight + Other Direct Charges |

## 4.11 End-of-Chapter Exam Preparation Pack

### 4.11.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Q1. Distinguish between direct materials and indirect materials, giving two examples of each.**

**Model Answer.** Direct materials become an integral part of the finished product and can be traced to it economically (e.g., cotton yarn in a textile mill, active ingredients in a pharmaceutical). Indirect materials are used in production but cannot be traced economically to a specific product and are treated as manufacturing overhead (e.g., maintenance lubricants, store supplies). The classification depends on traceability and materiality.

**Q2. Explain the difference between a bin card and a stores ledger.**

**Model Answer.** A bin card is maintained by the storekeeper and records only quantities (receipts, issues, and balance in units). It is attached to the storage bin for immediate physical reference. A stores ledger is maintained by the cost accounting department and records both quantities and monetary values. It supports product costing and financial reporting. The two records are periodically reconciled.

**Q3. Why does FIFO produce a higher reported profit than LIFO during inflation?**

**Model Answer.** Under FIFO, the oldest (and therefore cheapest) costs are charged to production, while ending inventory reflects the most recent (higher) prices. Because cost of goods sold is lower, reported profit is higher. Under LIFO, the most recent (higher) costs are charged to production, reducing reported profit. LIFO therefore artificially deflates profit during inflation and distorts the balance sheet by valuing inventory at obsolete costs.

**Q4. State the formulas for reorder level, minimum level, and maximum level.**

**Model Answer.** Reorder level = Maximum usage × Maximum lead time. Minimum level = Reorder level − (Normal usage × Normal lead time). Maximum level = Reorder level + Reorder quantity − (Minimum usage × Minimum lead time). The danger level = Normal usage × Emergency delivery lead time.

**Q5. What is the EOQ, and what two costs does it balance?**

**Model Answer.** The EOQ is the order quantity that minimises total annual inventory cost. It balances annual ordering costs (which decline as order size increases) against annual carrying costs (which rise as order size increases). At the EOQ, ordering cost equals carrying cost, and total cost is minimised.

**Q6. Distinguish between normal loss and abnormal loss in materials, and explain their accounting treatment.**

**Model Answer.** Normal loss is unavoidable shrinkage or waste inherent in the production process; it is absorbed into the cost of good units by inflating the unit cost. Abnormal loss arises from negligence, theft, or abnormal events; it is charged directly to the Costing Profit and Loss Account so that it does not distort product costs. The distinction ensures that product costs reflect efficient operating conditions.

## 4.12 Chapter Summary

Materials are the physical substances consumed in production. **Direct materials** are traceable to the product; **indirect materials** are treated as manufacturing overhead. Material cost control is strategically vital because materials dominate manufacturing cost structures.

The **procurement cycle** proceeds through purchase requisition, quotation, comparative statement, purchase order, bill of entry (for imports), goods received note, inspection, and material returned note. The **bin card** records quantities; the **stores ledger** records quantities and values.

Material issues are valued under **FIFO** (oldest costs first), **LIFO** (newest costs first; not IFRS-compliant), **weighted average** (periodic or moving), **specific identification**, or **replacement price**. Under inflation, FIFO produces the highest profit and inventory value; LIFO produces the lowest profit and an obsolete inventory valuation.

**Stock control levels**—reorder level, minimum level, maximum level, danger level, and average level—are calculated from usage and lead-time parameters. The **EOQ** minimises total annual inventory cost; **safety stock** protects against uncertainty. **ABC, VED, and FSN** analyses prioritise inventory control by value, criticality, and movement.

**Normal loss** is absorbed into good-unit cost; **abnormal loss** is written off to the Costing Profit and Loss Account. **Scrap** and **spoilage** are accounted for through scrap recovery and rework cost allocation.

## 4.13 Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Direct materials** | Materials that become an integral part of the finished product and are traceable to it economically. |
| **Indirect materials** | Materials used in production but not traceable economically to a specific product; treated as manufacturing overhead. |
| **Bin card** | A quantitative record of material receipts, issues, and balances maintained by the storekeeper. |
| **Stores ledger** | A record of material receipts, issues, and balances in both quantities and values, maintained by the cost accounting department. |
| **FIFO** | First-in, first-out; the assumption that the oldest materials are issued first. |
| **LIFO** | Last-in, first-out; the assumption that the newest materials are issued first; not permitted under IFRS. |
| **Weighted average cost (AVCO)** | A method that values issues at an average cost computed from goods available for sale. |
| **Reorder level (ROL)** | The inventory level at which a new order is placed: Maximum usage × Maximum lead time. |
| **EOQ** | Economic order quantity; the order size that minimises total annual ordering and carrying costs. |
| **Safety stock** | Additional inventory held to protect against demand or lead-time uncertainty. |
| **ABC analysis** | Classification of inventory by annual consumption value (A: high, B: moderate, C: low). |
| **VED analysis** | Classification of inventory by criticality (Vital, Essential, Desirable). |
| **FSN analysis** | Classification of inventory by movement velocity (Fast, Slow, Non-moving). |
| **Normal loss** | Unavoidable material shrinkage or waste absorbed into the cost of good units. |
| **Abnormal loss** | Material loss due to negligence or abnormal events, charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account. |
| **Scrap** | Material residue with minor recovery value, credited to the job, overhead, or revenue. |
