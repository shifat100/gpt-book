## 5.1 Measuring Labor Costs

### 5.1.1 Direct Labor versus Indirect Labor

**Direct labour** consists of the compensation of workers who physically transform materials into finished products and whose time can be traced to specific cost objects in an economically feasible way. Sewing-machine operators in a garment factory, machine operators in a spinning mill, and assembly-line workers in an appliance plant are direct labour. Their wages are charged directly to Work-in-Process and become part of the product cost.

**Indirect labour** consists of the compensation of workers who support production but whose time cannot be traced economically to specific units. Factory supervisors, maintenance technicians, stores clerks, quality-control inspectors, and janitorial staff are indirect labour. Their wages are treated as **manufacturing overhead** and allocated across products.

The distinction mirrors the direct/indirect materials distinction of Chapter 4: it depends on **traceability to the cost object**, not on the physical location of the worker or the nature of the task. A maintenance technician who works exclusively on a machine dedicated to one product line may be direct labour for that line; the same technician serving the entire factory is indirect.

**Payroll segregation.** In a well-designed cost system, the payroll is segregated by cost object and by direct/indirect classification at the point of data capture. Time tickets and job cards identify the hours spent on each job (direct labour); supervisory and support time is recorded against overhead cost centres (indirect labour). This segregation feeds directly into the cost distribution journal entries discussed in Section 5.6.

### 5.1.2 Gross Pay, Statutory Deductions, and Net Pay

**Gross pay** is the total earnings of an employee before any deductions—the sum of basic wages, dearness allowance, house rent allowance, medical allowance, transport allowance, overtime, and bonuses. **Net pay** (take-home pay) is gross pay less statutory and voluntary deductions.

In Bangladesh, payroll deductions commonly include:

- **Provident Fund (PF):** A retirement savings contribution, typically a percentage of basic wage, matched by the employer. The employee’s contribution is deducted from gross pay; the employer’s contribution is an additional labour cost.
- **Group Insurance:** A premium deducted for group life or health coverage.
- **Tax Deducted at Source (TDS):** Income tax withheld under the Income Tax Act, based on the employee’s taxable income and slab.
- **Other deductions:** Union dues, loan repayments, and salary advances.

The payroll equation is:

\[
\text{Net Pay} = \text{Gross Pay} - \text{Provident Fund} - \text{Group Insurance} - \text{TDS} - \text{Other Deductions}
\]

### 5.1.3 Treatment of Fringe Benefits

**Fringe benefits** are non-wage compensations provided to employees in addition to their regular earnings. Examples include:

- **Subsidised meals:** The cost of subsidising a factory canteen is a labour-related cost. If the benefit is provided to all production workers, it is typically treated as manufacturing overhead (indirect labour cost). If it is provided to a specific job’s workers as a contractual requirement, it may be direct.
- **Medical allowance:** A fixed monthly allowance is part of gross pay and is treated in the same way as basic wages—direct if the worker is direct labour, indirect otherwise.
- **Festival bonuses:** Annual or religious festival bonuses (e.g., Eid bonus) are usually treated as indirect labour cost (manufacturing overhead) because they relate to the workforce as a whole rather than to specific units of production.
- **Housing allowances:** A house rent allowance is a component of gross pay and follows the direct/indirect classification of the worker’s labour.

The general principle is that a fringe benefit is classified in the same way as the underlying labour cost unless it is clearly identifiable with a specific cost object. The Bangladesh Labour Act requires the basic wage to be at least **50% of the total gross wage**, and house rent allowance is typically set at **50% of basic wage**, with medical and transport/food allowances making up the balance. For a Grade 4 RMG worker under the 2023 wage structure, the monthly breakdown is: basic BDT 6,700; house rent BDT 3,350; medical BDT 750; transport and food BDT 1,700; **total BDT 12,500**.

## 5.2 Overtime Premium and Idle Time Accounting

### 5.2.1 Overtime Premium: Definition

**Overtime** is time worked beyond the normal working hours (normally 8 hours per day and 48 hours per week in Bangladesh). The **overtime premium** is the excess of the overtime rate over the normal wage rate. Under **Section 108 of the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006**, overtime work must be paid at **twice the ordinary basic wage rate**. Thus, if the normal rate is Tk 100 per hour, the overtime rate is Tk 200 per hour: Tk 100 is the **base rate** (charged as normal labour cost) and Tk 100 is the **overtime premium**.

The **allocation of the overtime premium** depends on the circumstances that caused the overtime:

| Circumstance of Overtime | Treatment of Premium | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **General factory workload / random scheduling** | **Manufacturing Overhead (MOH)** | The overtime arises from overall production pressure and benefits all products; it is a general factory cost. |
| **Specific rush request of a particular customer for a specific job** | **Direct Labour to that Job** | The overtime is incurred at the customer’s request and is traceable to the specific job; the customer should bear the cost. |
| **Abnormal emergency (e.g., equipment sabotage, fire recovery)** | **Costing Profit and Loss Account** | The cost is abnormal and should not be allowed to distort product costs or inventory valuation. |

The **base rate** of overtime hours is always treated as normal labour cost—direct if the worker is direct labour, indirect if indirect. Only the **premium** is subject to the allocation rules above.

In Bangladesh, the RMG sector operates under specific overtime limits. Normally, daily working hours may extend to 10 hours (8 normal + 2 overtime) and weekly hours to 60, with an annual average of 56 hours per week. A temporary exemption notification effective April–October 2026 for BGMEA and BKMEA member factories permits up to **4 hours of overtime per day**, provided overtime is voluntary and paid at **double the normal wage**.

### 5.2.2 Idle Time: Normal versus Abnormal

**Idle time** is time for which workers are paid but no production occurs. It is classified by cause:

- **Normal idle time** is unavoidable in the ordinary course of operations—rest breaks, machine setup, tool collection, material handling between jobs, and routine maintenance. Normal idle time is **absorbed into manufacturing overhead** and spread across all production. It is a legitimate cost of creating the capacity to produce.
- **Abnormal idle time** arises from unexpected and controllable events—power grid failure, strike, prolonged raw material stockout, machine breakdown due to negligence, or a natural disaster. Abnormal idle time is **excluded from product cost** and charged directly to the **Costing Profit and Loss Account**. Allowing abnormal idle time to inflate product cost would overstate inventory and distort pricing and performance evaluation.

### 5.2.3 Comparison Table: Overtime Premium Treatment and Idle Time Treatment

| Cost | Circumstance | Treatment | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overtime premium** | General workload | Manufacturing Overhead | Benefits all products; general factory cost |
| **Overtime premium** | Specific customer rush request | Direct Labour to the Job | Traceable to the specific job at the customer’s request |
| **Overtime premium** | Abnormal emergency | Costing P&L | Abnormal; should not distort product cost |
| **Idle time** | Normal (rest, setup, tool collection) | Manufacturing Overhead | Unavoidable cost of capacity |
| **Idle time** | Abnormal (power failure, strike, stockout) | Costing P&L | Abnormal; excluded from product cost |

## 5.3 Labor Cost Control and Productivity Systems

### 5.3.1 Time-Keeping versus Time-Booking

**Time-keeping** records the **attendance and total time** of each worker—when they enter and leave the factory. Its objectives are payroll computation, compliance with working-hour regulations, and attendance control. Mechanisms include biometric thumb scanners, RFID swipe cards, manual attendance registers, and daily time sheets.

**Time-booking** records **how each worker’s time is spent** during the working day—which job, process, or cost centre consumed the time. Its objectives are cost distribution (direct versus indirect, job A versus job B), productivity measurement, and variance analysis. Mechanisms include **job tickets** (one per job, recording start and finish times), **daily time sheets** (a worker’s allocation of hours across jobs), and **labour cost cards**.

Time-keeping answers *“Was the worker present?”*; time-booking answers *“What did the worker do?”* Both are essential: time-keeping feeds payroll, time-booking feeds product costing.

### 5.3.2 Labor Turnover: Definition, Causes, and Costs

**Labor turnover** (also called employee turnover or, in the Bangladeshi RMG sector, the **dropout rate**) is the rate at which workers leave and are replaced. In the garment sector, the average monthly turnover rate is around **5%**—five out of every 100 workers typically leave each month—although well-managed factories achieve rates below 2%.

**Causes** of labour turnover include low wages, excessive workload, poor working conditions, lack of promotion opportunities, supervisory behaviour, seasonal demand fluctuations, and personal reasons. In Bangladesh, workload is a dominant cause: surveys indicate that almost nine in every ten RMG workers quit within years of joining because they cannot endure the excessive work.

**Financial costs** of labour turnover fall into two categories:

- **Preventive costs:** costs incurred to reduce turnover—competitive wages, training, welfare facilities, career development, and improved working conditions.
- **Replacement (curative) costs:** costs incurred when a worker leaves—recruitment, selection, induction, training of the replacement, lost productivity during the learning period, and administrative processing.

### 5.3.3 Measuring Labor Turnover Rates

Three methods are used:

**1. Separation Method:**

\[
\text{Turnover Rate} = \frac{\text{Number of Separations during the Period}}{\text{Average Number of Workers}} \times 100
\]

The separation method measures the rate at which workers leave, regardless of whether they are replaced.

**2. Replacement Method:**

\[
\text{Turnover Rate} = \frac{\text{Number of Replacements Recruited during the Period}}{\text{Average Number of Workers}} \times 100
\]

The replacement method measures only the recruitment of replacements for departed workers; it excludes recruitment for expansion.

**3. Flux Method:**

\[
\text{Turnover Rate} = \frac{\text{Separations} + \text{Accessions}}{\text{Average Number of Workers}} \times 100
\]

or, alternatively,

\[
\text{Turnover Rate} = \frac{\text{Separations} + \text{Replacements}}{\text{Average Number of Workers}} \times 100
\]

The flux method captures the total movement of labour—both inflows and outflows—and is therefore the most comprehensive measure. The **average number of workers** is typically calculated as:

\[
\text{Average Workers} = \frac{\text{Workers at Beginning} + \text{Workers at End}}{2}
\]

## 5.4 Methods of Remuneration and Wage Systems

### 5.4.1 Time-Wage System

Under the **time-wage system**, earnings are based on time spent at work:

\[
\text{Earnings} = \text{Hours Worked} \times \text{Hourly Rate}
\]

**Merits:** Simplicity; stable income for workers; encourages quality over speed; suitable for work where output cannot be easily measured or quality is paramount. **Demerits:** No direct incentive for productivity; efficient and inefficient workers are paid alike; requires close supervision. **Suitability:** Supervisory, maintenance, clerical, and quality-control roles; jobs with highly variable output or stringent quality requirements.

### 5.4.2 Piece-Wage Systems

Under **piece-wage systems**, earnings are based on output:

**Straight piece-rate:**

\[
\text{Earnings} = \text{Units Produced} \times \text{Piece Rate per Unit}
\]

The straight piece-rate provides a direct incentive for productivity but may encourage quantity at the expense of quality and can lead to earnings volatility.

**Taylor’s differential piece-rate system:** A **punitively low rate** (e.g., 80% of the normal piece rate) applies to output below standard; a **high rate** (e.g., 120%) applies to output at or above standard. Taylor’s system is designed to reward high performers and penalise low performers sharply, but it can be harsh and demotivating for workers who fall marginally below standard.

**Merrick’s differential piece-rate system:** A **three-tiered** system:

- Up to **83% efficiency**: ordinary piece rate.
- **83% to 100% efficiency**: **110%** of the ordinary piece rate.
- **Above 100% efficiency**: **120%** of the ordinary piece rate.

Merrick’s system is less punitive than Taylor’s because it provides a graduated incentive rather than a sharp penalty at the standard.

### 5.4.3 Comparison Table: Time-Wage versus Piece-Wage System

| Dimension | Time-Wage System | Piece-Wage System |
|---|---|---|
| **Basis of payment** | Hours worked | Units produced |
| **Earnings formula** | Hours × Rate | Units × Piece rate |
| **Incentive for productivity** | Weak | Strong |
| **Quality focus** | Strong (no incentive to rush) | Weak (quantity may be prioritised) |
| **Income stability** | High | Variable |
| **Supervision requirement** | High | Lower (output self-monitors) |
| **Suitability** | Supervisory, maintenance, quality control | Repetitive, measurable production tasks |
| **Earnings calculation complexity** | Simple | More complex (especially differential systems) |

## 5.5 Individual and Group Incentive Plans

### 5.5.1 Halsey Premium Plan (50% Sharing)

The **Halsey Premium Plan** guarantees a day wage for the time actually worked and adds a bonus for time saved, shared **50:50** between worker and employer:

\[
\text{Total Earnings} = (T \times R) + \left[50\% \times (S - T) \times R\right]
\]

where \(T\) = actual time taken, \(S\) = standard time allowed, and \(R\) = wage rate per hour. If \(T \geq S\), no bonus is paid; earnings are simply \(T \times R\).

**Halsey-Weir variation:** The bonus sharing ratio is **\(33\frac{1}{3}\%\) to the worker** (and \(66\frac{2}{3}\%\) to the employer):

\[
\text{Total Earnings} = (T \times R) + \left[33\frac{1}{3}\% \times (S - T) \times R\right]
\]

### 5.5.2 Rowan Incentive Plan

The **Rowan Plan** also guarantees a day wage and pays a bonus for time saved, but the bonus is calculated as a **proportion of the time actually worked**:

\[
\text{Total Earnings} = (T \times R) + \left[\frac{S - T}{S}\right] \times (T \times R)
\]

The bonus fraction \((S - T)/S\) is the proportion of standard time saved. As \(T\) approaches \(S\), the bonus fraction approaches zero. As \(T\) becomes very small (large time saving), the bonus approaches \(T \times R\), so total earnings approach \(2TR\)—but never exceed twice the time wage.

**Theoretical safety: why Rowan prevents runaway bonus payouts.** Under Halsey, the bonus is \(50\% \times (S - T) \times R\). If the standard time \(S\) is set loosely (too generous), a worker can save enormous time and earn an unbounded bonus. Under Rowan, the bonus is capped by the proportional structure: even if \(T\) is tiny, the bonus cannot exceed \(T \times R\), and total earnings cannot exceed \(2TR\). Rowan therefore **self-limits** bonus payouts when standards are loose, protecting the employer against poorly set time standards.

### 5.5.3 Emerson’s Efficiency Plan and Gantt Task and Bonus Plan

**Emerson’s Efficiency Plan** guarantees a day wage and introduces a **stepped bonus** beginning at **\(66.67\%\) efficiency**. Below \(66.67\%\), the worker receives only the day wage. Above \(66.67\%\), a bonus increases in steps as efficiency rises, reaching a substantial bonus at \(100\%\) efficiency and above.

**Gantt Task and Bonus Plan** guarantees a day wage for output below standard. At standard output, a **bonus** (typically 20% of the day wage) is paid. Above standard, the worker earns a high piece rate. The Gantt plan is less punitive than Taylor’s system because it does not cut the wage of below-standard workers; it simply withholds the bonus.

### 5.5.4 Group Incentive Schemes and the Scanlon Plan

**Group incentive schemes** reward a team, department, or plant for collective performance. They are appropriate when individual output is difficult to measure, when cooperation is essential, or when technology imposes a team-based workflow.

**Conditions for success:** Clear and achievable targets; equitable sharing of rewards; worker participation in target-setting; stable production processes; and management commitment to the scheme.

The **Scanlon Plan** is a classic group incentive based on **labour cost savings**. A baseline ratio of labour cost to sales value (or value added) is established. If the actual ratio falls below the baseline, the resulting savings are shared between workers and the company, typically with a substantial portion distributed to employees. The Scanlon Plan emphasises **participation**, **cooperation**, and **continuous improvement** rather than individual competition.

### 5.5.5 Comparison Table: Halsey Plan versus Rowan Plan

| Dimension | Halsey Plan | Rowan Plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Bonus formula** | \(50\% \times (S - T) \times R\) | \(\left(\frac{S - T}{S}\right) \times (T \times R)\) |
| **Share to worker** | 50% of time saved | Proportion of time saved to standard time |
| **Bonus as \(T \to 0\)** | Approaches \(50\% \times S \times R\) | Approaches \(T \times R\) (total earnings approach \(2TR\)) |
| **Maximum total earnings** | \(TR + 50\%(S - T)R\) (can be large if \(S\) is loose) | \(2TR\) (capped) |
| **Protection against loose standards** | Weak | Strong (self-limiting) |
| **Incentive strength at moderate time savings** | Stronger (50% share) | Weaker (proportional share) |
| **Simplicity** | Simpler to compute | Slightly more complex |

## 5.6 Calculation and Recording of Labor Cost: Ledger Mechanics

Labour costs are recorded in the accounting system through a sequence of journal entries:

**1. To record gross payroll and liabilities:**

\[
\text{Dr. Payroll Control (or Wages Control)} \quad \text{Gross Pay}
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Provident Fund Payable} \quad \text{Employee contribution}
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Withholding Tax Payable} \quad \text{TDS}
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Wages Payable} \quad \text{Net Pay}
\]

**2. To record payroll payment:**

\[
\text{Dr. Wages Payable} \quad \text{Net Pay}
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Cash / Bank} \quad \text{Net Pay}
\]

**3. To allocate labour costs to production:**

\[
\text{Dr. Work-in-Process (Direct Labour)} \quad \text{Direct labour cost}
\]
\[
\text{Dr. Manufacturing Overhead Control} \quad \text{Indirect labour + overtime premium (general) + normal idle time}
\]
\[
\text{Dr. Costing Profit and Loss Account} \quad \text{Abnormal idle time + abnormal overtime premium}
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Payroll Control / Wages Control} \quad \text{Total allocated}
\]

The payroll control account is thus cleared: its debit (gross pay) is matched by credits to the liability accounts and to the cost distribution accounts.

## 5.7 Bangladesh Industrial Vignette: Labour Remuneration and Overtime Compliance on a Sewing Floor in Ashulia

**Background.** *Meghna Apparels Ltd.* (a fictionalised garment factory in Ashulia, Dhaka) operates a sewing and finishing floor producing knitwear for European buyers. The factory employs sewing operators, helpers, quality inspectors, and line supervisors. The cost accountant must compute monthly payroll, apply the Bangladesh Labour Act’s overtime provisions, and distribute labour costs between direct labour (sewing operators on specific buyer orders) and manufacturing overhead (supervisors, helpers, normal idle time).

**Wage Structure.** Under the 2023 wage structure for Grade 4 (Assistant Operator), the monthly minimum wage is **BDT 12,500**, comprising basic BDT 6,700, house rent BDT 3,350, medical BDT 750, and transport/food BDT 1,700. The basic wage is 53.6% of gross, satisfying the statutory requirement that basic wage be at least 50% of gross wage.

**Overtime Computation.** A sewing operator earning a basic wage of BDT 6,700 per month works 26 days of 8 hours (208 normal hours). The normal hourly rate based on basic wage is:

\[
\text{Normal hourly rate} = \frac{6,700}{208} = \text{BDT } 32.21
\]

Under Section 108, the overtime rate is **twice the ordinary basic wage rate**: BDT 64.42 per hour. If the operator works **2 hours of overtime per day for 26 days** (52 overtime hours), the overtime earnings are:

\[
52 \times 64.42 = \text{BDT } 3,350
\]

The overtime **premium** is the excess over the normal rate: \(52 \times (64.42 - 32.21) = \text{BDT } 1,675\). If the overtime is worked on general factory workload, this premium is charged to manufacturing overhead. If it is worked at the specific rush request of a European buyer for a particular order, the premium is charged directly to that order’s direct labour cost.

**Productivity Incentive.** Meghna Apparels operates a **group piece-rate bonus** for sewing lines that exceed the daily production target. The bonus pool is shared equally among line members, encouraging cooperation. The cost accountant treats the bonus as **indirect labour cost (manufacturing overhead)** because it relates to line-level performance rather than to traceable individual output.

**Idle Time.** During a two-day power grid failure, the sewing floor is idle but workers are paid. This is **abnormal idle time**; the cost accountant charges the idle wages directly to the **Costing Profit and Loss Account**, excluding them from product cost and inventory valuation. By contrast, the 15 minutes lost each morning to machine setup and tool collection is **normal idle time**, absorbed into manufacturing overhead.

**Turnover Context.** The factory’s monthly dropout rate is approximately **5%**, in line with the garment sector average. The cost accountant estimates replacement costs—recruitment, induction, and the lower productivity of new operators during their learning period—and reports them to management as part of the cost of labour turnover, supporting investment in retention measures such as timely wage payment, respectful supervision, and canteen facilities.

## 5.8 Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Problems

### Problem 1: Overtime Premium and Idle Time Allocation

**Problem Statement.** A worker is paid Tk 60 per hour for a normal 8-hour day. During a week, the worker works 50 hours, of which 6 hours are overtime. Of the overtime hours, 4 hours were worked at the specific request of a customer for Job 101, and 2 hours were due to general factory workload. The worker also lost 3 hours to a power failure. Calculate the allocation of the worker’s total wages to direct labour, manufacturing overhead, and Costing P&L.

**Solution.**

- Normal wages (50 hours × Tk 60) = Tk 3,000.
- Overtime premium (6 hours × Tk 60) = Tk 360.
- **Direct labour to Job 101:** Normal wages for all hours worked (50 × 60) + overtime premium for customer-specific overtime (4 × 60) = 3,000 + 240 = **Tk 3,240**.
- **Manufacturing overhead:** Overtime premium for general workload (2 × 60 = Tk 120) + abnormal idle time (3 hours × Tk 60 = Tk 180) = **Tk 300**.
- **Costing P&L:** Abnormal idle time is already included in overhead above; if the question requires separate treatment, abnormal idle time = **Tk 180**; general overtime premium = Tk 120 to MOH.

**Summary:** Direct labour Tk 3,240; MOH Tk 300 (including abnormal idle time Tk 180); Costing P&L Tk 180 (if separately identified).

---

### Problem 2: Labor Turnover—Separation, Replacement, and Flux Methods

**Problem Statement.** At the beginning of the year, a factory had 1,000 workers. During the year, 200 workers left, and 180 replacements were recruited. At the end of the year, the workforce was 1,050. Calculate the turnover rate under the separation, replacement, and flux methods.

**Solution.**

\[
\text{Average workers} = \frac{1,000 + 1,050}{2} = 1,025
\]

\[
\text{Separation rate} = \frac{200}{1,025} \times 100 = \mathbf{19.51\%}
\]

\[
\text{Replacement rate} = \frac{180}{1,025} \times 100 = \mathbf{17.56\%}
\]

\[
\text{Flux rate (separations + replacements)} = \frac{200 + 180}{1,025} \times 100 = \mathbf{37.07\%}
\]

The flux rate is higher because it captures both outflows and inflows.

---

### Problem 3: Taylor’s Differential Piece-Rate System

**Problem Statement.** Standard output is 100 units per day. The normal piece rate is Tk 10 per unit. Taylor’s system pays 80% of the normal rate for below-standard output and 120% for at-or-above-standard output. Worker A produces 90 units; Worker B produces 110 units. Calculate each worker’s earnings.

**Solution.**

- **Worker A (below standard):** Rate = 80% × Tk 10 = Tk 8. Earnings = 90 × 8 = **Tk 720**.
- **Worker B (at or above standard):** Rate = 120% × Tk 10 = Tk 12. Earnings = 110 × 12 = **Tk 1,320**.

The sharp differential (Tk 720 versus Tk 1,320) illustrates Taylor’s punitive-reward design.

---

### Problem 4: Merrick’s Multiple Piece-Rate System

**Problem Statement.** Standard output is 100 units. Normal piece rate is Tk 10. Merrick’s system pays: up to 83% efficiency—ordinary rate; 83% to 100%—110% of ordinary rate; above 100%—120% of ordinary rate. Calculate earnings for:
- Worker X: 80 units.
- Worker Y: 90 units.
- Worker Z: 110 units.

**Solution.**

- **Worker X (80% efficiency):** Ordinary rate Tk 10. Earnings = 80 × 10 = **Tk 800**.
- **Worker Y (90% efficiency):** Rate = 110% × 10 = Tk 11. Earnings = 90 × 11 = **Tk 990**.
- **Worker Z (110% efficiency):** Rate = 120% × 10 = Tk 12. Earnings = 110 × 12 = **Tk 1,320**.

Merrick’s graduated structure rewards Y more than Taylor’s system would (Taylor would pay Y only the low rate), while still penalising X.

---

### Problem 5: Comparative Earnings—Straight Piece, Halsey, and Rowan

**Problem Statement.** Standard time for a job is 10 hours; the hourly rate is Tk 50. A worker completes the job in 8 hours and produces 20 units. The straight piece rate is Tk 25 per unit. Calculate total earnings and effective hourly rate under (a) straight piece-rate, (b) Halsey Plan (50%), and (c) Rowan Plan.

**Solution.**

**(a) Straight piece-rate:** \(20 \times 25 = \mathbf{Tk\ 500}\). Effective hourly rate = \(500 / 8 = \mathbf{Tk\ 62.50}\).

**(b) Halsey:** \( (8 \times 50) + [50\% \times (10 - 8) \times 50] = 400 + 50 = \mathbf{Tk\ 450}\). Effective hourly rate = \(450 / 8 = \mathbf{Tk\ 56.25}\).

**(c) Rowan:** \( (8 \times 50) + \left[\frac{10 - 8}{10}\right] \times (8 \times 50) = 400 + (0.20 \times 400) = 400 + 80 = \mathbf{Tk\ 480}\). Effective hourly rate = \(480 / 8 = \mathbf{Tk\ 60.00}\).

**Ranking:** Straight piece (Tk 500) > Rowan (Tk 480) > Halsey (Tk 450). The straight piece-rate gives the highest earnings in this case because the piece rate is high relative to the time-based plans.

### Problem 6: Comprehensive Payroll Accounting with Journal Entries

**Problem Statement.** A factory’s monthly gross payroll is Tk 5,00,000. Deductions: Provident Fund Tk 25,000; TDS Tk 15,000. The net payroll is paid by bank. Of the gross payroll, Tk 3,50,000 is direct labour, Tk 1,20,000 is indirect labour, and Tk 30,000 is abnormal idle time. Record the journal entries.

**Solution.**

**1. Record gross payroll and liabilities:**

\[
\text{Dr. Payroll Control} \quad 5,00,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Provident Fund Payable} \quad 25,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Withholding Tax Payable} \quad 15,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Wages Payable} \quad 4,60,000
\]

**2. Record payment:**

\[
\text{Dr. Wages Payable} \quad 4,60,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Bank} \quad 4,60,000
\]

**3. Allocate labour costs:**

\[
\text{Dr. Work-in-Process} \quad 3,50,000
\]
\[
\text{Dr. Manufacturing Overhead Control} \quad 1,20,000
\]
\[
\text{Dr. Costing Profit and Loss Account} \quad 30,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Payroll Control} \quad 5,00,000
\]

The Payroll Control account is now cleared: debit Tk 5,00,000 (gross pay) and credits totalling Tk 5,00,000 (liabilities + cost distribution).

## 5.9 Formula Reference Cheat-Sheet

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|

## 5.10 End-of-Chapter Exam Preparation Pack

### 5.10.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Q1. Distinguish between direct labour and indirect labour. Why does the distinction matter for product costing?**

**Model Answer.** Direct labour is the compensation of workers whose time can be traced economically to specific cost objects (e.g., sewing operators on a specific order). Indirect labour is the compensation of support workers whose time cannot be traced economically (e.g., supervisors, maintenance staff). The distinction matters because direct labour is charged to Work-in-Process and becomes part of product cost, while indirect labour is treated as manufacturing overhead and allocated across products. Misclassifying indirect labour as direct would distort product costs and inventory valuation.

**Q2. Under what circumstances is the overtime premium charged to (a) manufacturing overhead, (b) direct labour to a specific job, and (c) the Costing P&L?**

**Model Answer.** (a) Overtime premium is charged to manufacturing overhead when overtime arises from general factory workload or random scheduling—it benefits all products and is a general factory cost. (b) It is charged directly to a specific job when overtime is worked at the specific rush request of a particular customer; the cost is traceable to that job. (c) It is charged to the Costing P&L when overtime is caused by abnormal emergencies such as equipment sabotage or fire recovery; the cost is abnormal and should not distort product costs.

**Q3. Distinguish between normal idle time and abnormal idle time, and explain their accounting treatment.**

**Model Answer.** Normal idle time is unavoidable in ordinary operations—rest breaks, setup, tool collection—and is absorbed into manufacturing overhead as a legitimate cost of capacity. Abnormal idle time arises from unexpected events—power failure, strike, stockout—and is excluded from product cost and charged to the Costing P&L. The distinction ensures that product costs reflect efficient operating conditions and that abnormal losses are separately visible to management.

**Q4. Explain the three methods of measuring labour turnover.**

**Model Answer.** The separation method divides the number of separations by average workers. The replacement method divides the number of replacements recruited by average workers. The flux method divides the sum of separations and replacements (or accessions) by average workers. The flux method is the most comprehensive because it captures both inflows and outflows. The replacement method excludes recruitment for expansion, focusing only on replacement of departed workers.

**Q5. Why does the Rowan Plan prevent runaway bonus payouts when time standards are loose?**

**Model Answer.** Under Rowan, the bonus is \(\frac{S-T}{S} \times (T \times R)\). As the actual time \(T\) becomes very small (large time saving), the bonus approaches \(T \times R\), so total earnings approach \(2TR\)—but never exceed it. The proportional structure self-limits the bonus, whereas under Halsey the bonus \(50\% \times (S-T) \times R\) can become very large if \(S\) is loosely set. Rowan therefore protects the employer against poorly set standards.

**Q6. What is the Scanlon Plan, and what conditions are necessary for a group incentive scheme to succeed?**

**Model Answer.** The Scanlon Plan is a group incentive based on labour cost savings: a baseline ratio of labour cost to sales value (or value added) is established, and savings below the baseline are shared between workers and the company. Conditions for success include clear targets, equitable sharing, worker participation, stable production processes, and management commitment. Group schemes are appropriate when individual output is difficult to measure and cooperation is essential.

**Essay Question 1.** *“The allocation of overtime premium and idle time is not a mechanical exercise; it requires judgment about causation and controllability.” Discuss.*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Allocation depends on the cause of the cost, not its amount.
- **Section 1: Overtime premium.** General workload (MOH) vs. customer-specific request (direct job) vs. abnormal emergency (Costing P&L).
- **Section 2: Idle time.** Normal (MOH) vs. abnormal (Costing P&L).
- **Section 3: Judgment and consequences.** Misallocation distorts product cost, inventory, pricing, and performance evaluation.
- **Section 4: Bangladesh context.** Overtime under the Labour Act and the importance of correct allocation for cost audit.
- **Conclusion:** Allocation is a causal and controllability judgment, not a routine calculation.

**Essay Question 2.** *Compare and contrast time-wage and piece-wage systems. Under what conditions is each appropriate, and how do differential piece-rate systems modify the basic piece-rate incentive?*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** The choice of wage system shapes productivity, quality, and earnings stability.
- **Section 1: Time-wage.** Mechanics, merits, demerits, suitability.
- **Section 2: Piece-wage.** Straight piece-rate, merits, demerits, suitability.
- **Section 3: Differential systems.** Taylor’s (punitive) and Merrick’s (graduated) modifications.
- **Section 4: Contextual fit.** Quality-critical vs. quantity-driven work; Bangladesh RMG piece-rate practices.
- **Conclusion:** No universally superior system; the choice depends on task characteristics and management objectives.

**Essay Question 3.** *Explain the Halsey, Rowan, Emerson, and Gantt incentive plans. How do their bonus structures differ, and what are the managerial implications of these differences?*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Incentive plans share a common goal—linking pay to productivity—but differ in risk-sharing.
- **Section 1: Halsey and Halsey-Weir.** 50% and 33⅓% sharing; simplicity and strong incentive.
- **Section 2: Rowan.** Proportional bonus; self-limiting protection against loose standards.
- **Section 3: Emerson and Gantt.** Stepped bonuses and guaranteed day wages; less punitive than Taylor.
- **Section 4: Managerial implications.** Standard-setting risk, worker motivation, earnings volatility, administrative complexity.
- **Conclusion:** Plan choice depends on the reliability of time standards and management’s risk preferences.

**Essay Question 4.** *Discuss the causes and financial costs of labour turnover. How can a Bangladeshi garment factory reduce turnover without necessarily increasing wages?*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Turnover is costly—recruitment, training, lost productivity—and the RMG sector averages around 5% monthly.
- **Section 1: Causes.** Wages, workload, working conditions, supervision, seasonal demand.
- **Section 2: Financial costs.** Preventive vs. replacement/curative costs.
- **Section 3: Non-wage retention strategies.** Timely wage payment, respectful supervision, canteen and daycare facilities, career development, safe working conditions (as illustrated by Rising Group’s below-2% dropout rate).
- **Section 4: Measurement.** Separation, replacement, and flux methods for monitoring turnover.

## 5.11 Chapter Summary

Labour is the human resource cost of production. **Direct labour** is traceable to specific cost objects and becomes part of product cost; **indirect labour** is treated as manufacturing overhead. **Gross pay** is total earnings before deductions; **net pay** is gross pay less Provident Fund, group insurance, TDS, and other deductions. **Fringe benefits** follow the direct/indirect classification of the underlying labour unless clearly identifiable with a specific cost object.

**Overtime premium**—the excess of the overtime rate over the normal rate—is allocated according to causation: general workload to **manufacturing overhead**, customer-specific rush requests to **direct labour of the job**, and abnormal emergencies to the **Costing P&L**. **Normal idle time** is absorbed into manufacturing overhead; **abnormal idle time** is charged to the Costing P&L.

**Time-keeping** records attendance; **time-booking** records job allocation. **Labour turnover** is measured by the separation, replacement, and flux methods; its costs include preventive and replacement/curative costs.

**Time-wage systems** pay for hours worked; **piece-wage systems** pay for output. **Taylor’s differential piece-rate** and **Merrick’s multiple piece-rate** modify the straight piece-rate with punitive or graduated incentives. **Halsey** shares time savings 50:50; **Rowan** shares proportionally and self-limits bonus payouts; **Emerson** and **Gantt** provide stepped bonuses with guaranteed day wages. **Group incentive schemes** and the **Scanlon Plan** reward collective performance.

Labour costs are recorded through **payroll journal entries**: gross payroll recognition, payment, and distribution to Work-in-Process, Manufacturing Overhead, and Costing P&L.
