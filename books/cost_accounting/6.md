## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

1. **Classify** overhead by function (factory, administrative, selling and distribution), by behaviour (fixed, variable, semi-variable), and by element (indirect materials, indirect labour, indirect expenses), and **distinguish** production cost centres from service cost centres.
2. **Compute** a predetermined manufacturing overhead rate (POHR) and **justify** its use over actual overhead rates, selecting an appropriate allocation base on causal grounds.
3. **Apportion** overhead across production and service departments using departmental bases (floor area, headcount, horsepower-hours, asset value) in a primary distribution.
4. **Re-apportion** service department costs using the direct, step-down, and reciprocal (simultaneous equation and repeated distribution) methods, and **evaluate** the trade-offs between simplicity and accuracy.
5. **Calculate** applied manufacturing overhead and **determine** whether overhead is underapplied or overapplied, **explaining** the spending and production-volume causes of the variance.
6. **Record** overhead transactions in T-accounts and **dispose** of under- or overapplied overhead through direct write-off to cost of goods sold or proration across work-in-process, finished goods, and cost of goods sold.
7. **Distinguish** administrative overhead from selling and distribution overhead, and **apportion** each using appropriate bases.

**Prerequisite Knowledge:** Chapter 2 (inventoriable versus period costs and the flow of manufacturing costs); Chapter 3 (cost behaviour, cost drivers, and the relevant range); Chapter 4 (materials and stores procedures); Chapter 5 (labour cost classification and payroll accounting); and elementary algebra (simultaneous equations and ratios).

## 6.1 Planning and Control of Overhead

### 6.1.1 The Nature of Indirect Costs

**Overhead** represents expenditure on labour, materials, or services that cannot be economically identified with specific saleable cost units. It is the cost of the resources that support production but are not directly traceable to individual products. Overhead comprises three elements:

- **Indirect materials:** consumables that do not become an integral part of the finished product—lubricants, cleaning agents, store supplies, and minor packaging items.
- **Indirect labour:** the compensation of support workers—supervisors, maintenance technicians, quality inspectors, stores clerks, and security staff.
- **Indirect factory expenses:** the costs of facilities and services—factory rent, depreciation, insurance, electricity, water, and property taxes.

### 6.1.2 Classifications of Overhead

**By function**, overhead is classified as manufacturing (factory) overhead, administrative overhead, and selling and distribution overhead. Manufacturing overhead is an **inventoriable product cost**: it flows through work-in-process and finished goods to cost of goods sold. Administrative and selling and distribution overheads are **period costs**, expensed in the period incurred.

**By behaviour**, overhead is fixed, variable, or semi-variable. Fixed overhead (e.g., factory rent) does not change with volume within the relevant range. Variable overhead (e.g., machine power) changes in proportion to activity. Semi-variable overhead (e.g., factory electricity with a standing charge plus a usage charge) contains both components.

**By element**, overhead is classified as indirect materials, indirect wages, and indirect expenses.

### 6.1.3 Cost Centres: Production versus Service

A **cost centre** is a location, function, or item of equipment for which costs are accumulated. **Production cost centres** are directly engaged in manufacturing—machining, assembly, finishing, and packing departments. **Service cost centres** support production without directly working on the product—maintenance, power house, stores, canteen, quality control, and the personnel department.

The distinction matters because service department costs must ultimately be **re-apportioned** to production departments before they can be absorbed into product costs. A service department does not produce a saleable output of its own; its costs are a cost of providing capacity to production.

## 6.2 Predetermined Manufacturing Overhead Rate (POHR)

### 6.2.1 The Concept and Necessity of POHR

A **predetermined manufacturing overhead rate (POHR)** is calculated *before* the accounting period begins, using budgeted overhead and budgeted activity. It is used throughout the period to apply overhead to production as jobs are completed, rather than waiting until the end of the period when actual overhead is known.

Companies do not use actual monthly overhead rates for three reasons. **First**, actual overhead includes fixed costs that are incurred unevenly across months (e.g., annual insurance premiums paid in one instalment), and dividing a lumpy monthly actual overhead by a seasonal monthly activity base produces a rate that swings wildly and bears no relationship to the true cost of production. **Second**, product costs would be **delayed** until the end of the period, making it impossible to quote prices, prepare interim financial statements, or evaluate job profitability in a timely manner. **Third**, actual overhead rates do not provide a stable benchmark for **control**: managers cannot compare actual spending against a rate that itself fluctuates with actual spending.

### 6.2.2 Formula and Allocation Bases

\[
\text{POHR} = \frac{\text{Estimated (Budgeted) Total Manufacturing Overhead}}{\text{Estimated Total Activity Level of Allocation Base}}
\]

The allocation base should be the **cost driver** most causally related to the overhead. Common bases include:

- **Direct labour hours:** appropriate when overhead is driven by labour time (labour-intensive assembly).
- **Machine hours:** appropriate when overhead is driven by machine usage (capital-intensive machining, spinning, ceramic firing).
- **Direct labour cost (percentage):** appropriate when wage rates are relatively stable and labour cost is a reasonable proxy for overhead consumption.
- **Prime cost (percentage):** combines materials and labour; used when both contribute to overhead.
- **Units of production:** appropriate when all units are homogeneous and consume overhead equally.

The choice of base must satisfy the **cause-and-effect criterion** (Chapter 3): the base should be the activity that actually drives the overhead cost. A machine-intensive department using direct labour hours as its base will misallocate overhead, over-costing labour-intensive products and under-costing machine-intensive ones.

## 6.3 Methods of Determining Allocation Ratios and Secondary Distribution

### 6.3.1 Primary Distribution of Overhead

**Primary distribution** is the process of allocating and apportioning overhead across *both* production and service departments. Some costs are **allocated** in their entirety to a single cost centre (e.g., the salary of a dedicated supervisor); others are **apportioned** in proportion to a suitable base.

| Overhead Item | Apportionment Base |
|---|---|
| Rent, rates, depreciation of building | Floor area |
| Canteen, welfare, personnel | Number of employees (headcount) |
| Electric power | Horsepower-hours or machine-hours |
| Insurance, depreciation of plant | Asset value |
| Supervision | Number of workers or direct labour hours |
| Stores department | Number of requisitions or value of materials issued |

The outcome of primary distribution is a **distribution summary sheet** showing the total overhead of each production and service department.

### 6.3.2 Secondary Distribution: Re-Apportioning Service Costs

**Secondary distribution** (re-apportionment) assigns service department costs to production departments. Four methods are available, differing in how they treat services exchanged *between* service departments.

**Direct method.** Service department costs are apportioned **only to production departments**, completely ignoring services rendered between service departments. It is simple but assumes that service departments do not serve each other—an assumption that is rarely true.

**Step-down (sequential) method.** Service departments are ranked, and their costs are allocated sequentially. The department that renders the **most service to other service departments** is allocated first; once a department’s costs have been allocated, it receives **no further allocation** from departments allocated later (no reverse allocation). The step-down method recognises *some* inter-service relationships but not all.

**Reciprocal (algebraic / simultaneous equation) method.** This method **fully recognises mutual services** exchanged between service departments. If service department \(S_1\) provides a proportion \(a\) of its service to \(S_2\), and \(S_2\) provides a proportion \(b\) of its service to \(S_1\), the total costs are:

\[
S_1 = C_1 + aS_2
\]
\[
S_2 = C_2 + bS_1
\]

Solving these simultaneous equations yields the total cost of each service department, which is then distributed to production departments. The reciprocal method is the most accurate but algebraically demanding.

**Repeated distribution method.** Service department costs are distributed to other service and production departments on agreed percentages, and the process is repeated until the service department balances are exhausted or reduced to a negligible amount. It approximates the reciprocal method without solving simultaneous equations.

### 6.3.3 Comparison Table: Secondary Distribution Methods

| Dimension | Direct Method | Step-Down Method | Reciprocal Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inter-service recognition** | None | Partial (one-directional) | Full (mutual) |
| **Assumption** | Service departments do not serve each other | Services flow in one direction only | Services flow in both directions |
| **Complexity** | Low | Moderate | High (simultaneous equations or iteration) |
| **Accuracy** | Lowest | Intermediate | Highest |
| **Data requirement** | Only service-to-production percentages | Ranking of service departments plus percentages | Full inter-service percentages |
| **Best use** | Quick estimates; negligible inter-service services | Moderate inter-service services | Significant mutual services (e.g., power house and maintenance) |

## 6.4 Overhead Absorption and Applied Manufacturing Overhead

### 6.4.1 Mechanics of Overhead Absorption

**Absorption** (recovery) is the process of charging overhead to production. Overhead is applied to jobs using the POHR:

\[
\text{Applied Overhead} = \text{POHR} \times \text{Actual Level of Allocation Base Used}
\]

If the POHR is Tk 25 per machine-hour and a job uses 400 machine-hours, applied overhead is Tk 10,000. This applied overhead is debited to Work-in-Process and credited to Manufacturing Overhead Applied (or directly to the Manufacturing Overhead Control account, depending on the ledger design).

### 6.4.2 Underapplied and Overapplied Overhead

Because the POHR is based on **estimates**, actual overhead will almost never equal applied overhead.

- **Underapplied overhead** occurs when **actual overhead incurred exceeds applied overhead**. The Manufacturing Overhead Control account carries a **debit balance**. Underapplied overhead means that product costs are **understated**: too little overhead has been charged to jobs, so cost of goods sold is too low and profit is too high. The underapplied amount must be **added** to cost of goods sold.
- **Overapplied overhead** occurs when **applied overhead exceeds actual overhead incurred**. The Manufacturing Overhead Control account carries a **credit balance**. Overapplied overhead means that product costs are **overstated**: too much overhead has been charged to jobs, so cost of goods sold is too high and profit is too low. The overapplied amount must be **deducted** from cost of goods sold.

### 6.4.3 Causes of the Variance: Spending versus Production Volume

The total under- or overapplied variance has two components:

**Spending variance.** Actual overhead spending differs from budgeted overhead for the actual activity level. Poor cost control, unexpected price increases, or inaccurate budgeting produce a spending variance.

**Production-volume variance.** Actual activity differs from the denominator activity used to set the POHR. If the firm operates below the budgeted activity level, fixed overhead is spread over fewer units, and overhead is likely to be **underapplied** even if spending is on budget. If the firm operates above the budgeted level, overhead is likely to be **overapplied**.

### 6.4.4 Comparison Table: Underapplied versus Overapplied Overhead

| Dimension | Underapplied Overhead | Overapplied Overhead |
|---|---|---|
| **Condition** | Actual overhead > Applied overhead | Applied overhead > Actual overhead |
| **Ledger balance in MOH Control** | Debit balance | Credit balance |
| **Effect on product cost** | Product cost understated | Product cost overstated |
| **Effect on profit** | Profit overstated (COGS too low) | Profit understated (COGS too high) |
| **Year-end adjustment** | Add to COGS (or debit WIP/FG/COGS in proration) | Deduct from COGS (or credit WIP/FG/COGS in proration) |
| **Common cause** | Actual activity below denominator; overspending | Actual activity above denominator; underspending |

## 6.5 Accounting for Overheads and Year-End Disposal

### 6.5.1 Ledger Flow

The **Manufacturing Overhead Control** account is **debited** with actual overhead costs incurred (indirect materials, indirect labour, factory rent, depreciation, and so on). The **Manufacturing Overhead Applied** account (or the credit side of the control account) is **credited** with overhead absorbed into Work-in-Process using the POHR. The difference between the debit and credit sides is the under- or overapplied balance.

### 6.5.2 Disposal Methods

**Method 1: Direct write-off to Cost of Goods Sold.** The entire under- or overapplied balance is closed to COGS. This is the **standard procedure for immaterial amounts**, because the cost of proration exceeds the benefit of greater precision. If overhead is underapplied, COGS is debited; if overapplied, COGS is credited.

**Method 2: Proration.** The variance is allocated proportionally across **Work-in-Process, Finished Goods, and Cost of Goods Sold** based on the overhead applied in each account’s ending balance. This method is **required when the variance is material**, because it restates inventory and COGS to reflect actual overhead more faithfully. The allocation percentages are:

\[
\text{Allocation \% to account} = \frac{\text{Overhead applied in the account}}{\text{Total overhead applied in WIP + FG + COGS}}
\]

### 6.5.3 Comparison Table: Write-Off to COGS versus Proration

| Dimension | Write-Off to COGS | Proration across WIP, FG, and COGS |
|---|---|---|
| **When used** | Immaterial variance | Material variance |
| **Accounts adjusted** | COGS only | WIP, Finished Goods, and COGS |
| **Accuracy** | Lower (inventory not restated) | Higher (inventory and COGS restated) |
| **Simplicity** | High | Moderate |
| **Effect on balance sheet** | Inventory remains at applied overhead | Inventory is adjusted towards actual overhead |
| **Effect on income statement** | Entire variance hits current profit | Variance is spread between current and future periods |

## 6.6 Administrative, Selling, and Distribution Overhead

### 6.6.1 Administrative Overhead

**Administrative overhead** comprises the costs of the general management and administration of the organisation—office salaries, office rent, stationery, postage, legal fees, audit fees, and depreciation of office equipment. Administrative overhead is a **period cost**: it is not inventoried as part of product cost and is expensed in the period incurred.

For internal cost analysis, administrative overhead may be absorbed into product cost using bases such as works cost, conversion cost, or sales value. However, under **Bangladesh Cost Accounting Standard (BCAS) 27**, administrative overheads relating to production activities may be included in cost of production, while administrative overheads relating to marketing, project management, and company office expense are excluded. This distinction is important for statutory cost audit.

### 6.6.2 Selling and Distribution Overhead

**Selling overhead** includes the costs of promoting and securing orders—advertising, salesmen’s salaries and commissions, travelling expenses, and market research. **Distribution overhead** includes the costs of storing and delivering finished goods—warehouse rent, packing, freight-out, and delivery van expenses.

These overheads are period costs and are apportioned to products or territories using bases such as **sales value**, **gross profit**, or **physical units sold**. The choice of base should reflect the driver of the cost: freight-out is driven by weight or volume, while advertising may be driven by sales value.

## 6.7 Bangladesh Corporate Vignette: POHR and Reciprocal Apportionment at a Gazipur Ceramic Manufacturer

**Background.** *Meghna Ceramics Ltd.* (a fictionalised export-oriented ceramic tile manufacturer in Gazipur) operates three production departments—**Pressing, Glazing, and Firing**—and two service departments—**Power House** and **Quality Control**. The company exports tiles to the Middle East and Europe and is subject to statutory cost audit under the Cost Audit (Report) Rules 1997.

**POHR on a Machine-Hour Basis.** The Firing department is capital-intensive: its overhead (kiln fuel, refractory replacement, depreciation) is driven by **kiln firing hours**, not labour hours. The budgeted annual overhead for the Firing department is Tk 1,20,00,000, and the budgeted machine-hours are 40,000. The POHR is:

\[
\text{POHR}_{\text{Firing}} = \frac{1,20,00,000}{40,000} = \textbf{Tk 300 per machine-hour}
\]

If a batch of tiles uses 250 firing hours, applied overhead is Tk 75,000.

**Reciprocal Apportionment of Service Costs.** The Power House provides 10% of its service to Quality Control; Quality Control provides 5% of its service to the Power House. The costs are:

- Power House: Tk 8,00,000
- Quality Control: Tk 3,00,000

Let \(P\) = total Power House cost and \(Q\) = total Quality Control cost:

\[
P = 8,00,000 + 0.05Q
\]
\[
Q = 3,00,000 + 0.10P
\]

Substituting:

\[
P = 8,00,000 + 0.05(3,00,000 + 0.10P) = 8,00,000 + 15,000 + 0.005P
\]
\[
0.995P = 8,15,000 \quad \Rightarrow \quad P = \textbf{Tk 8,19,095}
\]
\[
Q = 3,00,000 + 0.10(8,19,095) = \textbf{Tk 3,81,910}
\]

The total service costs (Tk 8,19,095 + Tk 3,81,910 = Tk 12,01,005) are then apportioned to Pressing, Glazing, and Firing on the basis of their service consumption. The reciprocal method captures the mutual service between the power house and quality control—a relationship that the direct method would ignore.

**Year-End Disposal.** At year-end, the Firing department’s actual overhead is Tk 1,25,00,000, while applied overhead (based on 39,000 actual machine-hours × Tk 300) is Tk 1,17,00,000. Overhead is **underapplied by Tk 8,00,000**. Because the amount is material (more than 5% of applied overhead), the cost accountant **prorates** the variance across WIP, Finished Goods, and COGS in proportion to the overhead applied in each account, restating inventory and cost of goods sold to reflect actual firing costs.

**Problem Statement.** A factory has three production departments (A, B, C) and two service departments (Stores and Maintenance). The following overheads are incurred:

- Rent: Tk 1,00,000 (apportioned on floor area: A 400 m², B 300 m², C 200 m², Stores 60 m², Maintenance 40 m²)

Prepare a primary distribution summary.

**Solution.**

**Problem Statement.** Using the same data, re-apportion the service costs using the **step-down method**. Maintenance renders service to Stores (10%) as well as to production (A 35%, B 35%, C 20%). Stores renders service only to production (A 50%, B 30%, C 20%). Maintenance is allocated first because it renders service to the other service department.

**Solution.**

**Step 1: Allocate Maintenance (Tk 11,333).**
- Stores: 10% × 11,333 = Tk 1,133

### Problem 4: Secondary Distribution—Reciprocal Method

**Problem Statement.** Using the same primary totals, solve the reciprocal relationships. Let \(S\) = total Stores cost, \(M\) = total Maintenance cost.

- Stores provides 10% to Maintenance; the rest to production (A 45%, B 27%, C 18%).
- Maintenance provides 20% to Stores; the rest to production (A 32%, B 32%, C 16%).

**Solution.**

\[
S = 13,333 + 0.20M
\]
\[
M = 11,333 + 0.10S
\]

Substituting:

\[
S = 13,333 + 0.20(11,333 + 0.10S) = 13,333 + 2,267 + 0.02S
\]
\[
0.98S = 15,600 \quad \Rightarrow \quad S = \textbf{Tk 15,918}
\]
\[
M = 11,333 + 0.10(15,918) = \textbf{Tk 12,925}
\]

**Allocation to production:**

- A: (45% × 15,918) + (32% × 12,925) = 7,163 + 4,136 = **Tk 11,299**
- B: (27% × 15,918) + (32% × 12,925) = 4,298 + 4,136 = **Tk 8,434**
- C: (18% × 15,918) + (16% × 12,925) = 2,865 + 2,068 = **Tk 4,933**

**Total production department overhead:**

| Department | Primary (Tk) | Service allocation (Tk) | **Total (Tk)** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,08,000 | 11,299 | **1,19,299** |

### Problem 5: POHR, Applied Overhead, and Under/Overapplied Overhead

**Problem Statement.** A department’s budgeted overhead is Tk 4,80,000, and budgeted machine-hours are 24,000. Actual overhead incurred is Tk 5,10,000, and actual machine-hours are 25,000. Calculate the POHR, applied overhead, and the under- or overapplied amount.

**Solution.**

\[
\text{POHR} = \frac{4,80,000}{24,000} = \textbf{Tk 20 per machine-hour}
\]

\[
\text{Applied overhead} = 20 \times 25,000 = \textbf{Tk 5,00,000}
\]

\[
\text{Actual overhead} = \textbf{Tk 5,10,000}
\]

\[
\text{Underapplied overhead} = 5,10,000 - 5,00,000 = \textbf{Tk 10,000}
\]

Overhead is **underapplied by Tk 10,000**. The spending variance is Tk 30,000 unfavourable (actual Tk 5,10,000 vs. budgeted Tk 4,80,000); the production-volume variance is Tk 20,000 favourable (actual 25,000 hours vs. budgeted 24,000 hours, valued at the fixed overhead rate component). The net effect is underapplied overhead of Tk 10,000.

### Problem 6: Year-End Disposal—Write-Off versus Proration

**Problem Statement.** At year-end, overhead is **overapplied by Tk 30,000**. The overhead applied in ending balances is: WIP Tk 68,000; Finished Goods Tk 2,04,000; COGS Tk 4,08,000. Total applied = Tk 6,80,000. Compare the write-off to COGS method with the proration method, and prepare the journal entries.

**Solution.**

**Method 1: Write-off to COGS.**

\[
\text{Dr. Manufacturing Overhead} \quad 30,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Cost of Goods Sold} \quad 30,000
\]

**Method 2: Proration.**

Allocation percentages:
- WIP: \(68,000 / 6,80,000 = 10\%\)
- FG: \(2,04,000 / 6,80,000 = 30\%\)
- COGS: \(4,08,000 / 6,80,000 = 60\%\)

Allocation of the Tk 30,000 overapplied amount:
- WIP: 10% × 30,000 = Tk 3,000
- FG: 30% × 30,000 = Tk 9,000
- COGS: 60% × 30,000 = Tk 18,000

**Journal entry:**

\[
\text{Dr. Manufacturing Overhead} \quad 30,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Work-in-Process} \quad 3,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Finished Goods} \quad 9,000
\]
\[
\quad \text{Cr. Cost of Goods Sold} \quad 18,000
\]

The proration method is more accurate because it restates both inventory and COGS. The write-off method charges the entire variance to current profit, which is acceptable only if the amount is immaterial.

## 6.9 Formula Reference Cheat-Sheet

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Predetermined overhead rate (POHR)** | \( \frac{\text{Budgeted total MOH}}{\text{Budgeted activity base}} \) |
| **Applied overhead** | \( \text{POHR} \times \text{Actual activity base} \) |
| **Underapplied overhead** | \( \text{Actual MOH} - \text{Applied MOH} \) (positive = underapplied) |
| **Overapplied overhead** | \( \text{Applied MOH} - \text{Actual MOH} \) (positive = overapplied) |
| **Reciprocal equations** | \( S_1 = C_1 + aS_2 \); \( S_2 = C_2 + bS_1 \) |
| **Proration percentage** | \( \frac{\text{Overhead applied in account}}{\text{Total overhead applied in WIP + FG + COGS}} \) |
| **Spending variance** | \( \text{Actual overhead} - \text{Budgeted overhead for actual activity} \) |
| **Production-volume variance** | \( (\text{Actual activity} - \text{Denominator activity}) \times \text{Fixed overhead rate} \) |

## 6.10 End-of-Chapter Exam Preparation Pack

### 6.10.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Q1. Why do companies use a predetermined overhead rate instead of an actual overhead rate?**

**Model Answer.** Companies use a POHR because actual overhead is not known until the end of the period, and waiting for actual overhead would delay product costing, pricing, and interim reporting. Actual monthly overhead also includes lumpy fixed costs and seasonal activity fluctuations, producing rates that swing wildly and do not reflect the true cost of production. A POHR, based on annual budgeted overhead and activity, provides a stable, timely, and comparable rate for applying overhead to jobs throughout the year.

**Q2. Distinguish between allocation, apportionment, and absorption of overhead.**

**Model Answer.** **Allocation** assigns an entire cost item to a single cost centre (e.g., a dedicated supervisor’s salary). **Apportionment** divides a cost item among multiple cost centres on a suitable basis (e.g., rent apportioned on floor area). **Absorption** (recovery) charges the overhead of a cost centre to its output using a predetermined rate (e.g., Tk 20 per machine-hour × actual machine-hours). Allocation and apportionment distribute costs to departments; absorption recovers departmental costs from products.

**Q3. Explain the direct, step-down, and reciprocal methods of secondary distribution. Which is most accurate, and why?**

**Model Answer.** The **direct method** apportions service department costs only to production departments, ignoring inter-service services. The **step-down method** allocates service costs sequentially, recognising one-directional services from the first-allocated department to later ones, but not reverse services. The **reciprocal method** fully recognises mutual services using simultaneous equations or repeated distribution. The reciprocal method is the most accurate because it captures the complete network of inter-service relationships; the direct and step-down methods are simpler but approximate.

**Q4. What causes underapplied or overapplied overhead? Distinguish between the spending and production-volume causes.**

**Model Answer.** Underapplied overhead occurs when actual overhead exceeds applied overhead; overapplied occurs when applied exceeds actual. The **spending variance** arises when actual spending differs from the budget for the actual activity level—due to price changes, inefficiency, or poor budgeting. The **production-volume variance** arises when actual activity differs from the denominator activity used to set the POHR: operating below the denominator underapplies fixed overhead, while operating above the denominator overapplies it.

**Q5. When is proration of under- or overapplied overhead required, and how does it differ from a direct write-off to COGS?**

**Model Answer.** Proration is required when the under- or overapplied amount is **material**. It allocates the variance across Work-in-Process, Finished Goods, and Cost of Goods Sold in proportion to the overhead applied in each account’s ending balance, restating both inventory and COGS towards actual overhead. A direct write-off to COGS charges the entire variance to current profit and is acceptable only for immaterial amounts, because it leaves inventory stated at applied rather than actual overhead.

**Q6. Distinguish between administrative overhead and selling and distribution overhead, and explain their treatment in cost accounts.**

**Model Answer.** Administrative overhead comprises the costs of general management and administration (office salaries, rent, audit fees). Selling and distribution overhead comprises the costs of promoting, storing, and delivering finished goods (advertising, sales commissions, warehouse rent, freight-out). Both are **period costs** under GAAP/IFRS and are expensed in the period incurred. For internal costing, they may be absorbed using bases such as works cost or sales value, but under BCAS 27 only administrative overheads relating to production may be included in the cost of production for statutory cost audit.

### 6.10.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions with Structural Outlines

**Essay Question 1.** *“The reciprocal method is theoretically superior, but the step-down method is often preferred in practice.” Critically evaluate this statement with reference to the direct, step-down, and reciprocal methods of secondary distribution.*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** The choice of secondary distribution method involves a trade-off between accuracy and complexity.
- **Section 1: Direct method.** Simplicity and information loss; ignores inter-service services.
- **Section 2: Step-down method.** Recognises one-directional services; requires ranking; practical compromise.
- **Section 3: Reciprocal method.** Full mutual recognition; simultaneous equations or repeated distribution; highest accuracy.
- **Section 4: Practical preference for step-down.** Data availability, computational simplicity, diminishing returns from greater precision, and materiality of inter-service costs.
- **Conclusion:** The reciprocal method is theoretically superior, but the step-down method is often a rational cost-benefit compromise.

**Essay Question 2.** *Explain the mechanics of overhead absorption and the accounting treatment of under- and overapplied overhead. Why is proration considered more accurate than a direct write-off to COGS?*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Absorption uses a POHR; actual overhead rarely equals applied overhead.
- **Section 1: Mechanics of absorption.** POHR × actual activity base; debit WIP, credit MOH Applied.
- **Section 2: Under- and overapplied overhead.** Definitions, ledger balances, profit effects.
- **Section 3: Disposal methods.** Write-off to COGS (immaterial) versus proration (material).
- **Section 4: Superiority of proration.** Restates inventory and COGS; respects the matching principle; avoids distorting current profit when the variance relates to inventory still on hand.
- **Conclusion:** Proration is more accurate but more costly; materiality governs the choice.

**Essay Question 3.** *Discuss the classification of overhead by function, behaviour, and element. Why is this classification essential for cost control and product costing?*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Overhead is heterogeneous; classification enables analysis and control.
- **Section 1: Classification by function.** Factory, administrative, selling and distribution; inventoriable versus period treatment.
- **Section 2: Classification by behaviour.** Fixed, variable, semi-variable; relevance for flexible budgeting and decision-making.
- **Section 3: Classification by element.** Indirect materials, labour, and expenses; basis for cost reduction.
- **Section 4: Importance.** Product costing, variance analysis, responsibility accounting, and cost audit.
- **Conclusion:** Classification is the foundation of overhead planning and control.

**Essay Question 4.** *“Predetermined overhead rates are estimates, and estimates are always wrong.” Discuss the implications of estimation error in overhead absorption and the mechanisms available to correct it.*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** POHRs are based on budgeted overhead and activity; errors are inevitable.
- **Section 1: Sources of estimation error.** Overhead budget error, activity forecast error, choice of allocation base, and changes in the relevant range.
- **Section 2: Consequences.** Under- or overapplied overhead; distorted product costs; pricing and inventory valuation errors.
- **Section 3: Corrective mechanisms.** Year-end disposal (write-off or proration); periodic review of POHRs; activity-based costing to improve causality.
- **Section 4: Managerial implications.** The POHR is a planning tool, not a precision instrument; materiality and cost-benefit govern the sophistication of correction.
- **Conclusion:** Estimation error is manageable through timely review, appropriate disposal, and continuous refinement of the allocation base.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The POHR is set before the period using budgeted overhead and budgeted activity. Options A and D mix actual and budgeted figures.

A. Recognises mutual services between service departments.
B. Allocates service costs only to production departments.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The direct method ignores inter-service services and allocates only to production. Option A describes the reciprocal method; Option C describes the step-down method.

A. Has the highest total cost.
B. Renders the most service to other service departments.
C. Receives the most service from other service departments.
D. Has the fewest employees.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The step-down method begins with the department that renders the maximum service to other service departments.

**MCQ 4.** Underapplied overhead means that:
A. Applied overhead exceeds actual overhead.
B. Actual overhead exceeds applied overhead.
C. The MOH account has a credit balance.
D. Product costs are overstated.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Underapplied overhead occurs when actual overhead is greater than applied overhead, leaving a debit balance in MOH Control. Option A and C describe overapplied overhead; Option D is a consequence of overapplied, not underapplied, overhead.

**MCQ 5.** A debit balance in the Manufacturing Overhead Control account indicates:
A. Overapplied overhead.
B. Underapplied overhead.
C. A zero balance.
D. That overhead has been fully absorbed.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Actual overhead (debit) exceeding applied overhead (credit) leaves a debit balance—underapplied overhead.

**MCQ 6.** The reciprocal method of secondary distribution is best described as:
A. Allocating service costs only to production departments.
B. Allocating service costs sequentially without reverse allocation.
C. Fully recognising mutual services between service departments using simultaneous equations.

**Answer: C.** Rationale: The reciprocal method captures mutual services. Options A and D describe the direct method; Option B describes the step-down method.

**MCQ 7.** Proration of under- or overapplied overhead is required when:
A. The variance is immaterial.
B. The variance is material.
C. The company uses a POHR.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Proration is required for material variances to restate inventory and COGS. Immaterial variances may be written off to COGS.

**MCQ 8.** Administrative overhead is:
A. An inventoriable product cost.
B. A period cost.
C. Always allocated to production.
D. Part of manufacturing overhead.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: Administrative overhead is a period cost, expensed in the period incurred. It is not inventoried unless a portion relates to production under specific cost accounting standards.

**MCQ 9.** The production-volume variance arises because:
A. Actual spending differs from budgeted spending.
B. Actual activity differs from the denominator activity.
C. The POHR is calculated incorrectly.

**Answer: B.** Rationale: The production-volume variance is caused by the difference between actual activity and the budgeted denominator activity used to set the POHR.

**MCQ 10.** In the reciprocal method, the equation \(S_1 = C_1 + aS_2\) indicates that:

## 6.11 Chapter Summary

**Overhead** is indirect expenditure on materials, labour, and services that cannot be economically traced to specific cost units. It is classified by function (factory, administrative, selling and distribution), by behaviour (fixed, variable, semi-variable), and by element (indirect materials, labour, expenses). **Production cost centres** directly manufacture; **service cost centres** support production and must be re-apportioned.

A **predetermined overhead rate (POHR)** is budgeted overhead divided by budgeted activity, set before the period to provide timely and stable overhead application. The allocation base should be the causal cost driver—direct labour hours, machine hours, direct labour cost, prime cost, or units.

**Primary distribution** apportions overhead to all departments. **Secondary distribution** re-apportions service costs to production using the **direct method** (no inter-service recognition), the **step-down method** (sequential, one-directional recognition), or the **reciprocal method** (full mutual recognition via simultaneous equations or repeated distribution).

**Applied overhead** = POHR × actual activity. **Underapplied overhead** (actual > applied) leaves a debit balance and understates product cost; **overapplied overhead** (applied > actual) leaves a credit balance and overstates product cost. The variance has **spending** and **production-volume** components. Year-end disposal is by **direct write-off to COGS** (immaterial) or **proration** across WIP, FG, and COGS (material).

**Administrative overhead** and **selling and distribution overhead** are period costs; for internal costing they may be absorbed using bases such as works cost or sales value, subject to the requirements of BCAS 27.

## 6.12 Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Overhead** | Indirect expenditure on labour, materials, or services that cannot be economically identified with specific cost units. |
| **Production cost centre** | A department directly engaged in manufacturing. |
| **Service cost centre** | A department that supports production without directly working on the product. |
| **Predetermined overhead rate (POHR)** | Budgeted overhead divided by budgeted activity, set before the period for overhead application. |
| **Allocation** | Assignment of an entire cost item to a single cost centre. |
| **Apportionment** | Division of a cost item among multiple cost centres on a suitable basis. |
| **Absorption** | Charging departmental overhead to output using a predetermined rate. |
| **Primary distribution** | Apportioning overhead across all production and service departments. |
| **Secondary distribution** | Re-apportioning service department costs to production departments. |
| **Direct method** | Secondary distribution that ignores inter-service services. |
| **Step-down method** | Secondary distribution that allocates service costs sequentially with no reverse allocation. |
| **Reciprocal method** | Secondary distribution that fully recognises mutual services using simultaneous equations or repeated distribution. |
| **Underapplied overhead** | Actual overhead exceeds applied overhead; debit balance in MOH Control. |
| **Overapplied overhead** | Applied overhead exceeds actual overhead; credit balance in MOH Control. |
| **Proration** | Allocation of under- or overapplied overhead across WIP, Finished Goods, and COGS in proportion to applied overhead. |
