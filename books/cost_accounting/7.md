## 7.1 Nature and Procedure of Contract Costing

### 7.1.1 Definition and Characteristics

**Contract costing** is a specialised form of job costing applied to large-scale, long-duration, high-value, site-based contracts. Basu and Das describe it as the method used for “big jobs which involve heavy expenditure and stretch over a long period and often are undertaken at different sites,” with each contract treated as a separate unit for costing and profit determination. Standard texts define it as “a special form of job costing used for ascertaining cost and profit on contracts undertaken for big jobs like constructing a building, a road” .

The distinguishing characteristics of contract costing are:

- **Long duration:** Contracts typically span more than one accounting period—often two to five years, and sometimes longer for major infrastructure.
- **Site-based activity:** Work is performed at the contractee’s site rather than in the contractor’s factory. The site becomes the cost centre.
- **High value:** Individual contracts involve substantial capital outlay for materials, plant, and labour.
- **Customer-specific:** Each contract is unique, with specifications, drawings, and bills of quantities agreed in advance.
- **Progressive payment:** The contractor receives periodic payments based on work certified by the contractee’s architect or engineer, rather than a single payment on completion .

### 7.1.2 Key Parties and Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Contractor** | The executing party that undertakes the construction work. |
| **Contractee** | The client or buyer who commissions the contract and makes progress payments. |
| **Contract price** | The agreed total price for the entire contract, as specified in the contract agreement. |
| **Architect / Site Engineer** | The contractee’s representative who inspects the work, certifies progress, and issues certificates for payment. |
| **Subcontracting** | The engagement of specialist firms by the contractor for portions of the work (e.g., electrical, plumbing, or piling), the cost of which is a direct expense of the main contract. |
| **Retention money** | A percentage of each certified payment withheld by the contractee as security against defects, released after the defect liability period . |
| **Defect liability period** | The maintenance period after completion during which the contractor must rectify defects; retention money is held until its expiry. |

### 7.1.3 Procedural Workflow

The lifecycle of a contract proceeds through the following stages:

1. **Site establishment:** The contractor mobilises at the site—constructing site offices, stores, and worker accommodation; installing plant and equipment.
2. **Drawing materials:** Materials are issued from the contractor’s central stores, purchased directly for the site, or transferred from other contracts. Site stores are maintained under bin cards and stores ledgers.
3. **Deploying labour squads:** Direct workers are deployed to the site; supervisory and indirect staff are assigned to the contract’s overhead.
4. **Equipment installation:** Specialised plant and machinery (cranes, batching plants, earth-movers) are installed at the site.
5. **Periodic architectural surveys:** The architect or site engineer conducts periodic measurements and issues **progress certificates** specifying the value of work completed and certified.
6. **Progressive billing:** The contractor submits bills based on certified work. The contractee pays the certified value less **retention money** (commonly 5–10% in Bangladeshi public contracts; the standard PPR retention is 5% of the contract price) .
7. **Final account and defect liability:** On completion, the final account is prepared, and retention money is released after the defect liability period—typically 12 months from the completion certificate, and up to two years on major infrastructure projects such as the Dhaka–Ashulia Elevated Expressway .

### 7.1.4 Contract Costing versus Factory Job Costing

| Dimension | Contract Costing | Factory Job Costing |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | Long-term (often multi-year) | Short-term (weeks or months) |
| **Location** | Site-based; work performed at the contractee’s site | Factory-based; work performed within the contractor’s premises |
| **Cost unit** | The entire contract | The individual job or batch |
| **Direct expenses** | A high proportion of costs are direct (site labour, materials, plant, subcontracting) | A lower proportion; many costs are indirect factory overhead |
| **Overhead** | Relatively small proportion; many “factory” overheads become direct site costs | Relatively large proportion of factory overhead to be absorbed |
| **Profit recognition** | Staged, conservative recognition on incomplete contracts | Profit recognised on completion of the job |
| **Revenue** | Progressive payments based on certification | Single payment on delivery |
| **Record-keeping** | Site-based stores, labour, and plant records; architect’s certificates | Factory job cards, material requisitions, and time tickets |

As one standard text summarises: “Contract costing pertains to construction while the job costing is confined to production… Contract costing is adopted in long-term contracts whereas the job costing is confined to finished goods for a small duration of time” .

## 7.2 Preparation of the Contract Account

The Contract Account is a nominal account prepared for each contract separately. It is debited with all costs incurred on the contract and credited with the value of work done, materials returned, plant remaining, and work-in-progress at the year-end.

### 7.2.1 Debit Entries

**Materials:**

- Materials issued from central stores to the site.
- Materials purchased directly for the contract.
- Materials transferred from other contracts.

**Labour:**

- Direct site wages paid and accrued.
- Wages of supervisory staff directly engaged on the contract (where identifiable).

**Plant and Machinery—Two Methods:**

*Method A: Plant Account Method.* The **full original value** of plant sent to the site is debited to the Contract Account. At the year-end, the **depreciated value** of plant remaining at the site is credited to the Contract Account, leaving the depreciation charge as an effective cost of the contract. This method treats the plant as a cost consumed by the contract and is conceptually consistent with the matching principle .

*Method B: Depreciation Method.* Only the **periodic depreciation charge** on plant used for the contract is debited to the Contract Account. The plant itself is carried in a separate Plant Account and does not enter the Contract Account at full value. This method is simpler where plant is shared across contracts and requires careful allocation of depreciation.

**Direct Expenses:**

- Site survey fees, municipal approval charges, architect’s fees (if borne by the contractor).
- Subcontracting costs.
- Site insurance, security, and site office expenses.

**Indirect Overhead:**

- A proportionate share of the contractor’s central office and administrative overhead, allocated on a suitable basis (e.g., contract cost, labour hours, or value of work done).

### 7.2.2 Credit Entries

- **Materials returned to stores** or transferred to other contracts.
- **Materials stolen, destroyed, or lost:** The cost (less any insurance recovery) is credited to the Contract Account and charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account as an abnormal loss.
- **Materials remaining on site** at the close of the period: valued at cost and credited to the Contract Account as closing stock.
- **Plant at site at depreciated value** (under Method A).
- **Work-in-Progress at year-end:** The value of work certified plus the cost of work uncertified is credited to the Contract Account, carrying forward the cost of incomplete work.

## 7.3 Valuation of Work-in-Progress and Core Terminology

### 7.3.1 The Terminology of Certification

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Cost of work certified** | The actual cost incurred on the portion of work that has been completed and formally approved by the contractee’s architect or surveyor. |
| **Value of work certified** | The contract price proportion agreed and formally certified by the architect—i.e., the sales value of the certified work. |
| **Cost of work uncertified** | The actual cost of work completed after the last survey, or work completed but not yet formally certified. It is valued at cost (including a reasonable proportion of profit, if desired, for internal reporting). |
| **Cash received** | The actual money paid by the contractee: Value of work certified less retention money (and less any amounts withheld for other reasons). |
| **Retention money** | The percentage of certified value withheld by the contractee as security against defects during the defect liability period. It is a liability of the contractee to the contractor, released after the defect liability period . |

### 7.3.2 Work Certified versus Work Uncertified

| Dimension | Work Certified | Work Uncertified |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Work approved by the architect/surveyor | Work completed but not yet formally approved |
| **Valuation** | At contract price (certified value) | At cost (actual cost incurred) |
| **Reliability** | High—independently verified | Lower—internal estimate |
| **Use in profit recognition** | Central—profit is recognised only on certified work | Used to compute cost of work certified; may attract a notional profit element if desired |
| **Balance sheet treatment** | Part of WIP; net of cash received and reserves | Part of WIP at cost |

## 7.4 Notional Profit and Determination of Profits and Losses of Incomplete Contracts

### 7.4.1 Calculation of Notional Profit

\[
\boxed{\text{Notional Profit} = \text{Value of Work Certified} - \text{Cost of Work Certified to Date}}
\]

Notional profit is the **difference between the certified value of work done and the cost of that certified work**. It is “notional” because the contract is incomplete: the profit has not been finally realised, and future costs, penalties, and unforeseen events may erode it .

### 7.4.2 The Principle of Prudence

Full notional profit cannot be recognised before total contract completion. The contractor must retain a **contingency reserve** against future unforeseen cost escalation, liquidated damages for delay, rectification of defects during the liability period, and general construction risks. Recognising the entire notional profit on an incomplete contract would overstate current profit and understate the provision for future obligations.

### 7.4.3 Stage-of-Completion Rules for Incomplete Contracts

The traditional rules, standardised in professional examinations and practice, depend on the percentage of the contract price that has been certified:

**Rule 1: Less than 25% certified.** The contract is in its early stages. No profit is transferred to the Profit and Loss Account. The entire notional profit is credited to a **Work-in-Progress Reserve** .

\[
\text{Profit transferred to P\&L} = \text{Nil}
\]

**Rule 2: 25% to less than 50% certified.** One-third of the notional profit, reduced in the ratio of cash received to work certified, is transferred:

\[
\boxed{\text{Profit to P\&L} = \frac{1}{3} \times \text{Notional Profit} \times \frac{\text{Cash Received}}{\text{Work Certified}}}
\]

The remaining two-thirds is retained as a reserve .

**Rule 3: 50% to less than 90% certified.** Two-thirds of the notional profit, reduced in the ratio of cash received to work certified, is transferred:

\[
\boxed{\text{Profit to P\&L} = \frac{2}{3} \times \text{Notional Profit} \times \frac{\text{Cash Received}}{\text{Work Certified}}}
\]

The remaining one-third is retained as a reserve .

**Rule 4: 90% or more certified (nearing completion).** The profit is estimated on the basis of the **total expected contract profit**, using one of the accepted formulas:

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = \text{Estimated Profit} \times \frac{\text{Work Certified}}{\text{Contract Price}}
\]

or

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = \text{Estimated Profit} \times \frac{\text{Work Certified}}{\text{Contract Price}} \times \frac{\text{Cash Received}}{\text{Work Certified}}
\]

or, alternatively, based on cost of work to date:

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = \text{Estimated Profit} \times \frac{\text{Cost of Work to Date}}{\text{Estimated Total Cost}}
\]

The estimated profit is:

\[
\text{Estimated Profit} = \text{Contract Price} - (\text{Cost Incurred to Date} + \text{Estimated Further Cost to Completion})
\]



### 7.4.4 Treatment of Anticipated Losses

If the contract is forecast to yield an overall net loss, the **entire projected loss must be recognised immediately in full** in the current period, irrespective of the stage of completion. This is the prudence principle applied to losses: “When it is probable that total contract costs will exceed total contract revenue, the expected loss should be recognised as an expense immediately” . The loss is charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account, and a provision is created for the foreseeable loss. This treatment applies regardless of whether work has commenced, the stage of completion, or the expected profits on other contracts .

### 7.4.5 Summary Table: Revenue/Profit Recognition Rules on Incomplete Contracts

| Stage of Completion (Work Certified / Contract Price) | Profit Transferred to P&L | Amount Retained as Reserve |
|---|---|---|
| **Less than 25%** | Nil | Entire notional profit |
| **25% to less than 50%** | \( \frac{1}{3} \times \text{Notional Profit} \times \frac{\text{Cash Received}}{\text{Work Certified}} \) | Balance of notional profit |
| **50% to less than 90%** | \( \frac{2}{3} \times \text{Notional Profit} \times \frac{\text{Cash Received}}{\text{Work Certified}} \) | Balance of notional profit |
| **90% or more** | Estimated Profit × \( \frac{\text{Work Certified}}{\text{Contract Price}} \) (or variant with cash received) | Balance of estimated profit not recognised |
| **Any stage—anticipated loss** | Entire projected loss recognised immediately | No reserve; provision for loss created |

## 7.5 Ledger Accounts and Balance Sheet Presentation

### 7.5.1 The Three Key Accounts

**Contract Account:** A nominal account accumulating all contract costs (debit) and crediting work certified, work uncertified, materials returned, and plant at site. The balance represents notional profit or loss.

**Contractee’s Personal Account:** A real/personal account recording amounts due from the contractee. It is debited with the value of work certified and credited with cash received and retention money withheld. The balance is the amount still receivable.

**Work-in-Progress Account:** An asset account accumulating the cost of work certified and uncertified, the recognised profit, and adjusting for cash received and reserves to arrive at the net WIP asset for the Balance Sheet.

### 7.5.2 Balance Sheet Presentation of WIP

The net Work-in-Progress is presented in the Balance Sheet as:

\[
\boxed{\text{WIP in Balance Sheet} = (\text{Cost of Work Certified} + \text{Cost of Work Uncertified} + \text{Recognised Profit}) - (\text{Reserve for Unrealised Profit}) - (\text{Cash Received from Contractee})}
\]

Equivalently, the WIP asset represents the **unbilled or uncertified portion of the contractor’s investment in the contract**, net of amounts already collected. Where cash received exceeds the WIP value, the excess is presented as a **current liability** (amount due to the contractee) rather than an asset. Retention money is disclosed separately as a receivable, reclassified from the contract asset when it becomes due .

### 7.5.3 Escalation Clauses

An **escalation clause** (price adjustment clause) is a contractual provision allowing the contract price to be adjusted for fluctuations in the market prices of key inputs—typically steel, cement, fuel, and labour—during the contract period. Its rationale is to allocate input-price risk between contractor and contractee, preventing the contractor from bearing uncontrollable market volatility and the contractee from paying an excessive risk premium in the tender price.

Bangladesh’s standard public procurement documents provide a **price adjustment formula**:

\[
P = A + B\left(\frac{I_m}{I_o}\right)
\]

where \(P\) is the adjustment factor, \(A\) is the non-adjustable portion, \(B\) is the adjustable portion, \(I_m\) is the index during the month of execution, and \(I_o\) is the base index . For multi-input contracts, the formula expands to:

\[
P_n = a + b\frac{LL_n}{LL_o} + c\frac{FU_n}{FU_o} + d\frac{CE_n}{CE_o} + e\frac{RS_n}{RS_o} + \cdots
\]

where \(LL\) = local labour, \(FU\) = diesel fuel, \(CE\) = cement, \(RS\) = reinforcing steel, and the lowercase coefficients represent the estimated proportions of each cost element in the contract . The indices are typically sourced from the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) monthly bulletin. Price adjustment is usually applicable only after twelve months from contract commencement, and no adjustment is payable on the advance payment portion .

## 7.6 Bangladesh Infrastructure Vignette: Contract Accounting on a Dhaka Flyover Package

**Background.** *Meghna Infrastructure Ltd.* (a fictionalised civil engineering contractor) is executing a **package of the Dhaka–Ashulia Elevated Expressway**, a major project under the Roads and Highways Department (RHD) framework. The contract price is **Tk 240 crore**, and the project is scheduled for completion over three years, with a **two-year defect liability period** . The contract provides for **5% retention money** on each progressive payment and a price adjustment clause for steel, cement, and fuel .

**Year 1 Data (Illustrative, Tk crore):**

- Materials issued to site: 38
- Direct labour: 22
- Plant sent to site (original cost): 30; depreciation for the year: 6
- Direct expenses (site establishment, subcontracting): 14
- Indirect overhead allocated: 4
- Work certified: 90
- Cost of work uncertified: 8
- Cash received: 81 (90 less 10% retention, for illustration; the contractual retention is 5%, but a 10% rate is used here to demonstrate the mechanics)
- Materials at site (closing): 3
- Plant at site (depreciated value): 24

**Preparation of the Contract Account (Depreciation Method):**

| Debit | Tk crore | Credit | Tk crore |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Materials issued | 38 | Work certified | 90 |
| Direct labour | 22 | Work uncertified | 8 |
| Plant depreciation | 6 | Materials at site (closing) | 3 |
| Direct expenses | 14 | | |
| Indirect overhead | 4 | | |
| **Notional profit c/d** | **17** | | |
| **Total** | **101** | **Total** | **101** |

**Notional Profit:** Tk 90 (certified value) − Tk 73 (cost of work certified = total cost Tk 84 less work uncertified Tk 8 and closing materials Tk 3) = **Tk 17 crore**.

**Stage of Completion:** Work certified Tk 90 / Contract price Tk 240 = **37.5%**. This falls in the **25% to less than 50%** band. Applying Rule 2:

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = \frac{1}{3} \times 17 \times \frac{81}{90} = \frac{1}{3} \times 17 \times 0.90 = \mathbf{Tk\ 5.10\ crore}
\]

**Reserve for Unrealised Profit:** Tk 17 − Tk 5.10 = **Tk 11.90 crore**.

**Balance Sheet WIP Presentation:**

\[
\text{WIP} = (73 + 8 + 5.10) - 11.90 - 81 = \mathbf{Tk\ -6.80\ crore}
\]

A negative WIP indicates that cash received exceeds the net book value of work done, which would be presented as a **current liability** (amount due to the contractee) rather than an asset. This is common in early-stage contracts where the contractee has paid more than the cost of certified work.

**Escalation Adjustment.** During the year, reinforcing steel prices rose by 12% over the base index, and the steel coefficient in the price adjustment formula is 0.18. The price adjustment factor is:

\[
P = A + B\left(\frac{I_m}{I_o}\right) = 0.60 + 0.18(1.12) = 0.60 + 0.2016 = \mathbf{0.8016}
\]

applied to the value of work executed in the relevant period. The contractor includes this adjustment in the progressive bill, subject to the engineer’s certification and the twelve-month eligibility threshold .

**Loss Contingency.** In Year 2, a major landslide causes additional site clearance costs of Tk 18 crore, and the revised estimated total cost exceeds the contract price by Tk 12 crore. Under the prudence principle, the **entire anticipated loss of Tk 12 crore is recognised immediately** in Year 2, irrespective of the stage of completion .

## 7.7 Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Problems

### Problem 1: Contract Account—Plant Account Method versus Depreciation Method

**Problem Statement.** A contract commenced on 1 January. The following data relate to the year ended 31 December:

- Materials issued: Tk 2,00,000
- Wages: Tk 1,50,000
- Plant sent to site (1 January): Tk 1,20,000; depreciation @ 15% p.a.
- Direct expenses: Tk 50,000
- Work certified: Tk 5,00,000
- Work uncertified: Tk 40,000
- Cash received: 80% of work certified
- Materials at site (31 December): Tk 20,000

Prepare the Contract Account under (a) the Plant Account Method and (b) the Depreciation Method.

**Solution.**

**(a) Plant Account Method:** The full plant value Tk 1,20,000 is debited; the depreciated value Tk 1,02,000 (1,20,000 − 18,000) is credited.

| Debit | Tk | Credit | Tk |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Materials | 2,00,000 | Work certified | 5,00,000 |
| Wages | 1,50,000 | Work uncertified | 40,000 |
| Plant (original value) | 1,20,000 | Plant at site (depreciated) | 1,02,000 |
| Direct expenses | 50,000 | Materials at site | 20,000 |
| **Notional profit c/d** | **1,42,000** | | |
| **Total** | **6,62,000** | **Total** | **6,62,000** |

**(b) Depreciation Method:** Only the depreciation charge Tk 18,000 is debited.

| Debit | Tk | Credit | Tk |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Materials | 2,00,000 | Work certified | 5,00,000 |
| Wages | 1,50,000 | Work uncertified | 40,000 |
| Depreciation on plant | 18,000 | Materials at site | 20,000 |
| Direct expenses | 50,000 | | |
| **Notional profit c/d** | **1,42,000** | | |
| **Total** | **5,60,000** | **Total** | **5,60,000** |

In both cases, the notional profit is **Tk 1,42,000**. The methods differ only in presentation, not in the ultimate profit recognised.

### Problem 2: Work Certified Less than 25% of Contract Price

**Problem Statement.** Contract price is Tk 20,00,000. Work certified is Tk 4,00,000; cost of work certified is Tk 3,20,000; cash received is Tk 3,60,000. Compute the profit to be transferred to the Profit and Loss Account.

**Solution.** Work certified is 20% of the contract price (4,00,000 / 20,00,000), which is **less than 25%**. Under Rule 1, **no profit is transferred to the P&L**. The entire notional profit of Tk 80,000 (4,00,000 − 3,20,000) is credited to the **Work-in-Progress Reserve**.

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = \mathbf{Nil}
\]

---

### Problem 3: Work Certified between 25% and 50%

**Problem Statement.** Contract price is Tk 30,00,000. Work certified is Tk 12,00,000; cost of work certified is Tk 10,00,000; cash received is Tk 10,80,000. Compute the profit to be transferred to the P&L.

**Solution.** Work certified is 40% of the contract price (12,00,000 / 30,00,000), falling in the **25% to less than 50%** band.

\[
\text{Notional Profit} = 12,00,000 - 10,00,000 = \text{Tk } 2,00,000
\]

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = \frac{1}{3} \times 2,00,000 \times \frac{10,80,000}{12,00,000} = \frac{1}{3} \times 2,00,000 \times 0.90 = \mathbf{Tk\ 60,000}
\]

**Reserve:** Tk 2,00,000 − Tk 60,000 = **Tk 1,40,000**.

### Problem 4: Work Certified between 50% and 90% (with Work Uncertified and Retention)

**Problem Statement.** Contract price is Tk 50,00,000. Work certified is Tk 35,00,000; cost of work certified is Tk 28,00,000; cost of work uncertified is Tk 3,00,000; cash received is Tk 31,50,000 (90% of certified value, i.e., 10% retention). Compute the profit to be transferred and prepare the Balance Sheet extract.

**Solution.** Work certified is 70% of the contract price (35,00,000 / 50,00,000), falling in the **50% to less than 90%** band.

\[
\text{Notional Profit} = 35,00,000 - 28,00,000 = \text{Tk } 7,00,000
\]

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = \frac{2}{3} \times 7,00,000 \times \frac{31,50,000}{35,00,000} = \frac{2}{3} \times 7,00,000 \times 0.90 = \mathbf{Tk\ 4,20,000}
\]

**Reserve:** Tk 7,00,000 − Tk 4,20,000 = **Tk 2,80,000**.

**Balance Sheet WIP:**

\[
\text{WIP} = (28,00,000 + 3,00,000 + 4,20,000) - 2,80,000 - 31,50,000 = \mathbf{Tk\ 90,000}
\]

The WIP asset of Tk 90,000 is presented in the Balance Sheet. Retention money of Tk 3,50,000 (10% of Tk 35,00,000) is disclosed as a receivable.

### Problem 5: Contract Nearing Completion (Over 90%)

**Problem Statement.** Contract price is Tk 80,00,000. Work certified is Tk 76,00,000. Cost incurred to date is Tk 60,00,000; estimated further cost to completion is Tk 8,00,000. Cash received is Tk 68,40,000 (90% of certified value). Compute the profit to be transferred to the P&L using the estimated profit method.

**Solution.** Work certified is 95% of the contract price (76,00,000 / 80,00,000), so the contract is **nearing completion**.

\[
\text{Estimated Total Profit} = 80,00,000 - (60,00,000 + 8,00,000) = \text{Tk } 12,00,000
\]

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = 12,00,000 \times \frac{76,00,000}{80,00,000} = 12,00,000 \times 0.95 = \mathbf{Tk\ 11,40,000}
\]

Alternatively, using the cash-received variant:

\[
\text{Profit to P\&L} = 12,00,000 \times \frac{76,00,000}{80,00,000} \times \frac{68,40,000}{76,00,000} = 12,00,000 \times 0.95 \times 0.90 = \mathbf{Tk\ 10,26,000}
\]

The first formula recognises profit on the basis of work certified; the second additionally weights by cash collection. The conservative approach prefers the second.

### Problem 6: Balance Sheet Presentation with Contractee’s Account

**Problem Statement.** From the following data, prepare the Contractee’s Account and show the relevant Balance Sheet extract:

- Work certified: Tk 20,00,000
- Cash received: Tk 18,00,000
- Retention money: 10% of work certified
- Cost of work certified: Tk 16,00,000
- Cost of work uncertified: Tk 1,00,000
- Profit transferred to P&L: Tk 2,00,000
- Reserve for unrealised profit: Tk 1,00,000

**Solution.**

**Contractee’s Account:**

| Debit | Tk | Credit | Tk |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| To Balance c/d (amount due) | 2,00,000 | By Cash received | 18,00,000 |
| | | By Retention money (10% of 20,00,000) | 2,00,000 |
| | | **Total** | **20,00,000** |
| **Total** | **20,00,000** | | |

The contractee owes Tk 2,00,000, of which Tk 2,00,000 is retention money (receivable after the defect liability period).

**Balance Sheet Extract:**

| Liabilities | Tk | Assets | Tk |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| | | Work-in-Progress: | |
| | | Cost of work certified | 16,00,000 |
| | | Cost of work uncertified | 1,00,000 |
| | | Add: Recognised profit | 2,00,000 |
| | | Less: Reserve for unrealised profit | (1,00,000) |
| | | Less: Cash received | (18,00,000) |
| | | **Net WIP** | **—** |
| | | Contractee’s Account (retention receivable) | 2,00,000 |

The net WIP is zero (or a liability if cash received exceeds the net book value); the retention receivable of Tk 2,00,000 is shown as a current asset.

## 7.8 Formula Reference Cheat-Sheet

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Notional Profit** | Value of Work Certified − Cost of Work Certified |
| **Rule 1 (<25%)** | Profit to P&L = Nil; entire notional profit to WIP Reserve |
| **Rule 2 (25–50%)** | \( \frac{1}{3} \times \text{Notional Profit} \times \frac{\text{Cash Received}}{\text{Work Certified}} \) |
| **Rule 3 (50–90%)** | \( \frac{2}{3} \times \text{Notional Profit} \times \frac{\text{Cash Received}}{\text{Work Certified}} \) |
| **Rule 4 (≥90%)** | Estimated Profit × \( \frac{\text{Work Certified}}{\text{Contract Price}} \) (or variant with cash received) |
| **Estimated Profit** | Contract Price − (Cost to Date + Estimated Further Cost) |
| **WIP in Balance Sheet** | (Cost Certified + Cost Uncertified + Recognised Profit) − Reserve − Cash Received |
| **Retention Money** | Certified Value × Retention % |
| **Cash Received** | Certified Value − Retention Money − Other Deductions |
| **Price Adjustment Factor (single input)** | \( P = A + B(I_m / I_o) \) |
| **Price Adjustment Multi-Input** | \( P_n = a + b(LL_n/LL_o) + c(FU_n/FU_o) + d(CE_n/CE_o) + \cdots \) |

### 7.9.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Q1. Define contract costing and explain how it differs from job costing.**

**Model Answer.** Contract costing is a specialised form of job costing applied to large-scale, long-duration, site-based contracts such as bridges, highways, and buildings. It differs from job costing in duration (multi-year versus short-term), location (site-based versus factory-based), the proportion of direct costs (high in contract costing), and profit recognition (staged, conservative recognition on incomplete contracts versus recognition on completion). In contract costing, each contract is a separate cost unit, and progressive payments are made based on architect certification .

**Q2. Explain the terms “work certified,” “work uncertified,” and “retention money.”**

**Model Answer.** Work certified is the portion of work completed and formally approved by the contractee’s architect, valued at the contract price proportion. Work uncertified is work completed but not yet formally approved, valued at cost. Retention money is a percentage of certified value withheld by the contractee as security against defects, released after the defect liability period .

**Q3. Why is only a portion of notional profit recognised on an incomplete contract?**

**Model Answer.** Notional profit is the difference between the certified value and the cost of certified work. It is not final because the contract is incomplete and future costs, penalties, and unforeseen events may reduce it. The prudence principle requires retaining a contingency reserve against future cost escalation, liquidated damages, and defect rectification. Recognising the full notional profit would overstate current profit and understate future obligations.

**Q4. State the four traditional rules for profit recognition on incomplete contracts.**

**Model Answer.** (1) Less than 25% certified: no profit transferred; entire notional profit to reserve. (2) 25% to less than 50%: one-third of notional profit, reduced by the cash-received-to-work-certified ratio, transferred. (3) 50% to less than 90%: two-thirds of notional profit, similarly reduced, transferred. (4) 90% or more: profit estimated on total expected profit, multiplied by work certified/contract price (or cash received/contract price) .

**Q5. How is an anticipated loss on a contract treated?**

**Model Answer.** If total expected contract costs exceed total expected revenue, the entire anticipated loss is recognised immediately in the current period, irrespective of the stage of completion or whether work has commenced. This is the prudence principle: losses are recognised as soon as they become evident, while profits are recognised only when realised with reasonable certainty .

**Q6. Explain the two methods of treating plant in the Contract Account.**

**Model Answer.** Under the **Plant Account Method**, the full original value of plant sent to site is debited to the Contract Account, and the depreciated value of plant remaining at the year-end is credited; the net effect is the depreciation charge. Under the **Depreciation Method**, only the periodic depreciation charge is debited; the plant itself is carried in a separate Plant Account. Both methods yield the same notional profit, differing only in presentation .

## 7.10 Chapter Summary

**Contract costing** is a specialised form of job costing for large-scale, long-duration, site-based contracts. Each contract is a separate cost unit. The **Contract Account** is debited with materials, labour, plant (under either the Plant Account Method or the Depreciation Method), direct expenses, and overhead, and credited with work certified, work uncertified, materials returned, and plant at site.

**Notional profit** is the excess of the value of work certified over its cost. Because the contract is incomplete, profit is recognised conservatively under **stage-of-completion rules**: no profit below 25% certified; one-third (adjusted for cash collection) at 25–50%; two-thirds at 50–90%; and estimated total profit apportioned by work certified or cash received at 90% or above. **Anticipated losses are recognised in full immediately.**

**Work-in-progress** is presented in the Balance Sheet net of cash received and reserves: (cost certified + cost uncertified + recognised profit) − reserve − cash received. **Retention money** is withheld as defect security and released after the **defect liability period**. **Escalation clauses** adjust the contract price for input-price fluctuations using index-based formulas, typically involving steel, cement, and fuel.
