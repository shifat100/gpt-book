## 8.1 Nature and Fundamentals of Service Costing (Operating Costing)

### 8.1.1 Definition and Scope

**Service costing**—also called **operating costing**—is the cost accounting methodology applied by undertakings that provide services rather than manufacture physical goods. Basu and Das describe operating costing as the method used “in those industries which provide services instead of manufacturing tangible products,” where the cost unit is typically a service unit rather than a physical article. The Institute of Cost Accountants of India defines operating costing as “a method of costing used to ascertain the cost of services rendered” by organisations such as transport companies, hospitals, hotels, educational institutions, electricity supply companies, and municipal services.

Unlike manufacturing costing, where the cost object is a tangible product that can be stored and inventoried, service costing accumulates costs around a **service delivery process**. The cost accountant must identify the cost centres through which the service is delivered, classify costs by behaviour and traceability, and select a cost unit that reflects the service output.

### 8.1.2 Distinctive Characteristics of Services

Four characteristics distinguish services from physical goods and shape the design of service costing systems:

**Intangibility.** Services cannot be seen, touched, or stored. A bus journey, a lecture, and a surgical operation are experienced rather than possessed. Consequently, service costs cannot be attached to a physical inventory; they must be accumulated by cost centre and period and recovered through the service price.

**Simultaneity of production and consumption.** A service is produced and consumed at the same moment. A seat on a bus is produced and consumed during the journey; an ICU bed-day is produced and consumed while the patient occupies it. There is no interval between production and sale in which unsold service capacity can be inventoried. This creates the **perishability of capacity**: an empty seat or an unoccupied bed represents revenue irretrievably lost, and its cost must be recovered from the services actually sold.

**Non-inventoriability.** Because services cannot be stored, the cost of unused capacity cannot be carried forward as inventory. It must be absorbed into the cost of the services actually delivered, which is why service cost per unit rises when capacity utilisation falls.

**Heterogeneity.** Services vary in quality and resource consumption across deliveries. No two bus journeys, hospital admissions, or academic semesters consume identical resources. Costing systems must therefore use **average cost units** (cost per passenger-km, cost per patient-day) while recognising that individual service episodes may differ substantially.

### 8.1.3 Cost Unit Selection: Simple and Composite Units

A **cost unit** is the unit of service for which cost is ascertained. Service industries use two broad categories:

**Simple cost units** measure a single dimension of service output: per kilometre travelled, per meal served, per bed occupied, per litre of water supplied, per kilowatt-hour of electricity generated.

**Composite (compound) cost units** combine two or more dimensions—typically **volume, weight, or capacity** with **distance or time**. They are necessary because the cost of many services depends jointly on how much is carried or accommodated and how far or how long it is carried or accommodated.

| Service Sector | Simple Cost Unit | Composite Cost Unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Passenger transport** | Per bus, per trip | **Passenger-kilometre** (passenger × km) |
| **Freight transport** | Per truck, per trip | **Ton-kilometre** (ton × km); quintal-kilometre |
| **Hospital** | Per bed, per visit | **Patient-day** (patient × day); bed-day |
| **Education** | Per student, per course | **Student-credit hour**; cost per student per year |
| **Hotel** | Per room, per night | **Room-night**; guest-day |
| **Electricity** | Per connection | **Kilowatt-hour** |

Composite units may be computed in two ways. The **absolute (weighted) method** calculates the composite unit separately for each leg or segment of a service and sums the results. The **commercial (simple average) method** multiplies an average load or occupancy by the total distance or time. Both methods are examined in Section 8.2.

## 8.2 Accounting System of Transport Companies

### 8.2.1 Operating Cost Classification for Passenger and Freight Services

Transport operating costs are conventionally classified into three groups according to their behaviour with respect to distance travelled and trips operated:

**1. Standing (Fixed) Charges.** These costs are incurred regardless of the distance run or the load carried. They include vehicle registration fees, road tax, vehicle insurance, route permit fees, garage rent, the fixed monthly salaries of drivers and conductors, administrative salaries, and interest on capital borrowed to acquire vehicles. Standing charges are **fixed within the relevant range** of trips and distance.

**2. Maintenance (Semi-Variable) Charges.** These costs have both fixed and variable elements. They include tyres and tubes, periodic overhauls, battery maintenance, workshop repairs, painting, and cleaning and servicing. Maintenance costs are partly driven by distance (tyre wear, engine hours) and partly by time (battery deterioration, periodic servicing intervals).

**3. Running (Operating / Variable) Charges.** These costs vary directly with distance travelled and with the load carried. They include diesel, petrol, CNG, or gas consumption; engine lubricants and transmission oil; mileage-based driver commissions; toll charges on expressways and bridges; and tyre replacement attributable to distance.

### 8.2.2 Calculating Composite Units

**Passenger-kilometres.** The annual passenger-kilometres generated by a fleet are:

\[
\boxed{\text{Passenger-km} = \text{Number of Vehicles} \times \text{Trips per Day} \times \text{Distance per Trip} \times \text{Seating Capacity} \times \text{Occupancy Rate} \times \text{Operating Days}}
\]

The **occupancy rate** (load factor) is the proportion of available seats actually occupied. A 42-seat bus operating at 85% occupancy carries, on average, 35.7 passengers. Passenger-kilometres measure the *revenue-earning output* of passenger transport.

**Absolute (weighted) ton-kilometres.** For a freight vehicle whose load changes across route segments:

\[
\boxed{\text{Absolute Ton-km} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\text{Weight in Tons on Leg } i \times \text{Distance in Km on Leg } i)}
\]

This method recognises that carrying 10 tons for 100 km and 5 tons for another 100 km is not the same as carrying 7.5 tons for 200 km. It is the more accurate measure of physical transport work.

**Commercial (simple average) ton-kilometres.** For a vehicle carrying an average load over a total distance:

\[
\boxed{\text{Commercial Ton-km} = \text{Average Load in Tons} \times \text{Total Distance in Km}}
\]

The commercial method is simpler but less precise; it is used where load variation is modest or where a quick estimate is sufficient.

The Transport Operating Cost Sheet aggregates the three cost groups and divides total operating cost by the composite units generated to obtain cost per passenger-km or ton-km. A target profit margin is then added to determine the fare or freight rate.

### 8.2.4 Comparison Table: Absolute versus Commercial Ton-Kilometres

| Dimension | Absolute (Weighted) Ton-Km | Commercial (Simple Average) Ton-Km |
|---|---|---|
| **Method** | Sum of (weight × distance) for each leg | Average load × total distance |
| **Accuracy** | High—reflects load changes across segments | Lower—assumes a uniform average load |
| **Data requirement** | Leg-by-leg weight and distance records | Total distance and average load |
| **Best use** | Freight with substantial load variation; billing by actual transport work | Quick estimates; stable loads; aggregate planning |
| **Example** | 10 t × 100 km + 5 t × 100 km = 1,500 t-km | 7.5 t × 200 km = 1,500 t-km (equal only if average is exact) |

## 8.3 Accounting System of Educational Institutes

### 8.3.1 Institutional Cost Centres

Educational institutions deliver teaching and research through a network of cost centres:

- **Academic departments:** Each faculty or department (Engineering, Business Administration, Pharmacy) is a cost centre consuming faculty salaries, laboratory consumables, and teaching materials.
- **Library:** Acquisitions, physical books, digital journal subscriptions, and library staff.
- **Computer laboratories:** Hardware, software licences, maintenance, and laboratory instructors.
- **Examination wing:** Question-setting, printing, invigilation, and script evaluation.
- **Administration:** Vice-chancellor’s office, registrar, admissions, accounts, and human resources.
- **Student hostels and welfare:** Accommodation, catering, and student activities.

Costs are first accumulated in these centres and then **allocated or apportioned** to academic programmes, often using bases such as student headcount, credit hours taught, faculty contact hours, or floor area.

### 8.3.2 Cost Classification in Educational Institutions

| Cost Category | Examples | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Faculty and academic salaries** | Professors, lecturers, teaching assistants | Fixed (salaried) |
| **Administrative and support staff** | Registrar, accounts, library staff, lab technicians | Fixed |
| **Academic consumables** | Laboratory reagents, software, examination paper printing | Semi-variable |
| **Infrastructure** | Campus rent, building depreciation, power and utilities, internet and software licences | Fixed (with some variable utilities) |
| **Library** | Book acquisitions, digital journal subscriptions, database licences | Fixed (annual subscriptions) |

### 8.3.3 Composite Cost Units in Education

Two composite units are widely used:

**Cost per student per academic year:**

\[
\text{Cost per Student} = \frac{\text{Total Institutional Cost for the Year}}{\text{Number of Enrolled Students}}
\]

**Cost per student-credit hour:** This unit recognises that programmes differ in their credit requirements and resource intensity:

\[
\text{Cost per Student-Credit Hour} = \frac{\text{Total Cost Allocated to the Programme}}{\text{Total Student-Credit Hours Delivered by the Programme}}
\]

where total student-credit hours = number of students × credit hours per student.

## 8.4 Accounting System of Hospitals

### 8.4.1 Healthcare Cost Centres

Hospitals are among the most complex service organisations because they combine intensive direct patient care with sophisticated diagnostic and surgical support services. Cost centres include:

- **Direct medical / inpatient departments:** General wards, single cabins, twin-sharing cabins, **Intensive Care Unit (ICU)**, **Coronary Care Unit (CCU)**, **Neonatal ICU (NICU)**, and High Dependency Unit (HDU).
- **Outpatient Department (OPD) and Emergency Room:** Consultation rooms, triage, and emergency resuscitation.
- **Diagnostic service cost centres:** Pathology laboratory, radiology and imaging (MRI, CT, ultrasound, X-ray), and cardiac diagnostics.
- **Surgical theatres (OT) and anaesthesia:** Operating theatres, recovery rooms, and anaesthesiology services.
- **General support centres:** Pharmacy, laundry, diet and kitchen, central sterilisation, and medical waste disposal.

Support centres are **service cost centres** and must be re-apportioned to the direct patient-care centres using the methods discussed in Chapter 6 (direct, step-down, or reciprocal).

### 8.4.2 Cost Units in Healthcare

Healthcare uses several cost units depending on the service:

- **Cost per patient-day (bed-day):** The cost of maintaining one inpatient for one day, including room, nursing, food, and routine monitoring. This is the primary unit for inpatient departments.
- **Cost per outpatient visit:** The cost of a single OPD consultation, including doctor time and examination-room overhead.
- **Cost per diagnostic test:** The cost of performing one pathology, radiology, or imaging test, including equipment depreciation and consumables.
- **Cost per surgical procedure:** The cost of theatre time, anaesthesia, consumables, and recovery for a specific procedure.

### 8.4.3 Cost Classification in Healthcare

| Cost Category | Examples | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Medical staff** | Doctors’ and specialists’ honoraria, permanent consultants | Fixed (salaried) or semi-variable (fee-for-service) |
| **Nursing and ward staff** | Permanent nurses, ward boys, ICU technicians | Fixed |
| **Pharmaceuticals and consumables** | Medicines, syringes, dressings, implants | Variable |
| **Biomedical equipment** | Depreciation of MRI, CT, ventilators, monitors | Fixed |
| **Support services** | Linen washing, patient diet, room maintenance and disinfection | Semi-variable |

### 8.4.4 Hospital Operational Metrics

**Bed-occupancy rate** measures the proportion of available bed-days actually occupied:

\[
\boxed{\text{Bed-Occupancy Rate} = \frac{\text{Actual Bed-Days Occupied}}{\text{Available Bed-Days}} \times 100}
\]

Available bed-days = number of beds × number of days in the period. A hospital with 100 beds and a 75% occupancy rate generates 75 occupied bed-days per day, or 27,375 occupied bed-days in a 365-day year.

Because fixed costs (nursing salaries, depreciation, rent) are incurred whether or not beds are occupied, a **low occupancy rate raises the cost per patient-day**: the same fixed cost is spread over fewer occupied days. This is the healthcare analogue of the unit-cost trap discussed in Chapter 2.

## 8.5 Summary Comparison Tables

### 8.5.1 Product Costing versus Service (Operating) Costing

| Dimension | Product (Manufacturing) Costing | Service (Operating) Costing |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost object** | Tangible product or batch | Service delivery process or service episode |
| **Cost unit** | Unit, kilogram, batch | Composite unit (passenger-km, patient-day, credit hour) |
| **Inventory** | Raw materials, WIP, finished goods | Generally no inventory of output; supplies only |
| **Production and consumption** | Separated by time and space | Simultaneous |
| **Cost classification** | Direct materials, direct labour, overhead | Standing, maintenance, running charges; direct and support centres |
| **Focus** | Product cost for inventory valuation and pricing | Cost per service unit for tariff-setting and capacity utilisation |
| **Perishability** | Output can be stored | Output cannot be stored; unused capacity is lost revenue |

### 8.5.2 Transport versus Education versus Hospital Costing

| Dimension | Transport | Educational Institution | Hospital |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary cost centres** | Vehicles, routes, maintenance depot | Academic departments, library, labs, administration | Inpatient wards, ICU, OPD, diagnostics, OT, support services |
| **Cost units** | Passenger-km, ton-km | Cost per student, student-credit hour | Patient-day, outpatient visit, diagnostic test, procedure |
| **Major cost components** | Fuel, driver salaries, maintenance, insurance | Faculty salaries, library, infrastructure, administration | Medical and nursing staff, medicines, equipment depreciation, support services |
| **Key operational metric** | Occupancy rate (load factor); km per litre | Student-faculty ratio; credit hours per faculty | Bed-occupancy rate; average length of stay |
| **Tariff-setting basis** | Cost per passenger-km or ton-km plus margin | Cost per credit hour plus surplus margin | Cost per patient-day plus margin |

## 8.6 Bangladesh Service Sector Vignette: Transport, Education, and Healthcare Costing in Practice

### (a) Intercity Transport: Dhaka–Chattogram Expressway Operations

*Meghna Express Ltd.* (a fictionalised intercity bus operator modelled on Green Line Paribahan and Hanif Enterprise) operates AC and non-AC coaches on the **Dhaka–Chattogram corridor**, a distance of approximately **250 kilometres** via the Dhaka–Chattogram Expressway. Market fares in 2025–2026 range from **Tk 650 for non-AC** (Hanif Enterprise) to **Tk 1,400 for AC** (Green Line Paribahan).

**Illustrative annual operating data for a fleet of 20 AC coaches:**

- Seating capacity: 40 passengers per coach.
- Trips per day: 2 (round trip), operating 350 days per year.
- Occupancy rate: 75% (30 passengers per trip).
- Distance per trip: 250 km.

**Passenger-kilometres:**

\[
20 \times 2 \times 250 \times 40 \times 0.75 \times 350 = \mathbf{10,50,00,000 \text{ passenger-km}}
\]

**Cost data (Tk crore):**

- Standing charges: vehicle insurance, road tax, permits, fixed driver and conductor salaries, garage rent, administrative salaries, interest: **Tk 12.00 crore**.
- Maintenance charges: tyres, overhauls, repairs, servicing: **Tk 6.00 crore**.
- Running charges: diesel, lubricants, tolls, mileage commissions: **Tk 18.00 crore**.
- **Total operating cost: Tk 36.00 crore.**

\[
\text{Cost per passenger-km} = \frac{36,00,00,000}{10,50,00,000} = \mathbf{Tk\ 3.43 \text{ per passenger-km}}
\]

Adding a 20% profit margin on cost, the fare per passenger-km would be Tk 4.12. For a 250-km journey, the theoretical fare is \(250 \times 4.12 = \mathbf{Tk\ 1,029}\). The actual AC fare of Tk 1,400 reflects premium service, expressway tolls, and higher occupancy during peak periods. The cost accountant’s role is to monitor the **load factor**: if occupancy falls to 60%, cost per passenger-km rises to \(36,00,00,000 / (20 \times 2 \times 250 \times 40 \times 0.60 \times 350) = \mathbf{Tk\ 4.29}\), squeezing the margin unless fares rise or costs fall.

### (b) Private University: Cost per Credit Hour in Dhaka

*Meghna University* (a fictionalised private university modelled on institutions such as IUB, where tuition is **Tk 6,500 per credit** for undergraduate programmes, with laboratory and activity fees of **Tk 8,000 per semester**) offers two faculties: **Engineering** and **Business Administration**.

**Annual data:**

- Engineering: 1,200 students, each taking 30 credit hours per year; laboratory-intensive; cost allocated Tk 18.00 crore.
- Business Administration: 1,800 students, each taking 30 credit hours per year; classroom-based; cost allocated Tk 13.50 crore.

**Student-credit hours:**

- Engineering: \(1,200 \times 30 = 36,000\) credit hours.
- Business: \(1,800 \times 30 = 54,000\) credit hours.

\[
\text{Cost per credit hour (Engineering)} = \frac{18,00,00,000}{36,000} = \mathbf{Tk\ 5,000}
\]

\[
\text{Cost per credit hour (Business)} = \frac{13,50,00,000}{54,000} = \mathbf{Tk\ 2,500}
\]

Engineering costs twice as much per credit hour because of laboratory equipment, workshop consumables, and lower student-faculty ratios. A **uniform** tuition fee of Tk 6,500 per credit would over-recover Business costs (surplus of Tk 4,000 per credit hour) while under-recovering Engineering costs if the fee were set at the institutional average. Differential programme costing supports a rational fee structure: Engineering might be priced at Tk 7,500 per credit and Business at Tk 4,500 per credit, each generating an appropriate surplus while reflecting underlying resource consumption.

### (c) Private Hospital: General Ward versus ICU Costing in Dhaka

*Meghna Health Ltd.* (a fictionalised private hospital modelled on Square Hospital, where ward charges are **Tk 6,000 per day** and ICU charges **Tk 16,500 per day**) operates 200 general-ward beds and 40 ICU beds. The hospital operates 365 days per year. The general ward’s bed-occupancy rate is 75%; the ICU’s is 85%.

**Available and occupied bed-days:**

- General ward: available \(200 \times 365 = 73,000\); occupied \(73,000 \times 0.75 = 54,750\).
- ICU: available \(40 \times 365 = 14,600\); occupied \(14,600 \times 0.85 = 12,410\).

**Annual cost data (Tk crore):**

| Cost | General Ward | ICU |
|---|---:|---:|
| Doctors’ honoraria and specialist fees | 4.00 | 6.00 |
| Nursing and ward staff salaries | 6.00 | 8.00 |
| Medicines and consumables | 3.00 | 5.00 |
| Depreciation of equipment | 1.00 | 4.00 |
| Linen, diet, and room maintenance | 2.00 | 1.50 |
| Apportioned support-centre costs | 2.00 | 2.50 |
| **Total operating cost** | **18.00** | **27.00** |

\[
\text{Cost per patient-day (General Ward)} = \frac{18,00,00,000}{54,750} = \mathbf{Tk\ 3,288}
\]

\[
\text{Cost per patient-day (ICU)} = \frac{27,00,00,000}{12,410} = \mathbf{Tk\ 21,757}
\]

The ICU cost per patient-day is more than six times the general-ward cost, reflecting intensive nursing, ventilator and monitoring equipment, and expensive consumables. The hospital’s published tariffs—Tk 6,000 for the ward and Tk 16,500 for the ICU—reflect pricing strategy, cross-subsidisation, and competitive positioning. The cost accountant’s analysis reveals that the **general ward generates a substantial margin over cost**, while the **ICU tariff is below the computed cost per patient-day** at the assumed cost structure; management must either review ICU cost efficiency (nursing ratios, equipment utilisation) or reconsider ICU pricing.

## 8.7 Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Problems

### Problem 1: Passenger-Kilometres with Round Trips and Occupancy

**Problem Statement.** A transport company operates 8 buses. Each bus makes 3 round trips per day over a route of 120 km one way. Each bus has a seating capacity of 45 passengers, and the average occupancy rate is 80%. The company operates 300 days per year. Calculate the annual passenger-kilometres.

**Solution.**

A round trip covers \(120 \times 2 = 240\) km. The annual passenger-km is:

\[
8 \times 3 \times 240 \times 45 \times 0.80 \times 300 = \mathbf{6,22,08,000 \text{ passenger-km}}
\]

### Problem 2: Absolute versus Commercial Ton-Kilometres

**Problem Statement.** A freight truck makes a three-leg journey:

- Leg 1: carries 12 tons for 80 km.
- Leg 2: carries 8 tons for 120 km.
- Leg 3: carries 5 tons for 100 km.

Calculate (a) absolute ton-kilometres and (b) commercial ton-kilometres using the average load over the total distance.

**Solution.**

**(a) Absolute ton-km:**

\[
(12 \times 80) + (8 \times 120) + (5 \times 100) = 960 + 960 + 500 = \mathbf{2,420 \text{ ton-km}}
\]

**(b) Commercial ton-km:**

Total distance = \(80 + 120 + 100 = 300\) km. Average load = \((12 + 8 + 5) / 3 = 8.33\) tons.

\[
8.33 \times 300 = \mathbf{2,500 \text{ ton-km (approximately)}}
\]

The commercial method overstates the transport work because it assumes the higher average load was carried over the entire distance. The absolute method is more accurate for billing and cost analysis.

---

### Problem 3: Comprehensive Transport Operating Cost Sheet with Profit on Takings

**Problem Statement.** A bus operator provides the following annual data:

- Standing charges: Tk 8,00,000
- Maintenance charges: Tk 4,00,000
- Running charges: Tk 12,00,000
- Passenger-kilometres: 80,00,000

The operator desires a profit of **20% on takings (revenue)**. Compute the cost per passenger-km and the fare per passenger-km.

**Solution.**

\[
\text{Total operating cost} = 8,00,000 + 4,00,000 + 12,00,000 = \text{Tk } 24,00,000
\]

\[
\text{Cost per passenger-km} = \frac{24,00,000}{80,00,000} = \mathbf{Tk\ 0.30}
\]

If profit is 20% **on takings**, then cost is 80% of takings:

\[
\text{Fare per passenger-km} = \frac{0.30}{1 - 0.20} = \frac{0.30}{0.80} = \mathbf{Tk\ 0.375}
\]

For a 200-km journey, the fare is \(200 \times 0.375 = \mathbf{Tk\ 75}\).

### Problem 4: Educational Operating Cost Sheet—Cost per Student and per Credit Hour

**Problem Statement.** A private university has 3,000 students, each taking 30 credit hours per year. Total annual operating cost is Tk 27,00,00,000. Compute the cost per student per year and the cost per student-credit hour.

**Solution.**

\[
\text{Cost per student per year} = \frac{27,00,00,000}{3,000} = \mathbf{Tk\ 9,00,000}
\]

\[
\text{Total student-credit hours} = 3,000 \times 30 = 90,000
\]

\[
\text{Cost per student-credit hour} = \frac{27,00,00,000}{90,000} = \mathbf{Tk\ 3,000}
\]

### Problem 5: Differential Tuition Fees—Engineering versus Business

**Problem Statement.** From the data in the Bangladesh vignette (Section 8.6b):

- Engineering: 36,000 credit hours; allocated cost Tk 18.00 crore.
- Business: 54,000 credit hours; allocated cost Tk 13.50 crore.

The university requires a **25% surplus margin on cost** for each faculty. Compute the tuition fee per credit hour for each faculty.

**Solution.**

\[
\text{Cost per credit hour (Engineering)} = \frac{18,00,00,000}{36,000} = \text{Tk } 5,000
\]

\[
\text{Fee (Engineering)} = 5,000 \times 1.25 = \mathbf{Tk\ 6,250 \text{ per credit hour}}
\]

\[
\text{Cost per credit hour (Business)} = \frac{13,50,00,000}{54,000} = \text{Tk } 2,500
\]

\[
\text{Fee (Business)} = 2,500 \times 1.25 = \mathbf{Tk\ 3,125 \text{ per credit hour}}
\]

The differential fee structure reflects the underlying resource intensity of each programme.

### Problem 6: Hospital Cost per Patient-Day—General Ward versus ICU

**Problem Statement.** Using the data from the Bangladesh vignette (Section 8.6c):

- General ward: 200 beds, 75% occupancy, total operating cost Tk 18.00 crore.
- ICU: 40 beds, 85% occupancy, total operating cost Tk 27.00 crore.

Compute (a) the bed-occupancy rates, (b) the occupied bed-days, and (c) the cost per patient-day for each department.

**Solution.**

**(a) Bed-occupancy rates:** General ward = **75%**; ICU = **85%**.

**(b) Occupied bed-days:**

- General ward: \(200 \times 365 \times 0.75 = \mathbf{54,750}\) bed-days.
- ICU: \(40 \times 365 \times 0.85 = \mathbf{12,410}\) bed-days.

**(c) Cost per patient-day:**

\[
\text{General ward} = \frac{18,00,00,000}{54,750} = \mathbf{Tk\ 3,288}

## 8.9 End-of-Chapter Exam Preparation Pack

### 8.9.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Q1. Define service costing and explain why it is also called operating costing.**

**Model Answer.** Service costing is the cost accounting methodology applied by organisations that provide services rather than manufacture tangible goods. It is called **operating costing** because it ascertains the cost of *operating* a service—running a bus fleet, operating a hospital ward, or operating an academic programme—rather than the cost of producing a physical article. The focus is on the cost of service delivery per composite unit (passenger-km, patient-day, credit hour).

**Q2. Explain the four distinctive characteristics of services that shape service costing.**

**Model Answer.** **Intangibility:** services cannot be seen or touched, so costs attach to service episodes rather than physical inventory. **Simultaneity:** production and consumption occur together, so unused capacity (empty seats, unoccupied beds) represents lost revenue that cannot be inventoried. **Non-inventoriability:** service output cannot be stored, so fixed costs must be absorbed by the services actually sold, raising unit cost when utilisation falls. **Heterogeneity:** services vary across deliveries, requiring average cost units and recognition that individual episodes may differ.

**Q3. Distinguish between a simple cost unit and a composite cost unit, giving two examples of each.**

**Model Answer.** A **simple cost unit** measures a single dimension of output—per kilometre travelled, per meal served. A **composite cost unit** combines two dimensions, typically volume or capacity with distance or time—passenger-kilometre (passengers × km), patient-day (patient × day), ton-kilometre (tons × km), and student-credit hour (students × credits). Composite units are necessary where the cost of service depends jointly on how much is carried or accommodated and how far or how long.

**Q4. Explain the three groups of transport operating costs.**

**Model Answer.** **Standing (fixed) charges** are incurred regardless of distance or load—insurance, road tax, permits, fixed driver salaries, garage rent, and interest. **Maintenance (semi-variable) charges** have both fixed and variable elements—tyres, overhauls, repairs, and servicing. **Running (variable) charges** vary directly with distance and load—fuel, lubricants, tolls, and mileage commissions. This three-part classification supports cost control and fare-setting.

**Q5. What is the bed-occupancy rate, and why does it affect the cost per patient-day?**

**Model Answer.** The bed-occupancy rate is the proportion of available bed-days actually occupied: (actual bed-days ÷ available bed-days) × 100. Because fixed costs—nursing salaries, depreciation, rent—are incurred whether or not beds are occupied, a low occupancy rate spreads the same fixed cost over fewer patient-days, raising the cost per patient-day. Conversely, higher occupancy lowers the fixed cost per patient-day, improving cost efficiency.

**Q6. Why is differential programme costing important in educational institutions?**

**Model Answer.** Programmes differ materially in resource intensity: engineering requires laboratories, workshop equipment, and low student-faculty ratios, while business administration is classroom-based. A uniform institution-wide fee either overcharges low-cost programmes (generating excessive surplus) or under-recovers high-cost programmes (generating deficits). Differential programme costing allocates costs to each faculty on the basis of actual resource consumption and sets tuition fees that reflect underlying cost, ensuring financial sustainability and equity across programmes.

### 8.9.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions with Structural Outlines

**Essay Question 1.** *“The composite cost unit is the defining feature of service costing.” Critically evaluate this statement with reference to transport, education, and healthcare.*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Services are multidimensional; a single-dimensional unit cannot capture service output.
- **Section 1: Transport.** Passenger-km and ton-km combine volume/weight with distance; occupancy and load factors determine output.
- **Section 2: Education.** Student-credit hour combines student numbers with credit volume; cost per student per year as an alternative.
- **Section 3: Healthcare.** Patient-day combines patient numbers with length of stay; occupancy rate as a capacity metric.
- **Section 4: Analytical power.** Composite units enable cost per unit, tariff-setting, capacity utilisation analysis, and cross-sector comparison.
- **Conclusion:** Composite cost units are the analytical foundation of service costing, converting heterogeneous service activity into measurable output.

**Essay Question 2.** *Prepare a Transport Operating Cost Sheet and explain how the three cost groups—standing, maintenance, and running—behave as distance and utilisation change.*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Transport costs are heterogeneous; classification by behaviour supports control and pricing.
- **Section 1: Standing charges.** Fixed within the relevant range; incurred regardless of distance; per-unit decline as distance rises.
- **Section 2: Maintenance charges.** Semi-variable; partly time-driven, partly distance-driven.
- **Section 3: Running charges.** Variable with distance and load; per-unit relatively constant but fuel efficiency varies with speed and load.
- **Section 4: Operating Cost Sheet.** Layout and computation of cost per passenger-km or ton-km; fare-setting with profit on cost or takings.
- **Conclusion:** The three-part classification reveals the cost structure and guides pricing, utilisation, and cost-reduction decisions.

**Essay Question 3.** *Discuss the cost centres and cost units used in hospital costing. How does the cost accountant compute and use the cost per patient-day for general wards and ICUs?*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Hospitals combine direct patient care with complex support services.
- **Section 1: Cost centres.** Inpatient wards, ICU/CCU, OPD, diagnostics, OT, and support centres (pharmacy, laundry, diet, sterilisation).
- **Section 2: Cost units.** Patient-day, outpatient visit, diagnostic test, surgical procedure.
- **Section 3: Cost per patient-day.** Accumulate direct and apportioned support costs; divide by occupied bed-days; distinguish general ward from ICU.
- **Section 4: Tariff-setting and utilisation.** Occupancy rate, cross-subsidisation, margin analysis, and capacity planning.
- **Conclusion:** Accurate cost per patient-day supports rational tariff-setting, resource allocation, and financial sustainability.

**Essay Question 4.** *“Service costing is more about capacity utilisation than about product cost.” Discuss with reference to transport, education, and healthcare.*

**Structural Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Services are perishable; unused capacity is lost revenue.
- **Section 1: Transport.** Load factor (occupancy rate) drives cost per passenger-km; empty seats are unrecoverable costs.
- **Section 2: Education.** Student-faculty ratios and classroom utilisation affect cost per credit hour; under-enrolled programmes raise unit costs.
- **Section 3: Healthcare.** Bed-occupancy rate determines cost per patient-day; empty beds still incur nursing and depreciation costs.
- **Section 4: Managerial implications.** Capacity planning, demand management, pricing to fill capacity, and cost control.
- **Conclusion:** Service costing is inseparable from capacity utilisation analysis; the cost accountant must measure and manage the utilisation of perishable service capacity.

## 8.10 Chapter Summary

**Service costing** (operating costing) is the cost accounting methodology for organisations that provide services rather than tangible goods. Services are **intangible, simultaneously produced and consumed, non-inventoriable, and heterogeneous**, which means that unused capacity is lost revenue and costs must be recovered from services actually delivered. Cost units are **simple** (per km, per meal) or **composite** (passenger-km, ton-km, patient-day, student-credit hour).

**Transport costing** classifies costs into **standing** (fixed), **maintenance** (semi-variable), and **running** (variable) charges. **Passenger-kilometres** and **ton-kilometres**—absolute or commercial—measure output. The Transport Operating Cost Sheet computes cost per composite unit and sets fares or freight charges with a target profit margin.

**Educational institution costing** identifies academic departments, libraries, laboratories, examinations, administration, and hostels as cost centres. **Cost per student** and **cost per student-credit hour** are the key composite units. **Differential programme costing** recognises that engineering and business programmes consume different resources and should bear different fees.

**Hospital costing** identifies inpatient wards, ICU/CCU, OPD, diagnostics, surgical theatres, and support centres as cost centres. **Patient-day**, outpatient visit, diagnostic test, and surgical procedure are the cost units. The **bed-occupancy rate** critically affects cost per patient-day: low occupancy spreads fixed costs over fewer days and raises unit cost. The Hospital Operating Cost Sheet computes cost per patient-day for general wards and ICUs and supports tariff-setting.

## 8.11 Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Service costing (operating costing)** | Cost accounting methodology for organisations providing services rather than tangible goods. |
| **Composite cost unit** | A cost unit combining two dimensions, such as passenger-kilometre or patient-day. |

| **Patient-day (bed-day)** | One patient occupying one bed for one day; the composite unit of inpatient care. |
