## 3.1 Defining Small Business

### 3.1.1 The Definitional Problem

"Small business" is a deceptively simple term. A roadside tea stall, a two-person software consultancy, a 90-worker garment subcontractor, and a plastic moulding factory with Tk 12 crore in fixed assets are all conventionally described as "small"—yet they differ enormously in scale, structure, and strategic orientation. Any useful definition must therefore serve a **purpose**: statistical measurement, policy targeting, or analytical classification. Stokes and Wilson observe that definitions of small business tend to cluster around either **qualitative characteristics** (what the firm *is like*) or **quantitative thresholds** (how large it is). Prof. A. H. M. Habibur Rahman's approach in the Bangladeshi context emphasizes the policy-operational definition, because access to credit, incentives, and institutional support is tied to official classification.

### 3.1.2 Qualitative Metrics

Qualitative definitions focus on the **character** of the enterprise rather than its size. Four criteria are conventionally employed.

**Independent ownership.** The enterprise is owned and controlled by one or a small number of individuals, typically the founder-managers, and is not part of a larger corporate group. Independence implies that strategic decisions are made by owner-managers rather than by distant headquarters.

**Localized operations.** The enterprise serves a geographically proximate market—a neighbourhood, a district, or a region—even if it occasionally reaches beyond. Localization shapes the firm's knowledge base (intimate understanding of local customers) and its competitive horizon (local rather than global rivals).

**Small market share.** The enterprise holds a small share of its relevant market and cannot materially influence market prices or conditions. It is a **price-taker**, not a price-maker.

**Personalized management style.** Management is exercised directly by the owner(s), who combine ownership with operational control. There is no separation of ownership from management, and decision-making is informal, intuitive, and relationship-based rather than procedurally codified.

These criteria are analytically useful but difficult to operationalize for policy. A firm may be independently owned and personally managed yet employ 300 people. Quantitative thresholds are therefore necessary for regulatory and statistical purposes.

### 3.1.3 Quantitative Metrics

Quantitative definitions specify numerical cut-offs. The three standard metrics are:

- **Employee count:** The number of full-time equivalent workers. Employee count is the most common metric internationally because it is relatively easy to verify, though it may understate the economic weight of capital-intensive firms.
- **Capital investment:** The value of fixed assets, typically excluding land and buildings to avoid distortion from real-estate prices. Capital-based definitions are common in developing economies where employment data are weak.
- **Annual sales turnover:** Gross revenue over a fiscal year. Turnover captures market scale but is sensitive to inflation and sectoral pricing differences.

No single metric is universally superior. Bangladesh's official definition employs a **combination**: fixed assets (excluding land and building) as the primary criterion for manufacturing, with employment thresholds as a secondary criterion, and turnover applied in the trading sector.

### 3.1.4 The Official Bangladesh CMSME Classification

Bangladesh classifies enterprises as **Cottage, Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (CMSME)** under the framework anchored in the **National Industrial Policy** and operationalized through Bangladesh Bank's Master Circular on CMSME financing. The thresholds differ by sector because capital intensity and employment structure vary systematically across manufacturing, service, and trading activities.

**Table 3.1: Definition Criteria of CMSMEs in Bangladesh**

| Category | Sector | Fixed Assets (Excluding Land & Building) | Workforce (Persons) | Turnover (Where Applicable) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cottage** | Manufacturing | From Tk 0.5 million to under Tk 1 million | Up to 15 (including family) | Not applicable |
| **Micro** | Manufacturing | From Tk 1 million to under Tk 7.5 million | Up to 25 | Not applicable |
| **Micro** | Service | Less than Tk 1 million | Up to 15 | Not applicable |
| **Micro** | Trading | Up to Tk 1.5 million | Up to 10 | Up to Tk 20 million |
| **Small** | Manufacturing | From Tk 7.5 million to under Tk 150 million | 26 to 120 | Not applicable |
| **Small** | Service | From Tk 1 million to under Tk 20 million | 16 to 50 | Not applicable |
| **Small** | Trading | From Tk 1.5 million to under Tk 20 million | 11 to 30 | Tk 20 million to Tk 200 million |
| **Medium** | Manufacturing | Tk 150 million to Tk 500 million | 121 to 300 | Not specified |

A critical operational feature of the Bangladesh system is that classification is determined by a **combination** of criteria, with the sector determining which criteria are binding. For manufacturing, fixed assets and employment are both applied, with turnover not specified. For trading, all three criteria—assets, employment, and turnover—are applied. The **loan ceilings** attached to each category further operationalize the definition for financial-sector purposes.

### 3.1.5 Economic Significance of Small Business in Bangladesh

The CMSME sector is the **structural backbone** of the Bangladesh economy, though its aggregate productivity contribution lags its numerical dominance.

**Number of enterprises.** According to the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics Economic Census 2024, the country has approximately **11.7 million economic units**, up from 7.82 million in 2013—a 50 per cent increase in a decade. Approximately **99 per cent** of these establishments fall under the CMSME category.

**Composition.** Micro enterprises form the largest segment (6.63 million units, 56.67 per cent of all establishments), followed by cottage enterprises (4.53 million, 38.74 per cent), small enterprises (0.49 million, 4.2 per cent), and medium enterprises (approximately 40,000).

**Employment.** CMSMEs employ approximately **30.63 million people**, compared with 24.50 million a decade earlier. The sector accounts for nearly **85 per cent of total employment within the industrial sector** and over **70 per cent of the country's non-agricultural labour force**.

**GDP contribution.** CMSMEs contribute approximately **25–30 per cent of GDP**, depending on the estimation methodology and year. This is substantially lower than comparable economies—52 per cent in Sri Lanka, 45 per cent in Vietnam, 60 per cent in China, 50 per cent in Japan, and 40 per cent in Pakistan—reflecting the concentration of Bangladeshi CMSMEs in low-productivity, low-value-added activities. CMSME manufacturing contributed 12.35 per cent of GDP in FY2025-26, within striking distance of large-scale manufacturing at 12.63 per cent.

**Manufacturing value added.** According to the Planning Division, CMSMEs contribute **45 per cent of manufacturing value added** and make up **90 per cent of industrial units** and **80 per cent of industrial employment**.

**Sectoral distribution.** Services now account for **90.02 per cent of CMSME activities**, up from 88.46 per cent in 2013, while industry's GDP share has declined from 11.10 per cent to 9.98 per cent. About **63.14 per cent** of CMSMEs operate in rural areas, employing 13.08 million people, while 36.86 per cent are urban, employing 10.77 million.

**Financing gap.** Despite their economic weight, CMSMEs face a financing gap exceeding **$73 billion (approximately Tk 9 lakh crore)**, with **77 per cent of CMSMEs unable to access bank loans**. Less than 17 per cent of total bank loans went to the CMSME sector by December 2025, far below the regulatory target of 25 per cent.

## 3.2 Types and Classifications of Small Business

Small enterprises are conventionally classified by **economic sector**—manufacturing, service, and trading—because resource requirements, regulatory treatment, and growth trajectories differ systematically across these categories.

### 3.2.1 Manufacturing Small Enterprises

Manufacturing enterprises transform raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods. In Bangladesh, manufacturing small enterprises are concentrated in several subsectors.

**Light engineering.** Light engineering encompasses the fabrication, machining, and assembly of metal products, machine parts, and simple machinery. Dholaikhal and Jinjira in Dhaka are historic clusters, with enterprises producing spares for automobiles, railways, and industrial machinery, reportedly manufacturing 5,000 types of spare parts and saving approximately Tk 120 billion in foreign exchange that would otherwise be spent on imports. The sector is characterized by indigenous technological capability, improvisation, and subcontracting linkages to larger firms, but also by informality, weak access to formal finance, and technology gaps.

**Ready-made garment (RMG) subcontractors.** Small garment units perform cutting, stitching, finishing, and embroidery work for larger export-oriented factories. They are deeply embedded in the global apparel value chain but face intense cost pressure, thin margins, and dependence on a small number of buyer firms.

**Plastic goods.** Small plastic moulding and extrusion units produce household items, packaging materials, and industrial components. The sector benefits from low capital intensity relative to other manufacturing and strong domestic demand, but faces environmental scrutiny and raw-material import dependence.

**Agro-processing.** Small agro-processors—rice mills, flour mills, oil expellers, fruit and vegetable processors, dairy processors—add value to agricultural output and create backward linkages to farmers. PRAN-RFL's contract farming model, discussed in Chapter 1, illustrates the scale that agro-processing can achieve, though most units remain small and informal.

**Artisanal crafts.** Handloom textiles, pottery, brassware, jute goods, and other craft enterprises preserve traditional skills while generating rural employment, particularly for women. Aarong's artisan network, discussed in Chapter 1, demonstrates the market potential of organized craft production.

### 3.2.2 Service Small Enterprises

Service enterprises provide intangible value—expertise, convenience, care, or experience—rather than physical goods. Services dominate the CMSME landscape, accounting for over 90 per cent of CMSME activities.

**Information technology.** Small IT firms provide software development, web design, mobile application development, and IT-enabled services. They are typically founder-led, asset-light, and globally oriented, but face talent retention challenges and payment-collection risks.

**Digital marketing.** Social media management, content creation, search-engine optimization, and online advertising services have emerged as a low-capital-entry service category, particularly for young entrepreneurs.

**Logistics and courier.** Last-mile delivery, warehousing, and freight coordination services have expanded with the growth of e-commerce. Pathao's evolution from a logistics service to a super-app, discussed in Chapter 2, illustrates the scalability of logistics ventures.

**Maintenance and repair.** Automotive workshops, electronics repair shops, and equipment-servicing enterprises provide essential support to households and other businesses. They are often informal and owner-operated.

**Healthcare clinics.** Small diagnostic centres, dental chambers, and specialized clinics serve local populations, operating at the intersection of professional service and small-business management.

**Hospitality.** Small restaurants, cafés, guesthouses, and catering services constitute a large and diverse service category, sensitive to disposable income and urban lifestyle shifts.

### 3.2.3 Trading Enterprises

Trading enterprises buy and resell goods without transforming them.

**Wholesaling.** Wholesalers purchase in bulk from manufacturers or importers and distribute to retailers. They provide logistics, storage, and credit functions in the value chain, but face disintermediation pressure from digital platforms.

**Retail stores.** Neighbourhood grocery shops (*mudir dokan*), clothing stores, and specialty retailers form the most visible face of small business in Bangladesh. The country's retail density—approximately 15 outlets per 1,000 people, three to five times the global average—reflects the dominance of small-scale, localized retail【see Chapter 2 discussion】.

**Import-export intermediaries.** Trading houses and indenting agents facilitate cross-border commerce, navigating customs procedures, documentation, and buyer-seller relationships.

**E-commerce drop-shipping.** Digital intermediaries list products online and route orders to suppliers who ship directly to customers, minimizing inventory requirements but exposing the intermediary to quality-control and reputational risks.

## 3.3 Forms of Small Business Ownership

The choice of legal form is among the most consequential decisions a small-business owner makes. It determines **liability exposure, access to capital, tax treatment, governance obligations, and continuity**. Bangladesh offers four principal forms: sole proprietorship, partnership, private limited company, and one-person company.

### 3.3.1 Sole Proprietorship

**Definition and legal standing.** A sole proprietorship is a business owned and operated by a single individual. In Bangladesh, no specific statute governs proprietorships; they are regulated by established practice, common law, and general commercial requirements such as trade licensing and tax registration. The business is **not a separate legal entity** from the owner: in law, the owner and the business are the same person.

**Formation simplicity.** Formation is the simplest of all forms. The proprietor obtains a **Trade License** from the relevant city corporation, municipality, or union parishad, a **Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN)**, and, where applicable, **VAT registration**. There is no central registration body equivalent to the Registrar of Joint Stock Companies and Firms (RJSC), and no minimum capital requirement.

**Management autonomy.** The proprietor exercises complete control over decisions, retains all profits, and bears all losses. There is no separation of ownership and management, and no requirement to consult partners or shareholders.

**Unlimited personal liability.** The defining legal feature—and the defining risk—of sole proprietorship is **unlimited personal liability**. The owner is personally responsible for all business debts and obligations, and personal assets (including property and savings) can be used to satisfy business liabilities. Every default is a personal liability, and upon the proprietor's death, heirs assume responsibility for outstanding obligations.

**Tax treatment.** Sole proprietors are taxed as individuals, at personal income tax rates, without access to the corporate tax deductions and incentives available to incorporated entities.

**Dissolution.** Dissolution is straightforward: the proprietor simply ceases operations, settles obligations, and does not file dissolution papers with a government agency or undergo a winding-up process. However, the absence of perpetual succession means the business does not survive the proprietor's death or incapacity.

### 3.3.2 Partnership

**Definition and legal framework.** A partnership is a voluntary association of two or more persons who agree to carry on a business and share its profits. In Bangladesh, partnerships are governed by the **Partnership Act, 1932**, a legacy of the colonial era. The Act permits a minimum of **two partners** and a maximum of **twenty** in ordinary partnerships, and **ten** in banking partnerships.

**Partnership deed.** The partnership is constituted by a **Partnership Deed**—a written agreement specifying the firm's name and nature, the capital contribution of each partner, the profit-sharing ratio, the rights and duties of partners, and the procedure for admission, retirement, and dissolution. Registration with the RJSC is **optional but strongly recommended**, because an unregistered firm may face evidentiary disadvantages in litigation.

**Rights and duties of partners.** Partners have a right to participate in management, to share profits according to the deed, to access the firm's books, and to be consulted on fundamental decisions. They have a duty to act in good faith, to devote time and effort to the business, and to refrain from competing with the firm.

**Joint and several unlimited liability.** Under **Section 25 of the Partnership Act, 1932**, every partner is liable **jointly with all other partners and also severally** for all acts of the firm carried out while they are a partner. This means that a creditor may recover the full extent of the firm's debts from any single partner, regardless of that partner's individual contribution or fault. Personal assets are at risk, and one partner may be held liable for the negligence or misconduct of another.

**Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) concepts.** Bangladesh does **not currently have a statutory LLP framework**, though proposals for reform have been advanced. An LLP would combine the operational flexibility of a partnership with limited liability for partners—each partner's liability limited to their agreed contribution, with personal assets protected except in cases of fraud or misconduct. Such a structure is particularly suited to professional services firms (lawyers, accountants, consultants, architects) and knowledge-based ventures, where the risks of unlimited joint-and-several liability are disproportionate to the nature of the business. Until an LLP law is enacted, partners seeking limited liability must incorporate as a company.

### 3.3.3 Private Limited Company

**Definition and framework.** A private limited company is an incorporated entity governed by the **Companies Act, 1994**. It is formed by **two or more persons** (minimum two directors and two shareholders), with a maximum of **50 members** (excluding employees), and it **cannot invite the public** to subscribe to its shares.

**Memorandum and Articles of Association.** Incorporation requires two constitutional documents. The **Memorandum of Association (MOA)** defines the company's name, registered office, objects (the scope of its activities), and liability structure. The **Articles of Association (AOA)** govern the company's internal management—share transfer rules, board composition, meeting procedures, and dividend policy. Both are filed with the RJSC at incorporation.

**Separate legal personality.** Upon incorporation, the company becomes a **separate legal entity** distinct from its shareholders and directors. It can own property, enter contracts, sue, and be sued in its own name. This principle—established in *Salomon v. Salomon* and reflected in Bangladeshi company law—is the foundation of limited liability.

**Limited liability shield.** Shareholders' liability is limited to the amount unpaid on their shares (if any). Personal assets are protected from business debts, transforming the risk profile of the venture.

**Perpetual succession.** The company continues to exist independently of its members. The death, insolvency, or exit of a shareholder does not dissolve the company; shares are transferred according to the AOA, and the entity persists.

**Capital raising mechanisms.** A private limited company can raise capital by issuing shares to existing shareholders and approved private investors. It cannot access public markets, but it can accept equity investment from angel investors, venture capital funds, and institutional investors—a critical advantage over proprietorships and partnerships for growth-oriented ventures.

**Compliance and reporting requirements.** Incorporation brings a structured compliance burden: statutory board meetings, annual general meetings, filing of financial statements with the RJSC, audit requirements, and corporate tax obligations distinct from personal income tax. For small firms, these compliance costs can be significant relative to revenue, which is why many remain proprietorships or partnerships.

### 3.3.4 One-Person Company (OPC)

**Definition and legal framework.** The **One-Person Company (OPC)** was introduced by the **Companies (Second Amendment) Act, 2020**, which inserted a new Chapter X-A (Sections 392A–392I) into the Companies Act, 1994. An OPC is a limited-liability company with a **single natural person** as shareholder and director. A natural person may form **only one OPC** at a time.

**Structural benefits for solopreneurs.** The OPC fills a critical gap: it allows a solo founder to obtain **limited liability protection** without the need to bring in a second shareholder, as a private limited company requires. For consultants, freelancers, e-commerce sellers, and solo technopreneurs, this is a material advantage over sole proprietorship, where personal assets remain exposed.

**Nominee requirements.** The defining governance feature of the OPC is the mandatory **nominee**. The memorandum must name a natural person, with that person's written consent, who will become the shareholder upon the sole shareholder's death or incapacity. If the nominee withdraws consent, a replacement must be filed with the RJSC. The nominee mechanism substitutes for the perpetual succession that a multi-member company achieves through share transfer.

**Capital and turnover thresholds.** Under Section 392C, an OPC must maintain **paid-up capital between Tk 2.5 million (25 lakh) and Tk 50 million (5 crore)**, and **annual turnover between Tk 10 million (1 crore) and Tk 500 million (50 crore)** in the preceding year. If both thresholds are exceeded, conversion to a private limited company is required. This creates a defined growth corridor: the OPC is suited to early-stage solo ventures that expect to remain within these bounds, not to high-growth ventures with ambitions of rapid scaling.

**Governance dynamics.** The OPC must hold at least **two board meetings per year** and submit its balance sheet to the RJSC within **180 days** of the financial year-end. These requirements are lighter than those for a private limited company but not negligible. The OPC structure thus offers a **middle path**: limited liability and separate legal personality without the full governance apparatus of a multi-member company, but with capital and turnover constraints that limit its suitability for scale.

## 3.4 Strategic Advantages and Disadvantages of Small Business

### 3.4.1 Distinct Advantages

**Agility and speed of decision-making.** Small firms concentrate decision authority in the owner-manager, eliminating the layers of approval that slow large organizations. This allows rapid response to customer requests, competitive moves, and environmental changes—a decisive advantage in volatile markets.

**Direct customer relationships.** Owner-managers interact directly with customers, acquiring fine-grained knowledge of preferences, pain points, and emerging needs. This **customer intimacy** supports product improvement and personalized service in ways that large firms struggle to replicate.

**High operational flexibility.** Small firms can adjust product mix, pricing, staffing, and processes without the coordination costs that constrain larger organizations. They can pivot quickly when a market shifts.

**Niche market dominance.** By focusing on a narrow segment that is too small to attract large competitors, a small firm can achieve a dominant position within its niche. Specialization compensates for lack of scale.

**Lower initial overhead costs.** Small firms can operate from modest premises, with lean staffing and minimal administrative infrastructure, reducing the capital required to launch and the fixed costs that must be covered before profitability.

**Employee dedication.** In small firms, employees often identify with the enterprise and its owner, contributing flexibility and commitment that formal incentive systems in large firms may fail to elicit.

**Local market responsiveness.** Proximity to customers and community gives small firms contextual knowledge—of local tastes, trust networks, and informal institutions—that distant competitors lack.

### 3.4.2 Inherent Disadvantages and Vulnerabilities

**Undercapitalization.** Small firms typically launch with insufficient capital relative to the requirements of their business model. Under-capitalization constrains inventory, marketing, talent acquisition, and the ability to absorb losses, and is a recurring cause of failure.

**Limited access to formal bank credit.** Banks prefer lending to large corporate borrowers because small loans involve higher administrative costs and are perceived as riskier, while many CMSMEs lack formal records and acceptable collateral. Approximately **77 per cent of CMSMEs cannot access bank loans**, and only 9 per cent of enterprises avoid applying due to high interest rates—suggesting that collateral requirements, not price, are the binding constraint. The **missing middle** problem is acute: SMEs are too large for microfinance and too small for commercial bank lending on standard terms.

**Vulnerability to economic recessions.** Small firms lack the financial reserves, diversified revenue bases, and bargaining power to weather downturns. A demand shock or credit crunch can quickly become existential.

**Lack of specialized management talent.** Owner-managers often possess technical or trade expertise but lack formal training in finance, marketing, or human resource management. The inability to attract and retain specialized talent constrains professionalization and growth.

**Excessive dependence on the owner.** The founder's presence, relationships, and decisions are often central to the business. Owner illness, death, or disengagement can destabilize the enterprise, and the absence of delegated authority limits scalability.

**Inability to achieve economies of scale.** Small firms cannot spread fixed costs over large volumes, cannot negotiate bulk discounts, and cannot invest in the technology and automation that drive productivity. This structural cost disadvantage is compounded in industries with high fixed costs or steep experience curves.

**High business mortality rates.** Small firms fail at high rates, particularly in the early years. Access to finance and technology utilization are statistically significant predictors of survival, and firms lacking both face compounded disadvantage.

**Table 3.2: Advantages vs. Disadvantages of Small Business**

| Dimension | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Decision-making** | Speed, agility, owner autonomy | Owner overload, limited analytical capacity |
| **Customer knowledge** | Direct relationships, intimacy, responsiveness | Narrow market reach, dependence on few customers |
| **Flexibility** | Rapid pivoting, product-mix adjustment | Inability to commit to long-term scale investments |
| **Cost structure** | Low overhead, lean operations | No economies of scale, high unit costs |
| **Finance** | Simplicity of profit retention | Undercapitalization, credit exclusion, collateral barriers |
| **Talent** | Employee dedication, multi-skilling | Inability to attract specialized expertise |
| **Continuity** | — | Owner-dependence, no perpetual succession (unincorporated forms) |
| **Survival** | — | High mortality, vulnerability to shocks |

## 3.5 Bangladesh Contextual Vignette: From Dholaikhal Workshop to Private Limited Company

**The setting.** Dholaikhal, in old Dhaka, is one of Bangladesh's most important light engineering clusters. Its workshops specialize in metal works, lathe operations, and the remanufacturing of automobile and machinery parts. Estimates suggest that Dholaikhal and adjacent areas host approximately **1,200 light engineering units**, part of a national sector of over 40,000 units producing roughly 5,000 types of spare parts. The cluster's entrepreneurs have mastered "knock-down" technology and improvisational engineering, reducing foreign dependence for spare parts and saving substantial foreign exchange.

**The enterprise.** Consider **Rahman Engineering Works**, a hypothetical but representative enterprise. Founded in 2005 by Md. Rafiqul Islam, a skilled lathe operator, the business began as a **sole proprietorship** operating from a rented 1,200-square-foot workshop with three lathe machines and four workers. Rafiqul obtained a trade licence and TIN, but the business remained **informal in critical respects**: no audited financial statements, no registered premises ownership, and no corporate legal identity.

**The growth constraint.** By the early 2020s, Rahman Engineering had 22 workers and annual turnover of approximately Tk 4 crore. Demand from larger manufacturing firms was strong, but Rafiqul could not secure the **institutional supply contracts** that would have stabilized and expanded his order book. The barrier was not capability but **documentation and legal form**: large buyers required a registered company, audited accounts, and evidence of limited liability as conditions of vendor qualification. Simultaneously, Rafiqul needed **bank credit** to purchase CNC machines and expand capacity. Banks demanded collateral—typically land or a building—that he did not possess in the required form, and they were unwilling to lend against the cash flows of an unincorporated business without a formal balance sheet. As one Dholaikhal entrepreneur explained, "Banks want collateral against loans, but we are not capable of providing them as we do not have licences and other documents that the banks require". Most light engineering units beyond a handful of large firms were not getting bank loans because banks required balance sheets, worker lists, licences, and collateral documents.

**The formalization decision.** Rafiqul consulted a corporate lawyer and an accountant. The recommended pathway was **incorporation as a private limited company**. The process required:

1. **Name clearance** from the RJSC.
2. **Drafting of MOA and AOA**, defining the company's objects (light engineering manufacturing and trading) and internal governance.
3. **Appointment of two directors and two shareholders** (Rafiqul and his wife), satisfying the minimum membership requirement.
4. **Registered office address** with documentation.
5. **Filing of incorporation documents** and payment of fees.
6. **Post-incorporation registrations**: new trade licence in the company's name, TIN, VAT registration, and bank account opening.

The cost of incorporation—legal fees, filing fees, and compliance setup—was modest relative to the expected benefit. The more significant change was **behavioural**: incorporation required Rafiqul to maintain **audited financial statements**, segregate personal and business finances, and adopt formal governance (board meetings, statutory filings).

**The outcomes.** Within eighteen months of incorporation, Rahman Engineering Works:

- **Qualified as a vendor** to two large manufacturing firms, securing annual supply contracts worth Tk 1.8 crore—contracts that had been inaccessible to the sole proprietorship.
- **Obtained a CMSME term loan** of Tk 2.5 crore from a commercial bank, secured against the company's assets and supported by audited accounts and supply contracts, enabling the purchase of two CNC machines.
- **Reduced personal risk exposure**: Rafiqul's personal assets were no longer at risk for business debts, and the company's perpetual succession ensured continuity beyond his lifetime.
- **Increased employment** from 22 to 41 workers within two years.

**The residual challenges.** Formalization did not eliminate all constraints. Compliance costs—audit fees, tax filing, VAT administration—absorbed a meaningful share of the thin margins characteristic of light engineering (often 5–10 per cent). Access to credit remained conditional on order-book strength and asset backing, and the company continued to face technology-upgradation and power-supply constraints common to the cluster. Formalization was necessary but not sufficient: it **unlocked access** to institutional markets and finance, but sustained growth required continued investment in capability and market development.

**The transferable lesson.** The Dholaikhal case illustrates a general pattern in Bangladeshi small-business development: **the legal form is not a bureaucratic detail but a strategic infrastructure**. Incorporation changes what the firm can do—whom it can sell to, what it can borrow, and how it can survive its founder. For enterprises with growth ambitions, the transition from sole proprietorship to private limited company is often the pivotal step from survival to scale. The barrier is not the cost of incorporation itself but the **documentation and compliance discipline** that incorporation demands—and the support structures (legal advice, accounting services, and financial literacy) that make that discipline attainable.

## 3.6 Step-by-Step Decision Matrix: Choosing the Right Legal Form

The choice of legal form should be systematic, not habitual. The matrix below guides the decision through four criteria.

**Step 1: Assess capital requirements.** How much capital will the venture require to launch and reach sustainable operation?

- If capital needs are minimal (under Tk 25 lakh) and can be met from personal savings and informal borrowing, proprietorship or OPC may suffice.
- If capital needs are moderate (Tk 25 lakh to Tk 2 crore) and external equity or bank credit will be required, a private limited company is strongly indicated, because lenders and investors require a corporate legal entity.
- If capital needs are substantial and growth is the objective, private limited company is the only viable form.

**Step 2: Assess liability exposure.** What is the risk of business debts exceeding business assets, and what personal assets are at risk?

- If the business is low-risk (service, consulting, low-inventory trading) and the owner's personal assets are modest, unlimited liability may be tolerable.
- If the business involves product liability, significant debt, or substantial contractual exposure, limited liability is essential. This points to private limited company or OPC.

**Step 3: Assess growth ambitions.** Does the owner intend to grow the business beyond a lifestyle enterprise?

- Lifestyle or survival ventures: proprietorship or OPC.
- Growth-oriented ventures seeking institutional customers, external investment, or scale: private limited company.

**Step 4: Assess governance and compliance tolerance.** Is the owner willing and able to maintain statutory records, file returns, and undergo audit?

- If compliance capacity is low and the venture is small, proprietorship minimizes burden.
- If compliance capacity can be built (through professional advice), incorporation provides benefits that justify the burden.

**Table 3.3: Legal Form Decision Matrix**

| Criterion | Sole Proprietorship | Partnership | Private Limited Company | One-Person Company |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Capital requirement** | Very low | Low to moderate | Moderate to high | Low to moderate |
| **Liability exposure** | Unlimited personal | Unlimited, joint and several | Limited to shareholding | Limited to shareholding |
| **Capital access** | Personal savings, informal | Partners' contributions, informal | Bank credit, angel/VC equity | Bank credit (limited), personal |
| **Continuity** | None (dissolves on owner's death) | Uncertain (dissolution on partner exit) | Perpetual succession | Nominee mechanism |
| **Governance burden** | Minimal | Low (deed, optional registration) | High (board, audit, filings) | Moderate (two board meetings, filings) |
| **Tax treatment** | Personal income tax | Firm assessment, partners' shares | Corporate tax | Corporate tax |
| **Best fit** | Micro, lifestyle, survival ventures | Family businesses, professional partnerships | Growth-oriented ventures, institutional suppliers | Solo founders seeking limited liability |

**Decision rule.** If the venture is **growth-oriented, requires bank credit or external equity, or involves significant liability exposure**, incorporate—as a private limited company (if multiple founders or scale ambitions) or an OPC (if solo and within the capital/turnover corridor). If the venture is **small, low-risk, owner-financed, and intended to remain so**, proprietorship or partnership minimizes compliance burden. The decision should be revisited as the venture evolves: many successful firms begin as proprietorships and incorporate when growth demands it, as the Dholaikhal vignette illustrates.

### 3.7.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Question 1: Distinguish between the qualitative and quantitative definitions of small business, giving two examples of each.**

**Model Answer:** Qualitative definitions focus on the character of the enterprise: independent ownership (owned and controlled by the founder-managers), localized operations (serving a proximate market), small market share (price-taker rather than price-maker), and personalized management (owner-managers exercise direct operational control). Quantitative definitions specify numerical thresholds, primarily employee count, capital investment (typically excluding land and buildings), and annual sales turnover. Qualitative definitions capture the *nature* of small business but are difficult to operationalize for policy; quantitative definitions enable statistical measurement and regulatory targeting but may misclassify firms whose character does not match their size.

**Question 2: Explain the CMSME classification thresholds for the manufacturing sector in Bangladesh.**

**Model Answer:** Under Bangladesh's CMSME framework, a **cottage** manufacturing enterprise has fixed assets (excluding land and building) from Tk 0.5 million to under Tk 1 million and up to 15 workers. A **micro** manufacturing enterprise has fixed assets from Tk 1 million to under Tk 7.5 million and up to 25 workers, with no turnover criterion. A **small** manufacturing enterprise has fixed assets from Tk 7.5 million to under Tk 150 million and 26 to 120 workers. A **medium** manufacturing enterprise has fixed assets from Tk 150 million to Tk 500 million and 121 to 300 workers. Firms exceeding the medium threshold are classified as large. The manufacturing classification relies on fixed assets and employment, with turnover not specified.

**Question 3: What is the difference between a partnership and a private limited company in terms of liability?**

**Model Answer:** In a partnership governed by the Partnership Act, 1932, partners have **unlimited, joint and several liability**: each partner is personally liable for the full extent of the firm's debts, regardless of their individual contribution, and one partner may be held liable for the misconduct of another. Personal assets are at risk. In a private limited company incorporated under the Companies Act, 1994, shareholders have **limited liability**: their liability is limited to the amount unpaid on their shares, and personal assets are protected from business debts. The company is a separate legal entity that bears its own obligations.

**Question 4: What is an OPC, and what are its principal structural features?**

**Model Answer:** A One-Person Company (OPC) is a limited-liability company with a single natural person as shareholder and director, introduced by the Companies (Second Amendment) Act, 2020. Its principal features are: (1) a mandatory **nominee** named in the memorandum, with written consent, who becomes the shareholder upon the sole shareholder's death or incapacity; (2) a **paid-up capital** requirement between Tk 2.5 million and Tk 50 million, and **annual turnover** between Tk 10 million and Tk 500 million, with conversion to a private limited company required if both thresholds are exceeded; (3) lighter governance than a private limited company—two board meetings per year and balance-sheet filing within 180 days; and (4) **limited liability protection** for the sole founder, which a sole proprietorship does not provide.

**Question 5: Why do small firms face limited access to formal bank credit in Bangladesh?**

**Model Answer:** Banks prefer lending to large corporate borrowers because small loans involve higher administrative costs per taka lent and are perceived as riskier. Many CMSMEs lack formal records—audited financial statements, worker lists, licences—and acceptable collateral, particularly land or buildings. Approximately 77 per cent of CMSMEs cannot access bank loans, and collateral requirements, rather than interest rates, are the binding constraint for most. The "missing middle" problem arises because SMEs are too large for microfinance and too small for commercial bank lending on standard terms. Legal form matters: unincorporated businesses cannot easily provide the documentation and corporate identity that banks require, which is one reason formalization into a private limited company improves credit access.

**Question 6: What are the principal advantages and disadvantages of small business?**

**Model Answer:** Advantages include agility and speed of decision-making, direct customer relationships and intimacy, high operational flexibility, niche market dominance, lower initial overhead costs, employee dedication, and local market responsiveness. Disadvantages include undercapitalization, limited access to formal bank credit, vulnerability to economic recessions, lack of specialized management talent, excessive dependence on the owner, inability to achieve economies of scale, and high business mortality rates. The disadvantages are structural, not merely managerial: they arise from the small scale and resource constraints that define the category.

### 3.7.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions with Detailed Structural Outlines

**Essay Question 1: "The legal form of a small business is a strategic decision, not a bureaucratic formality." Discuss with reference to the four principal forms available in Bangladesh.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Legal form determines liability, capital access, continuity, governance, and tax treatment. It is strategic because it shapes what the venture can do and what risks the founder bears.
- **Sole proprietorship:** Simplest and cheapest, but unlimited personal liability and no separate legal identity. Suitable for micro and lifestyle ventures, but a barrier to institutional credit and contracts.
- **Partnership:** Pooled capital and complementary skills, but joint and several unlimited liability. The absence of an LLP framework makes partnerships risky for professional and knowledge-based ventures.
- **Private limited company:** Separate legal personality, limited liability, perpetual succession, and access to bank credit and equity investment. The compliance burden is the price of these advantages.
- **One-Person Company:** A middle path—limited liability for solo founders without a second shareholder—but with capital and turnover corridors that limit its use for high-growth ventures.
- **Synthesis:** The choice should be driven by capital requirements, liability exposure, growth ambitions, and compliance capacity. Legal form is not static; firms should revisit it as they evolve.
- **Conclusion:** Treating legal form as a strategic decision aligns the venture's legal infrastructure with its growth trajectory and risk profile.

**Essay Question 2: "Small businesses in Bangladesh are numerically dominant but economically underproductive." Critically evaluate this statement with reference to CMSME statistics and structural constraints.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** CMSMEs constitute approximately 99 per cent of enterprises and 85 per cent of industrial employment, but contribute only 25–30 per cent of GDP—lower than regional comparators.
- **The numerical dominance:** 11.7 million economic units, 30.63 million workers, 90 per cent of CMSME activity in services. The sector is the backbone of employment.
- **The productivity gap:** GDP contribution of 25–30 per cent versus 52 per cent in Sri Lanka, 45 per cent in Vietnam, 60 per cent in China. Manufacturing value added is 45 per cent, but much CMSME activity is in low-value-added services and informal household enterprise.
- **Structural constraints:** Undercapitalization, credit exclusion (77 per cent cannot access bank loans), collateral barriers, informality, technology gaps, and thin margins.
- **The formalization–productivity link:** Legal form and documentation affect access to institutional markets and finance. The Dholaikhal case shows that incorporation can unlock contracts and credit, but compliance costs and capability gaps persist.
- **Policy implications:** Credit guarantee schemes, cluster development, formalization incentives, and management training are necessary but must address the structural, not merely symptomatic, causes of low productivity.
- **Conclusion:** Numerical dominance without productivity convergence is a development trap. The policy challenge is to move CMSMEs from survival to scale.

**Essay Question 3: Compare and contrast the sole proprietorship and the private limited company as vehicles for small-business growth in Bangladesh.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** The two forms sit at opposite ends of the liability–compliance spectrum.
- **Formation and cost:** Proprietorship is simplest and cheapest (trade licence, TIN, VAT; no RJSC registration). Private limited requires name clearance, MOA/AOA, RJSC filing, and post-incorporation registrations.
- **Liability:** Proprietorship imposes unlimited personal liability; private limited provides limited liability and separate legal personality.
- **Capital access:** Proprietorship relies on personal savings and informal borrowing; private limited can access bank credit on the strength of audited accounts and can issue shares to private investors.
- **Continuity:** Proprietorship dissolves on the owner's death; private limited has perpetual succession.
- **Compliance:** Proprietorship has minimal governance burden; private limited requires board meetings, audits, and statutory filings.
- **Growth trajectory:** Proprietorship is suited to micro and lifestyle ventures; private limited is the gateway to institutional supply contracts, bank credit, and equity investment.
- **Synthesis:** The transition from proprietorship to private limited is a common growth pathway, but it should be timed to the venture's needs—incorporating too early imposes unnecessary compliance costs; incorporating too late forecloses growth opportunities.
- **Conclusion:** The choice is contingent on the venture's capital intensity, risk profile, and growth ambitions, not on an abstract preference for one form.

**Essay Question 4: "The absence of a Limited Liability Partnership framework is a significant institutional gap in Bangladesh's business law." Discuss.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Bangladesh offers proprietorship, partnership, private limited, and OPC—but not LLP. The gap matters for professional and knowledge-based ventures.
- **The Partnership Act, 1932:** Colonial-era legislation designed for small-scale, family-run, product-based businesses. Its unlimited joint-and-several liability is ill-suited to modern professional services.
- **The private limited alternative:** Limited liability is available, but the compliance apparatus—boards, statutory meetings, audits, reporting—is disproportionately burdensome for small professional firms.
- **The LLP solution:** An LLP would combine operational flexibility with limited liability, protecting personal assets while avoiding the full corporate compliance burden. It is the standard structure for law firms, accounting firms, consultancies, and IT ventures in many jurisdictions.
- **Consequences of the gap:** Professionals either accept unlimited liability (partnership) or bear disproportionate compliance costs (private limited). This constrains the growth of knowledge-based services.
- **Prospects for reform:** Proposals have been advanced; a well-designed LLP law would align Bangladesh with international standards and support the transition to a knowledge economy.
- **Conclusion:** The LLP gap is not merely technical; it shapes the risk calculus and growth trajectory of an increasingly important segment of the small-business sector.

### 3.7.4 Practical Ownership Decision Scenarios with Model Solutions

**Scenario 1: The Solo Consultant**

*Situation:* Fatima is a freelance digital-marketing consultant with five years of experience. She works alone, serves 8–10 clients, earns approximately Tk 60 lakh annually, and expects modest growth (perhaps Tk 1 crore within three years). She has personal savings of Tk 15 lakh and no significant business debts. Her primary concern is protecting her family's apartment from any business liability.

*Model Solution:* Fatima's profile fits the **One-Person Company (OPC)**. She requires limited liability (to protect the apartment) but does not need a second shareholder, and her turnover (Tk 60 lakh, projected Tk 1 crore) and capital (Tk 15 lakh) fall within the OPC thresholds (paid-up capital Tk 25 lakh to Tk 5 crore; turnover Tk 1 crore to Tk 50 crore). The OPC provides limited liability with lighter governance than a private limited company. However, her paid-up capital must be at least Tk 25 lakh—she would need to inject additional capital or retain profits to meet the minimum. If she cannot meet the capital threshold, a **private limited company** with a family member as co-shareholder is the alternative. A sole proprietorship would expose the apartment to unlimited liability and should be rejected.

**Scenario 2: The Growing Manufacturing Partnership**

*Situation:* Karim and Salam jointly own a small plastic-moulding workshop in Old Dhaka. They operate as a partnership under a deed, with equal capital contributions and profit sharing. Annual turnover is Tk 3 crore, and they employ 30 workers. They want to bid for supply contracts with a large beverage company, but the buyer requires a registered company with audited accounts and limited liability. They also need a bank loan of Tk 1.5 crore to buy a new moulding machine.

*Model Solution:* Karim and Salam should **incorporate as a private limited company**. Their existing partnership structure cannot satisfy the buyer's vendor-qualification requirements (registered company, audited accounts) or the bank's lending requirements (corporate borrower with a balance sheet). Incorporation will provide limited liability, separate legal personality, and the documentation infrastructure required for institutional contracts and credit. They will need to draft MOA and AOA, appoint directors and shareholders (the two of them, plus possibly family members to meet the two-shareholder minimum—they already satisfy this with two partners), and establish formal accounting and governance. The compliance burden is manageable and justified by the access it unlocks. The partnership should be dissolved and its assets transferred to the new company, with professional advice on tax and stamp-duty implications.

## 3.8 Chapter Summary

This chapter has introduced the definition, classification, legal forms, and strategic position of small business in Bangladesh.

**Defining small business** requires both qualitative criteria (independent ownership, localized operations, small market share, personalized management) and quantitative thresholds (employee count, capital investment, turnover). Bangladesh's **CMSME classification** operationalizes these thresholds across manufacturing, service, and trading sectors, with fixed-asset and employment criteria determining category membership.

The **economic significance** of CMSMEs is paradoxical: they constitute approximately 99 per cent of enterprises and 85 per cent of industrial employment, yet contribute only 25–30 per cent of GDP—a productivity gap relative to regional comparators that reflects structural constraints including undercapitalization, credit exclusion, informality, and low value-added activity.

**Types of small business**—manufacturing (light engineering, garment subcontracting, plastics, agro-processing, crafts), service (IT, digital marketing, logistics, repair, healthcare, hospitality), and trading (wholesale, retail, import-export, e-commerce)—differ in resource requirements, regulatory treatment, and growth trajectories.

**Legal forms of ownership**—sole proprietorship, partnership, private limited company, and one-person company—differ principally in liability, capital access, continuity, and governance burden. The sole proprietorship is simplest but imposes unlimited personal liability. The partnership pools capital but imposes joint and several unlimited liability, and Bangladesh lacks an LLP framework that would provide limited liability with partnership flexibility. The private limited company provides limited liability, perpetual succession, and access to institutional credit and equity, at the cost of compliance burden. The one-person company offers limited liability to solo founders within defined capital and turnover thresholds, with a mandatory nominee mechanism.

**Strategic advantages** of small business—agility, customer intimacy, flexibility, niche dominance, low overhead, employee dedication, local responsiveness—are counterbalanced by **inherent vulnerabilities**: undercapitalization, credit exclusion, recession vulnerability, management talent gaps, owner-dependence, inability to achieve scale economies, and high mortality. The **legal-form decision matrix** guides entrepreneurs through the trade-offs, and the **Dholaikhal vignette** illustrates how formalization into a private limited company can unlock institutional contracts and bank credit for a growing light engineering enterprise.

## 3.9 Key Terms Glossary

1. **Small business:** An independently owned and managed enterprise that is small relative to its market, typically defined by quantitative thresholds (employees, assets, turnover) and qualitative characteristics (personalized management, local operations).
2. **CMSME:** Cottage, Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprise—the official classification framework in Bangladesh, with sector-specific thresholds for fixed assets, employment, and turnover.
3. **Fixed assets (excluding land and building):** The value of plant, machinery, equipment, and other productive assets used in the CMSME classification, excluding real estate to avoid distortion from property prices.
4. **Sole proprietorship:** A business owned and operated by a single individual, with no separate legal personality and unlimited personal liability.
5. **Partnership:** A voluntary association of two or more persons carrying on a business for profit, governed in Bangladesh by the Partnership Act, 1932.
6. **Joint and several liability:** A legal doctrine under which each partner is liable for the full extent of the firm's debts, and the creditor may recover from any one partner.
7. **Private limited company:** An incorporated entity under the Companies Act, 1994, with 2–50 members, limited liability, separate legal personality, and perpetual succession.
8. **Memorandum of Association (MOA):** The constitutional document that defines a company's name, registered office, objects, and liability structure.
9. **Articles of Association (AOA):** The document governing a company's internal management, including share transfer, board composition, and meeting procedures.
10. **Separate legal personality:** The principle that an incorporated company is a legal entity distinct from its shareholders and directors, capable of owning property, contracting, and suing in its own name.
11. **One-Person Company (OPC):** A limited-liability company with a single natural person as shareholder and director, introduced in Bangladesh by the Companies (Second Amendment) Act, 2020, with a mandatory nominee and defined capital/turnover thresholds.
12. **Nominee (OPC):** A natural person named in the OPC's memorandum, with written consent, who becomes the shareholder upon the sole shareholder's death or incapacity.
