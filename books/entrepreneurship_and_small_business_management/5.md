## 5.1 Planning Financial Needs and Capital Usage

### 5.1.1 Forecasting Startup Capital Requirements

The first financing question is not "where will the money come from?" but "how much money is needed, for what, and when?" A venture that raises too little capital is fragile; a venture that raises too much dilutes ownership unnecessarily and may deploy capital without discipline. Forecasting capital requirements therefore requires disaggregating the total into two distinct categories.

**Capital expenditure (CapEx)** is the investment in long-lived assets that the venture will use over multiple years: land, buildings, plant and machinery, vehicles, furniture, and technology infrastructure. CapEx is **lumpy**—it is incurred upfront, in large amounts, before the venture generates any revenue. It is also **illiquid**: the assets purchased cannot easily be converted back into cash without significant loss. CapEx is financed with **long-term capital**—equity or long-term debt—because the assets it funds will generate returns over an extended period. Financing long-lived assets with short-term borrowing creates a **maturity mismatch** that can trigger insolvency when the short-term loan matures before the asset has generated sufficient cash.

**Operating working capital reserve** is the cash required to fund day-to-day operations until the venture's cash inflows are sufficient to cover its cash outflows. It includes the cost of raw materials and inventory, payroll, rent, utilities, marketing, and other operating expenses incurred before revenue stabilizes. Working capital is **recurring** rather than lumpy: it is consumed continuously and replenished by sales revenue. It is financed with **short-term capital**—overdrafts, cash credit, trade credit, or a working capital term loan—because it is recovered within the operating cycle.

The distinction is not academic. A venture that budgets Tk 1 crore for machinery but nothing for the six months of salaries, rent, and supplier payments required before the machinery generates saleable output is **structurally undercapitalized**. The machinery is not the problem; the absence of a working capital reserve is. This is the single most common financial planning error among small ventures, and it is fatal because the cash trough arrives precisely when the venture is most vulnerable—before it has established customer relationships, supplier credibility, or a revenue track record.

**A practical forecasting sequence.** The entrepreneur should: (1) list every CapEx item with its quotation or estimate; (2) estimate monthly operating expenses at launch and during ramp-up; (3) estimate the month in which cash inflows will first cover monthly outflows; (4) multiply monthly outflows by the number of months until that crossover point, and add a contingency buffer (typically 10–20 per cent). The sum of CapEx and the working capital reserve is the **total project cost**. The financing plan must cover the total project cost, not merely the CapEx.

### 5.1.2 Capital Usage in Small Business: Fixed vs. Circulating Capital

The assets that capital funds are conventionally divided into **fixed capital** and **circulating (working) capital**.

**Fixed capital** is invested in long-term assets—premises, machinery, equipment, vehicles, and leasehold improvements—that are used repeatedly in production or service delivery and are not consumed within a single operating cycle. Fixed capital determines the venture's **capacity**: how much it can produce, store, or deliver. It is relatively illiquid and is depreciated over its useful life. Because fixed capital is long-lived, it should be financed with long-term funds.

**Circulating capital** (working capital) is invested in assets that circulate through the operating cycle: raw materials and inventory, work-in-progress, finished goods, accounts receivable, and the cash held for day-to-day transactions. These assets are continuously converted from cash into inventory, into receivables, and back into cash. Working capital determines the venture's **liquidity**: whether it can pay its bills as they fall due. It is short-lived and should be financed with short-term funds.

**The matching principle.** The foundational rule of small-business financing is that the **maturity of finance should match the maturity of the asset financed**. Long-term assets should be financed with long-term debt or equity; short-term assets should be financed with short-term debt, trade credit, or a revolving facility. Violating this principle—financing a factory with a three-month overdraft, or financing inventory with a ten-year term loan—creates either refinancing risk or unnecessary interest cost.

### 5.1.3 Equity Financing vs. Debt Financing: The Trade-Offs

Every external financing decision involves a choice between **equity** (selling a share of ownership) and **debt** (borrowing money that must be repaid with interest). The two are not interchangeable; they impose different costs, risks, and control consequences.

**Equity financing** involves raising money in exchange for an ownership stake. The investor becomes a shareholder, shares in the venture's profits (through dividends) and in any capital appreciation, and typically acquires governance rights—a vote on major decisions, board representation, or veto rights over specified actions.

**Debt financing** involves borrowing money that must be repaid according to a fixed schedule, with interest. The lender does not acquire ownership, does not share in profits beyond the agreed interest, and does not acquire governance rights (though loan covenants may restrict certain actions). Debt creates a **fixed legal obligation**: interest and principal must be paid whether or not the venture is profitable.

**Table 5.1: Debt Financing vs. Equity Financing for Small Firms**

| Dimension | Debt Financing | Equity Financing |
|---|---|---|
| **Ownership dilution** | None—lender acquires no ownership | Dilutes founders' ownership percentage |
| **Repayment obligation** | Fixed schedule; mandatory regardless of profitability | Discretionary dividends; no mandatory repayment |
| **Cash-flow burden** | Interest and principal consume cash during lean periods | No cash drain unless dividends are declared |
| **Financial risk** | High—default can trigger insolvency and asset loss | Low—no default risk from the financing itself |
| **Tax treatment** | Interest is tax-deductible (tax shield) | Dividends are paid from after-tax profit; not tax-deductible |
| **Control** | Retained by founders, subject to covenants | Shared with investors; board seats and veto rights |
| **Cost of capital** | Interest rate, typically 12–16% in Bangladesh (lower for concessional schemes) | Expected return of 25–40%+ for VC; angel returns vary |
| **Accessibility for small firms** | Requires collateral, track record, CIB clearance | Requires scalable model, team credibility, and growth potential |
| **Suitability** | Asset-backed ventures with predictable cash flow | High-growth, asset-light, scalable ventures |

The trade-off is rarely resolved by choosing one extreme. Most small ventures use a **blended capital structure**: founders' equity, retained earnings, bank debt for working capital and equipment, and—for high-growth ventures—external equity at a later stage. The optimal mix depends on the venture's cash-flow predictability, asset base, growth ambitions, and the founder's tolerance for dilution and financial risk.

## 5.2 Sources of Financing for Small Firms

The financing landscape for small firms is best understood as a **spectrum** running from internal and informal sources (which are fast, flexible, and non-dilutive but limited in scale) to external and formal sources (which are larger but slower, more demanding, and often require collateral or equity).

### 5.2.1 Internal and Informal Sources

**Entrepreneurial bootstrapping.** Bootstrapping is the practice of launching and growing a venture with minimal external capital, using creative tactics to conserve cash and leverage resources the entrepreneur does not own. Common tactics include:

- **Equipment leasing** rather than purchasing, converting a large CapEx outlay into a manageable monthly operating expense.
- **Shared or co-working office space**, avoiding the cost of dedicated premises.
- **Lean inventory**—holding minimal stock and ordering to demand—reduces working capital tied up in goods.
- **Sweat equity**: offering ownership stakes to co-founders, early employees, or advisers in lieu of cash compensation.
- **Customer prepayment** or deposits, which converts customers into a source of working capital.
- **Barter arrangements**, exchanging products or services for needed inputs.

Bootstrapping preserves ownership and avoids debt service, but it constrains growth speed and may limit the venture's ability to compete with better-funded rivals.

**Personal savings and retained earnings.** Personal savings are the most common source of startup capital for small ventures, and retained earnings—profits reinvested in the business rather than distributed—are the most important source of growth capital for established small firms. Both are **internal** sources: they involve no dilution, no interest, and no external approval. Their limitation is scale: savings are finite, and retained earnings accumulate slowly. The **pecking order** observed in small-business finance—internal funds first, debt second, external equity last—reflects both the cost and the control advantages of internal capital.

**Financing from family and friends.** Family and friends provide **informal capital** that is typically faster and more flexible than bank finance, and often available without collateral or a formal credit history. The financing may take the form of an informal loan (with or without interest), a partnership stake, or a gift. The advantages are speed and accessibility; the risks are **relationship strain**. When a venture fails—as many do—family lenders may lose their savings and the relationships may be irreparably damaged. Best practice is to document the arrangement in writing, specify whether it is debt or equity, state the repayment terms, and ensure that family investors understand the risk of total loss.

**Trade credit from suppliers.** Trade credit is the practice of purchasing goods or services on **open account** terms—receiving the goods now and paying later. It is, in effect, a short-term loan from the supplier, and it is one of the largest sources of working capital for small firms. Standard terms are typically **30, 60, 90, or 120 days**, as agreed between buyer and supplier. In Bangladesh's export-oriented garment sector, for example, suppliers and buyers operate on extended credit terms, with back-to-back arrangements and deferred payment practices shaping cash flow across the value chain. Trade credit is attractive because it is **non-interest-bearing** (though suppliers may embed a financing cost in the price or offer a discount for early payment) and requires no collateral. Its limitation is dependence on supplier goodwill: a supplier facing its own liquidity pressure may tighten terms abruptly, creating a cash-flow shock for the buyer.

### 5.2.2 External and Formal Sources

**Commercial bank loans.** Banks are the principal source of external debt finance for small firms in Bangladesh. Two broad categories of facility are relevant.

**Short-term working capital facilities** include **overdrafts** and **cash credit (CC) accounts**. An overdraft allows the borrower to draw on a current account up to an approved limit; interest is charged on the amount actually drawn, and the facility is typically reviewed annually. A cash credit account operates similarly, secured by hypothecation of inventory and receivables. These facilities are designed to finance the **working capital cycle**—the gap between paying for inputs and collecting payment from customers—and are repaid as receivables are collected.

**Term loans** are used to finance **capital assets**—machinery, vehicles, premises, and expansion projects. They are repaid over a defined period (typically three to seven years, sometimes longer for large projects) through equal instalments, and they are secured by the asset financed and/or other collateral. Term loans are the appropriate instrument for CapEx because their maturity matches the asset's useful life.

**Interest rate landscape (2026).** Bangladesh Bank withdrew the SMART-based lending cap in May 2024, shifting pricing toward banker-customer relationships. Mainstream business lending in Bangladesh in 2026 generally clusters between **12.5 per cent and 16 per cent per annum**, with SME term and working capital lending typically around **14–14.5 per cent**. Publicly posted rates for small industry term loans at major banks include: Prime Bank at 12–15 per cent, BRAC Bank at 12–16 per cent, and a range of other banks from 8 per cent (state-owned and foreign banks) to 16 per cent. Special concessional windows—startup schemes, women entrepreneur schemes, and government refinance windows—can price as low as **4–8 per cent**.

**Microfinance institutions (MFIs).** Bangladesh has one of the world's most extensive microfinance sectors, with 693 MRA-licensed MFIs operating through 27,113 branches, serving 43.98 million members and 33.68 million active borrowers. The four largest institutions—**Grameen Bank, BRAC, ASA, and TMSS**—dominate the sector. BRAC alone reports 11.4 million people accessing microfinance services in 2025, with 8 million using $6.6 billion in microloans. ASA ranks third with 6.77 million active members and 5.43 million outstanding borrowers.

A critical distinction within microfinance is between **microcredit** and **microenterprise (ME) loans**. Microcredit is the smaller, subsistence-style loan associated with the sector's anti-poverty origins. ME loans are larger and business-focused, designed for entrepreneurs who already have a small business and need working capital or expansion funds. In FY2024–25, ME loans made up about **40 per cent** of combined ME and microcredit disbursement among MFIs, and that share has been rising. The top 20 MFIs served 4.14 million ME borrowers as of June 2025, with ME loan outstanding of BDT 570.96 billion. **BRAC and ASA together hold more than half of all microenterprise loan outstanding** among the top 20 MFIs.

Group-lending—the classic Grameen model in which borrowers form small groups that guarantee each other's repayment—remains important for microcredit, but ME loans are increasingly individual, reflecting the larger amounts and business-specific underwriting they require. PKSF acts as the main wholesale funder for smaller MFIs through its Agrosor programme.

**Non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs) and leasing.** NBFIs provide **machinery and vehicle leasing**, which allows a small firm to acquire productive assets without a large upfront cash outlay. The leasing company retains legal ownership of the asset until the lease is fully paid, and the lessee pays a monthly rental. Leasing is particularly useful for ventures that lack the collateral or track record for a term loan, because the leased asset itself serves as security. IDLC Finance, one of Bangladesh's leading NBFIs, pioneered supply chain finance in the country in 1999, and factoring and bill discounting are widely used forms of receivables financing for MSMEs.

**Venture capital (VC) and angel investors.** Venture capital and angel investment are **equity** sources for high-growth ventures. They are not suitable for every small business: they require a scalable business model, a large addressable market, a credible founding team, and a plausible exit route (acquisition or public listing) within five to seven years.

**Angel investors** are wealthy individuals who invest their own money in early-stage ventures, typically at the **seed** stage. In Bangladesh, angel investment amounts typically range from **BDT 10 lakh to BDT 1 crore** (roughly $8,000–$80,000), and there are at least five active angel networks, including Bangladesh Angels (BAN) and BD Angels. **Venture capital firms** invest institutional funds at later stages—**Series A** and beyond—and typically take larger equity stakes and board seats. Bangladesh has 15+ VC/PE firms, and average seed rounds are BDT 50 lakh–2 crore, with Series A rounds at BDT 5–20 crore.

The Bangladesh startup funding ecosystem remains small and externally dependent. In the first half of 2026, startups raised only **$6 million**—a 95 per cent decline—and **none of it was domestic**; 92 per cent of startup capital has historically come from abroad. Government-backed **Startup Bangladesh Limited** has committed over $100 million, and 39 commercial banks have pooled capital for a **$35 million Bangladesh Startup Investment Company (BSIC)**, whose inaugural **Onkur** fund is expected to make its first three investments in Q4 2026. These initiatives are nascent; for most small firms, VC and angel capital remain inaccessible, and bank debt or internal funds are the realistic options.

**Government concessionary schemes.** The Government of Bangladesh and Bangladesh Bank operate several schemes to reduce the cost and improve the accessibility of SME finance.

- **Bangladesh Bank refinance schemes** provide funds to banks at concessional rates for on-lending to priority sectors. Under a 2026 arrangement, BRAC Bank signed deals with Bangladesh Bank to expand MSME financing at **7 per cent interest**, with micro enterprises eligible for up to **Tk 1 crore** and small and medium enterprises up to **Tk 5 crore**. Bangladesh Bank has also operated a **Tk 500 crore startup refinance fund**, offering loans of up to **Tk 1 crore** at **4 per cent interest** against personal guarantee or academic certificate, with a grace period of up to one year and phased disbursement.
- **Credit guarantee schemes** enable banks to lend without traditional collateral. Bangladesh Bank launched a **Tk 2,000 crore credit guarantee scheme** allowing banks to issue up to **Tk 50 lakh in collateral-free loans** to cottage, micro, and small enterprises, with guarantee fees of 1 per cent in the first year and reduced rates thereafter for banks with low non-performing loans.
- **SME Foundation credit wholesaling.** The SME Foundation operates a **Credit Wholesaling Programme** under a government **Tk 300 crore revolving fund**, channeling low-cost funds through partner banks. Under the SME Foundation–Prime Bank agreement, loans range from **Tk 1 lakh to Tk 25 lakh**, with the interest rate capped at **8 per cent per annum**, a repayment window of up to four years including a six-month grace period, and **no formal collateral required for loans up to Tk 10 lakh**. The programme prioritizes women entrepreneurs, cluster-based enterprises, young ICT innovators, import-substituting manufacturers, and enterprises in climate-vulnerable regions.

**Table 5.2: Informal Sources vs. Commercial Banks vs. Venture Capital**

| Dimension | Informal Sources (Family, Friends, Trade Credit) | Commercial Banks | Venture Capital / Angel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | Low or zero explicit interest (trade credit embeds cost in price) | 12–16% mainstream; 7–8% concessional; 4% startup schemes | High: 25–40%+ expected return; significant equity dilution |
| **Speed** | Fast—days to weeks | Slow—weeks to months (CIB, appraisal, documentation) | Very slow—months of due diligence |
| **Control impact** | Minimal, unless equity stake is given | None, subject to loan covenants | Significant: board seats, veto rights, reporting obligations |
| **Collateral requirement** | None | Yes—land, building, FDR, hypothecation; collateral-free up to Tk 50 lakh under CGS, Tk 10 lakh under SME Foundation | None, but equity stake is the "collateral" |
| **Scale** | Limited | Moderate to large | Large, but only for scalable ventures |
| **Suitability** | Early-stage, micro, working capital bridge | Asset-backed, cash-generating SMEs | High-growth, scalable, exit-oriented ventures |

## 5.3 Working Capital Management in Small Firms

Working capital is the lifeblood of a small enterprise. A venture can survive temporary unprofitability, but it cannot survive running out of cash. Working capital management is therefore not a back-office function; it is a core entrepreneurial discipline.

### 5.3.1 The Working Capital Cycle and the Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)

The **working capital cycle** (or operating cycle) is the sequence of activities through which a venture converts cash into inventory, inventory into sales, sales into receivables, and receivables back into cash. The **Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)** measures, in days, how long the venture's cash is tied up in this cycle.

\[
\text{CCC} = \text{Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)} + \text{Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)} - \text{Days Payables Outstanding (DPO)}
\]

**Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)** is the average number of days it takes to collect payment from customers after a sale:
\[
\text{DSO} = \frac{\text{Accounts Receivable}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 365
\]

**Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)** is the average number of days inventory is held before being sold:
\[
\text{DIO} = \frac{\text{Inventory}}{\text{Cost of Goods Sold}} \times 365
\]

**Days Payables Outstanding (DPO)** is the average number of days the venture takes to pay its suppliers:
\[
\text{DPO} = \frac{\text{Accounts Payable}}{\text{Cost of Goods Sold}} \times 365
\]

**Interpretation.** A **shorter CCC** is better: it means cash is tied up for fewer days, reducing the working capital the venture must finance. A **longer CCC** means the venture must fund a larger gap between cash outflows (to suppliers, employees) and cash inflows (from customers), increasing its financing requirement. A **negative CCC**—where the venture collects from customers before it pays suppliers—is a powerful source of internal financing, characteristic of some retail and subscription models.

**Levers for shortening the CCC:** reduce DSO (faster collection, stricter credit terms, prompt-payment discounts), reduce DIO (leaner inventory, better demand forecasting), and extend DPO (negotiate longer payment terms with suppliers, without damaging relationships).

### 5.3.2 Cash Management and the Danger of "Growing Broke"

**Overtrading**—also called "growing broke"—occurs when a venture expands its sales faster than its working capital can support. Each additional sale requires the venture to purchase inventory and extend credit before it collects cash. If sales grow rapidly, the working capital requirement grows faster than retained profits can fund, and the venture runs out of cash despite being profitable and growing. This is a particularly insidious failure mode because the symptoms—rising sales, rising profits—appear positive while the underlying liquidity position deteriorates.

**The cash budget** is the primary defence against overtrading. A monthly cash budget projects **cash receipts** (from cash sales, collections of receivables, and financing inflows) and **cash payments** (for purchases, payroll, rent, loan repayments, and taxes), producing a **net cash flow** and an **ending cash balance** for each month. The minimum ending balance—the **cash trough**—identifies the point at which external financing is required. The cash budget should be prepared before the venture launches and updated monthly thereafter, because the timing of cash flows is rarely as assumed.

### 5.3.3 Accounts Receivable Control

Accounts receivable arise when the venture sells on credit. Credit is a competitive necessity—many customers expect it—but uncontrolled credit is a cash trap.

**Credit policy formulation.** The venture must decide: **to whom** credit will be extended (credit standards), **how much** credit will be extended (credit limits), **for how long** (credit terms), and **what discount** will be offered for early payment (prompt-payment discount). A typical policy might offer 30-day terms with a 2 per cent discount for payment within 10 days. The discount is expensive—2 per cent for 20 days' acceleration implies an annualized cost of over 36 per cent—but it may be cheaper than financing the receivables gap.

**Aging schedules.** An aging schedule classifies receivables by how long they have been outstanding: current, 1–30 days, 31–60 days, 61–90 days, and over 90 days. A deteriorating aging profile—a growing proportion of receivables in the older buckets—is an early warning of collection problems and should trigger tightened credit or more aggressive collection.

**Collection procedures.** A clear collection procedure—reminder letters, phone calls, escalation to senior management, suspension of further credit, and ultimately legal action—signals to customers that payment is expected and reduces the risk of habitual late payment.

### 5.3.4 Inventory Management

Inventory—raw materials, work-in-progress, and finished goods—is necessary to satisfy customer demand and smooth production, but it consumes cash and incurs holding costs (storage, insurance, obsolescence, and the opportunity cost of tied-up capital). The entrepreneurial challenge is to balance the cost of holding inventory against the cost of **stock-outs** (lost sales and damaged customer relationships).

**Economic Order Quantity (EOQ).** EOQ identifies the order quantity that minimizes the total of ordering costs and holding costs:

\[
\text{EOQ} = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}}
\]

where **D** = annual demand in units, **S** = ordering cost per order, and **H** = holding cost per unit per year.

**Reorder Level (ROL).** ROL is the inventory level at which a new order should be placed, allowing for the lead time between ordering and delivery:

\[
\text{ROL} = \text{Average daily demand} \times \text{Lead time in days} + \text{Safety stock}
\]

Safety stock is the buffer held against unexpected demand spikes or supply delays. Higher safety stock reduces stock-out risk but increases holding cost; the optimal level balances the two.

**The overstocking trap.** Small firms often over-order to obtain quantity discounts or to guard against supply disruption, tying up cash that could be deployed elsewhere. In perishable or fashion-sensitive categories, overstocking can become worthless inventory. The discipline of EOQ and ROL, combined with regular review of slow-moving stock, keeps inventory aligned with demand.

## 5.4 Request for Institutional Bank Loans

### 5.4.1 Structuring a Bankable Loan Proposal

A bankable loan proposal is a structured document that answers the lender's questions before they are asked. It typically contains:

- **Executive summary:** The venture, the loan amount requested, the purpose, and the expected outcome.
- **Loan amount and purpose:** A precise statement of how much is required and exactly what it will be used for—machinery, working capital, expansion, or refinancing. Lenders are wary of vague purposes.
- **Debt repayment schedule:** The proposed tenor, moratorium (grace period), and instalment structure, aligned with the venture's projected cash flow.
- **Collateral offered:** The assets pledged as security—land, building, fixed deposit receipts, or hypothecation of inventory and receivables—with valuations.
- **Pro forma financial projections:** Projected income statement, cash flow, and balance sheet, with the assumptions stated and the DSCR calculated.
- **Supporting documents:** Trade licence, TIN, VAT registration, audited accounts (where available), bank statements, CIB clearance, and any sector-specific licences (environmental, sanitary, fire safety).

The proposal's credibility rests on **internal consistency**: the loan amount must match the project cost, the repayment schedule must match the cash flow, and the DSCR must exceed the lender's threshold.

### 5.4.2 The 5 Cs of Credit Appraisal

Bank loan officers evaluate applications through the **5 Cs of Credit**. Each C addresses a distinct dimension of credit risk.

**Table 5.3: The 5 Cs of Credit**

| C | Definition | Assessment Tool | Primary Risk Factor Addressed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Character** | The borrower's integrity, business reputation, and credit history | CIB report verification; track record; references; site visit | Willingness to repay |
| **Capacity** | The borrower's ability to generate cash flow to service debt | DSCR = Net Operating Income ÷ Total Debt Service; cash flow projections | Ability to repay |
| **Capital** | The entrepreneur's own equity invested in the venture ("skin in the game") | Debt-to-equity ratio; promoter contribution percentage | Commitment and financial buffer |
| **Collateral** | Secondary security pledged against the loan | Valuation of land, building, FDR, machinery; hypothecation | Recovery in case of default |
| **Conditions** | The economic climate, industry prospects, and competitive environment | PESTLE and Five Forces analysis; sector outlook; loan purpose | External risk factors |

**Character.** The lender asks: *Is this borrower trustworthy?* The **CIB (Credit Information Bureau) report** from Bangladesh Bank is the primary tool for verifying credit history. A clean CIB report—no defaults, no classified loans, no over-exposure—is a precondition for loan approval. Banks verify both the borrower's and the guarantor's CIB status.

**Capacity.** The lender asks: *Can this borrower generate enough cash to repay?* Capacity is assessed through the **Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)**:

\[
\text{DSCR} = \frac{\text{Net Operating Income}}{\text{Total Debt Service}}
\]

where Net Operating Income is profit before interest and tax (or net profit plus depreciation and interest), and Total Debt Service is principal repayment plus interest. A DSCR of **1.25–1.5** is typically required; a ratio below 1.0 means the venture cannot cover its debt service from operating cash flow.

**Capital.** The lender asks: *How much of their own money does the borrower have at risk?* A meaningful equity contribution—typically **20–30 per cent** of project cost—signals commitment and provides a buffer against loss. The debt-to-equity ratio is the standard metric.

**Collateral.** The lender asks: *What can we recover if the borrower defaults?* Collateral may be primary (the asset financed) or secondary (land, building, FDR). Where collateral is insufficient, **credit guarantee schemes** can substitute: Bangladesh Bank's CGS allows up to **Tk 50 lakh** collateral-free for CMSMEs, and the SME Foundation–Prime Bank programme allows up to **Tk 10 lakh** without formal collateral. Bangladesh Bank's master circular also requires banks to lend up to **Tk 5 lakh collateral-free** to every CMSME borrower, and up to **Tk 25 lakh and beyond** for specific cases, especially women entrepreneurs.

**Conditions.** The lender asks: *Is the broader environment conducive to repayment?* Conditions encompass the economic climate, the industry's prospects, competitive intensity, and the specific purpose of the loan. A loan to expand capacity in a declining industry, or to a venture exposed to a single customer, faces a higher conditions risk.

### 5.4.3 The Bank Loan Processing Workflow in Bangladesh

The loan approval process in Bangladeshi commercial banks follows a structured sequence. While procedures vary by bank, the broad workflow is consistent.

**Step 1: Loan application submission.** The borrower submits a completed application with the business plan, financial projections, and supporting documents—trade licence, TIN, VAT registration, bank statements, and audited accounts where available.

**Step 2: CIB report verification.** The bank obtains the borrower's **Credit Information Bureau (CIB) report** from Bangladesh Bank, verifying credit history, existing exposures, and any classified loans. A clean CIB report is mandatory; a default record typically results in rejection.

**Step 3: Site inspection.** A loan officer visits the business premises to verify that the venture exists, that the assets claimed are present and operational, and that the business activity is consistent with the application. The site visit also provides an opportunity to assess the borrower's character and operational competence.

**Step 4: Credit committee appraisal.** The credit committee evaluates the application against the 5 Cs, reviews the financial projections, calculates the DSCR, assesses collateral coverage, and considers the sector outlook. The committee may approve, approve with conditions, or decline.

**Step 5: Sanction letter issuance.** If approved, the bank issues a **sanction letter** specifying the loan amount, interest rate, tenor, repayment schedule, collateral requirements, and covenants. The borrower must accept the terms in writing.

**Step 6: Legal mortgage documentation.** The bank's legal department prepares and registers the mortgage or hypothecation documents, perfecting its security interest in the collateral.

**Step 7: Loan disbursement.** Funds are disbursed, often in phases linked to project milestones rather than in a single payment. Phased disbursement allows the bank to monitor utilization and reduces the risk of diversion.

**Step 8: Post-disbursement monitoring.** The bank monitors the borrower's account for repayment regularity, financial performance, and compliance with covenants. Early warning signs—late payments, deteriorating balances, adverse CIB updates—trigger intervention before the loan becomes non-performing.

## 5.5 Bangladesh Banking Vignette: A Tk 25 Lakh SME Term Loan in Gazipur

**The entrepreneur and the venture.** Md. Shahidul Islam owns a small plastic-moulding unit in Gazipur, on the outskirts of Dhaka. The unit produces household plastic items—buckets, mugs, storage containers—and supplies local wholesalers and a few retail chains. Shahidul started as a sole proprietor in 2016 with two second-hand injection-moulding machines and six workers. By 2026, he employs 18 workers, has annual turnover of approximately Tk 1.8 crore, and holds a valid trade licence, TIN, and VAT registration. His CIB record is clean.

**The financing need.** Shahidul wants to purchase two new injection-moulding machines and a small delivery truck, and to increase his raw-material inventory to meet rising demand. The total project cost is estimated at **Tk 35 lakh**: Tk 22 lakh for machinery and vehicle (CapEx) and Tk 13 lakh for working capital. He has **Tk 10 lakh** in personal savings and retained earnings, and he approaches **BRAC Bank** for a term loan of **Tk 25 lakh**.

**The application and CIB verification.** Shahidul submits a loan application with his business plan, three years of financial statements (prepared by an accountant, though not audited), bank statements, trade licence, TIN, VAT certificate, and quotations for the machinery and vehicle. BRAC Bank obtains his **CIB report** from Bangladesh Bank, which confirms no defaults and no excessive existing exposure. The report is clean.

**Site inspection.** A loan officer visits the Gazipur unit. She verifies the two existing machines, the inventory, the worker roster, and the order book. She notes that the unit operates from rented premises (monthly rent Tk 45,000), that the machines are well maintained, and that the entrepreneur has a waiting list of wholesale orders—evidence of demand.

**The 5 Cs assessment.**

- **Character:** Clean CIB, six years of operation, satisfactory bank statement conduct, and positive references from two suppliers. **Strong.**
- **Capacity:** Projected net operating income (after adding back depreciation and interest) is Tk 9.5 lakh in Year 1. Total debt service on a Tk 25 lakh loan at 14 per cent over five years, with a six-month moratorium, is approximately Tk 7.1 lakh per year (interest of Tk 3.5 lakh plus principal of Tk 3.6 lakh). **DSCR = 9.5 ÷ 7.1 = 1.34**, above the bank's 1.25 threshold. **Adequate.**
- **Capital:** Shahidul's equity contribution is Tk 10 lakh on a Tk 35 lakh project—**28.6 per cent**, above the typical 20–30 per cent margin requirement. **Adequate.**
- **Collateral:** The machinery and vehicle (Tk 22 lakh) serve as primary security. Shahidul offers a **Tk 8 lakh fixed deposit receipt** as secondary collateral, plus hypothecation of inventory and receivables. Total collateral coverage is approximately **Tk 30 lakh against a Tk 25 lakh loan**—a coverage ratio of 1.2, which the bank accepts, particularly given the availability of Bangladesh Bank's credit guarantee scheme if needed.
- **Conditions:** The plastic household-goods sector is growing with urbanization and rising disposable income, though raw-material (polymer) prices are import-linked and volatile. The bank notes the sector risk but considers it manageable given the venture's diversified customer base.

**The sanction and disbursement.** The credit committee approves a **Tk 25 lakh term loan at 14 per cent per annum**, with a **six-month moratorium** and a **five-year repayment period** (60 monthly instalments after the moratorium). The loan is disbursed in two phases: Tk 18 lakh for machinery and vehicle upon documentation, and Tk 7 lakh for working capital after the machines are installed and production begins. The sanction letter specifies the repayment schedule, the collateral, and covenants requiring the borrower to maintain insurance on the machinery and to submit quarterly financial statements.

**Post-disbursement monitoring.** The bank's relationship manager monitors the account monthly. In the first year, Shahidul's repayments are timely, and the unit's turnover grows to Tk 2.2 crore. The bank conducts an annual review, updates the CIB report, and considers a working capital enhancement the following year.

**Lessons from the vignette.** The loan was approved not because the venture was large or the entrepreneur was wealthy, but because the application satisfied each of the 5 Cs: a clean credit history, a DSCR above threshold, a meaningful equity contribution, adequate collateral, and a sector with favourable conditions. The phased disbursement and post-disbursement monitoring reduced the bank's risk and imposed a discipline on the borrower that supported the venture's growth.

## 5.6 Mathematical Worked Problems

### 5.6.1 Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)

**Problem.** A small garment-accessories supplier reports the following annual figures: Revenue Tk 1,20,00,000; Cost of Goods Sold Tk 80,00,000; Accounts Receivable Tk 18,00,000; Inventory Tk 12,00,000; Accounts Payable Tk 9,00,000. Calculate the CCC.

**Solution.**

\[
\text{DSO} = \frac{18,00,000}{1,20,00,000} \times 365 = 0.15 \times 365 = 54.75 \text{ days}
\]

\[
\text{DIO} = \frac{12,00,000}{80,00,000} \times 365 = 0.15 \times 365 = 54.75 \text{ days}
\]

\[
\text{DPO} = \frac{9,00,000}{80,00,000} \times 365 = 0.1125 \times 365 = 41.06 \text{ days}
\]

\[
\text{CCC} = 54.75 + 54.75 - 41.06 = \mathbf{68.44 \text{ days}}
\]

**Interpretation.** The venture's cash is tied up for approximately 68 days between paying for inputs and collecting from customers. To finance this gap, the venture needs working capital equal to roughly 68 days of operating costs. If the entrepreneur can reduce DSO to 40 days (through stricter collection) and DIO to 45 days (through leaner inventory), the CCC falls to approximately 44 days, releasing cash for other uses.

### 5.6.2 Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

**Problem.** A dairy venture projects net profit of Tk 12,00,000, depreciation of Tk 4,00,000, and interest expense of Tk 3,00,000 in Year 2. Its scheduled principal repayment is Tk 5,00,000, and interest is Tk 3,00,000. Calculate the DSCR.

**Solution.**

\[
\text{Net Operating Income} = \text{Net Profit} + \text{Depreciation} + \text{Interest} = 12,00,000 + 4,00,000 + 3,00,000 = 19,00,000
\]

\[
\text{Total Debt Service} = \text{Principal} + \text{Interest} = 5,00,000 + 3,00,000 = 8,00,000
\]

\[
\text{DSCR} = \frac{19,00,000}{8,00,000} = \mathbf{2.375}
\]

**Interpretation.** The venture generates Tk 2.375 of operating cash flow for every Tk 1 of debt service. This is comfortably above the typical 1.25–1.5 threshold, indicating strong debt-service capacity. A DSCR below 1.0 would signal that the venture cannot cover its debt obligations from operations.

### 5.6.3 Six-Month Cash Budget

**Problem.** A small bakery is launching operations. Prepare a six-month cash budget from the following assumptions: Opening cash balance Tk 5,00,000. Monthly sales: Month 1 Tk 3,00,000, Month 2 Tk 4,50,000, Month 3 Tk 6,00,000, Month 4 Tk 7,00,000, Month 5 Tk 7,50,000, Month 6 Tk 8,00,000. Of each month's sales, 40 per cent is cash and 60 per cent is collected in the following month. Monthly cash purchases of ingredients are 45 per cent of the current month's sales. Monthly operating expenses (rent, salaries, utilities) are Tk 2,50,000. A loan instalment of Tk 50,000 is due in Month 3 and Month 6.

**Solution (Tk in lakh).**

| Item | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 | Month 4 | Month 5 | Month 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Opening cash** | 5.00 | 3.05 | 2.10 | 1.85 | 2.30 | 3.05 |
| Cash sales (40%) | 1.20 | 1.80 | 2.40 | 2.80 | 3.00 | 3.20 |
| Collection of prior month (60%) | 0.00 | 1.80 | 2.70 | 3.60 | 4.20 | 4.50 |
| **Total receipts** | **1.20** | **3.60** | **5.10** | **6.40** | **7.20** | **7.70** |
| Ingredient purchases (45% of sales) | 1.35 | 2.03 | 2.70 | 3.15 | 3.38 | 3.60 |
| Operating expenses | 2.50 | 2.50 | 2.50 | 2.50 | 2.50 | 2.50 |
| Loan instalment | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.50 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.50 |
| **Total payments** | **3.85** | **4.53** | **5.70** | **5.65** | **5.88** | **6.60** |
| **Net cash flow** | **(2.65)** | **(0.93)** | **(0.60)** | **0.75** | **1.32** | **1.10** |
| **Closing cash** | **2.35** | **1.42** | **0.82** | **1.57** | **2.89** | **3.99** |

*Note:* Figures are rounded; collections in Month 1 are zero because no prior-month sales exist. The closing cash balance in Month 1 is Tk 5.00 − 2.65 = Tk 2.35 lakh. The cash trough occurs in **Month 3**, when the closing balance falls to Tk 0.82 lakh—still positive, but thin. If the bakery had budgeted no opening cash beyond CapEx, it would have faced a **negative balance of Tk 1.68 lakh** in Month 3, requiring emergency financing. The cash budget reveals the timing and magnitude of the financing need before it becomes a crisis.

## 5.7 Formula Reference Cheat-Sheet

| Concept | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash Conversion Cycle** | CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO | Days cash is tied up; shorter is better |
| **Days Sales Outstanding** | DSO = (AR ÷ Revenue) × 365 | Average collection period |
| **Days Inventory Outstanding** | DIO = (Inventory ÷ COGS) × 365 | Average inventory holding period |
| **Days Payables Outstanding** | DPO = (AP ÷ COGS) × 365 | Average supplier payment period |
| **Economic Order Quantity** | EOQ = √(2DS ÷ H) | Order quantity minimizing ordering + holding costs |
| **Reorder Level** | ROL = (Average daily demand × Lead time) + Safety stock | Inventory level triggering a new order |
| **Debt Service Coverage Ratio** | DSCR = Net Operating Income ÷ Total Debt Service | Debt-service capacity; ≥1.25–1.5 preferred |
| **Current Ratio** | Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities | Short-term liquidity; ≥1.2–1.5 preferred |
| **Debt-to-Equity Ratio** | Total Debt ÷ Total Equity | Leverage and capital structure |
| **Net Profit Margin** | (Net Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100 | Profitability per taka of sales |
| **Return on Equity** | (Net Profit ÷ Equity) × 100 | Return on owners' capital |

## 5.8 End-of-Chapter Exam Preparation Pack

### 5.8.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Question 1: Distinguish between CapEx and operating working capital, and explain why both must be financed.**

**Model Answer:** CapEx is investment in long-lived assets—machinery, vehicles, premises—that generate returns over multiple years and should be financed with long-term capital. Operating working capital is the cash required to cover day-to-day expenses—inventory, payroll, rent, marketing—until cash inflows stabilize, and should be financed with short-term capital. Both must be financed because a venture that funds only CapEx will run out of cash before it generates revenue; the working capital reserve bridges the gap between launch and sustainable cash flow. Financing CapEx without working capital is the most common cause of early-stage failure.

**Question 2: Compare equity and debt financing across ownership, cash-flow burden, and tax treatment.**

**Model Answer:** Equity financing dilutes ownership and shares control with investors, but imposes no mandatory cash-flow burden—dividends are discretionary—and is not tax-deductible. Debt financing preserves ownership and control, but imposes a fixed legal obligation to pay interest and principal regardless of profitability, creating financial risk; interest is tax-deductible, providing a tax shield. Equity is suited to high-growth, asset-light ventures with uncertain cash flow; debt is suited to asset-backed ventures with predictable cash flow.

**Question 3: Explain the Cash Conversion Cycle and its three components.**

**Model Answer:** The CCC measures the number of days a venture's cash is tied up in its operating cycle. It equals DSO + DIO − DPO. DSO is the average collection period for receivables; DIO is the average holding period for inventory; DPO is the average period before paying suppliers. A shorter CCC reduces the working capital the venture must finance. Negative CCC—collecting from customers before paying suppliers—is a source of internal financing.

**Question 4: What are the 5 Cs of Credit, and which risk does each address?**

**Model Answer:** The 5 Cs are: **Character** (integrity and credit history, verified through the CIB report)—willingness to repay; **Capacity** (cash-flow generation, measured by DSCR)—ability to repay; **Capital** (the entrepreneur's equity contribution)—commitment and buffer; **Collateral** (secondary security)—recovery in default; and **Conditions** (economic and industry environment)—external risk. Together they provide a structured framework for credit appraisal.

**Question 5: What is "growing broke," and how can a cash budget prevent it?**

**Model Answer:** "Growing broke" (overtrading) occurs when a venture expands sales faster than its working capital can support. Each additional sale requires inventory and credit before cash is collected, so rapid growth consumes cash faster than profits can replenish it. A cash budget projects monthly receipts and payments, identifies the **cash trough**—the month of minimum cash balance—and reveals the timing and magnitude of the financing need before it becomes a crisis. It is the primary defence against overtrading.

**Question 6: Describe the bank loan processing workflow in Bangladesh.**

**Model Answer:** The workflow proceeds through eight steps: (1) loan application submission with the business plan and documents; (2) CIB report verification from Bangladesh Bank; (3) site inspection by a loan officer; (4) credit committee appraisal against the 5 Cs; (5) sanction letter issuance specifying terms; (6) legal mortgage documentation; (7) phased loan disbursement; and (8) post-disbursement monitoring. Each step is a control point that reduces the bank's risk and imposes discipline on the borrower.

### 5.8.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions with Detailed Structural Outlines

**Essay Question 1: "Small firms in Bangladesh face a financing gap that is structural, not merely cyclical." Discuss with reference to the sources of finance available and the constraints on each.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** The CMSME financing gap is estimated at $2.8 billion (IFC), and 77 per cent of CMSMEs cannot access bank loans. The gap is structural—rooted in information asymmetry, collateral scarcity, and the mismatch between lending technology and small-firm characteristics.
- **Internal sources:** Savings and retained earnings are limited in scale; family and friends are finite and relationship-risky; trade credit depends on supplier goodwill.
- **Bank finance:** Banks require collateral, audited accounts, and CIB clearance; 80 per cent of CMSMEs are informal, lacking these. The "missing middle" is too large for microfinance and too small for corporate banking.
- **MFIs:** ME loans are growing (40 per cent of MFI disbursement) but remain small relative to need.
- **VC and angel:** The ecosystem is tiny ($6 million in H1 2026, 95 per cent decline, no domestic capital) and accessible only to scalable ventures.
- **Government schemes:** Refinance, credit guarantees (up to Tk 50 lakh collateral-free), and SME Foundation credit wholesaling (8 per cent capped, Tk 10 lakh collateral-free) are meaningful but underutilized.
- **Conclusion:** The gap is structural because the lending technology (collateral-based, audited-account-based) is mismatched to the borrower population (informal, collateral-scarce). Closing it requires credit guarantees, alternative data, and formalization support—not merely cheaper credit.

**Essay Question 2: "Working capital management is more important than profitability for the survival of a small firm." Critically evaluate.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Profit is an accounting construct; cash is a biological necessity. A venture can survive temporary losses but not a liquidity crisis.
- **The primacy of cash:** Overtrading illustrates that profitable, growing ventures can fail from cash starvation. The cash trough arrives before profitability.
- **The CCC as a management tool:** Shortening the CCC releases cash; lengthening it consumes cash. DSO, DIO, and DPO are levers.
- **The counterargument:** Profitability is the source of long-term cash generation. A venture that never profits will eventually exhaust its capital. Working capital management buys time; profitability creates sustainability.
- **Synthesis:** The two are complementary, not competing. Working capital management ensures survival in the short term; profitability ensures viability in the long term. The entrepreneur must manage both, but in the early stage, liquidity is the binding constraint.
- **Conclusion:** For a small firm, cash is king—but profit is the heir. Working capital discipline is the precondition for capturing the value that profitability represents.

**Essay Question 3: Evaluate the role of government concessionary schemes in closing the SME financing gap in Bangladesh.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Government schemes—refinance, credit guarantees, and SME Foundation credit wholesaling—aim to reduce cost and collateral barriers. Their reach and impact require assessment.
- **Refinance schemes:** Bangladesh Bank provides funds at concessional rates (7 per cent for MSMEs, 4 per cent for startups), enabling banks to lend below market rates. Strengths: lower cost. Weaknesses: uptake constrained by bank risk appetite and borrower documentation.
- **Credit guarantee schemes:** Tk 2,000 crore CGS allows up to Tk 50 lakh collateral-free. Strengths: addresses the collateral barrier. Weaknesses: guarantee fees, bank NPL conditions, and limited awareness.
- **SME Foundation credit wholesaling:** 8 per cent capped, Tk 1–25 lakh, Tk 10 lakh collateral-free, prioritizes women and clusters. Strengths: targeted, accessible. Weaknesses: scale (Tk 300 crore revolving fund is small relative to the $2.8 billion gap).
- **Assessment:** Schemes are necessary but insufficient. They address price and collateral but not the underlying information asymmetry or the capacity of small firms to produce bankable documentation.
- **Conclusion:** Concessionary schemes are a bridge, not a solution. Sustainable access requires building the credit infrastructure—guarantees, alternative data, formalization—that makes small firms bankable on commercial terms.

**Essay Question 4: "The 5 Cs of Credit are necessary but not sufficient for evaluating small-business loan applications." Discuss.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** The 5 Cs provide a structured framework, but their application to small firms is complicated by informality, data scarcity, and relationship lending.
- **Character:** CIB reports capture formal credit history but miss informal reputation and community standing, which matter in small-business lending.
- **Capacity:** DSCR relies on projected cash flow, which for informal firms is often estimated rather than audited. Projections are vulnerable to optimism.
- **Capital:** Equity contribution is measurable, but its source (savings vs. borrowed) matters.
- **Collateral:** Collateral is a recovery tool, not a repayment source. Over-reliance on collateral can substitute for genuine capacity assessment.
- **Conditions:** Sector and macroeconomic analysis is often generic; small firms may be more resilient (or more vulnerable) than sector averages suggest.
- **The missing dimensions:** Relationship history, business model viability, owner competence, and resilience are not fully captured by the 5 Cs. Relationship lending—where the loan officer's knowledge supplements the framework—fills some of these gaps.
- **Conclusion:** The 5 Cs are necessary as a checklist but insufficient as a decision rule. Sound credit appraisal combines the framework with judgment, site verification, and relationship knowledge.

### 5.8.4 Applied Credit Appraisal Problems with Solutions

**Problem 1: Credit Appraisal Decision**

*Situation:* A small agro-processing venture applies for a Tk 40 lakh term loan. The venture reports net profit of Tk 8 lakh, depreciation of Tk 3 lakh, and interest of Tk 4 lakh. Scheduled principal repayment is Tk 6 lakh, and interest is Tk 4 lakh. The entrepreneur contributes Tk 12 lakh equity on a Tk 52 lakh project. Collateral offered is Tk 35 lakh (land and machinery). The CIB report is clean. The sector is growing at 8 per cent annually.

*Task:* Calculate the DSCR and assess the application against the 5 Cs. Recommend whether the bank should approve.

*Model solution:*
- **DSCR:** Net Operating Income = 8 + 3 + 4 = Tk 15 lakh. Total Debt Service = 6 + 4 = Tk 10 lakh. **DSCR = 1.5**, above the typical 1.25–1.5 threshold. Adequate.
- **Character:** Clean CIB. Strong.
- **Capacity:** DSCR of 1.5 indicates adequate debt-service capacity.
- **Capital:** Equity of Tk 12 lakh on Tk 52 lakh = **23.1 per cent**, within the 20–30 per cent range. Adequate.
- **Collateral:** Tk 35 lakh against Tk 40 lakh loan = **0.875 coverage**, below 1.0. This is a weakness; the bank may require additional collateral or a credit guarantee.
- **Conditions:** Sector growing at 8 per cent. Favourable.
- **Recommendation:** Approve with conditions—additional collateral or a credit guarantee to raise coverage above 1.0, and a covenant requiring quarterly financial reporting.

**Problem 2: Working Capital Financing Decision**

*Situation:* A trading firm has annual revenue of Tk 2 crore, COGS of Tk 1.4 crore, accounts receivable of Tk 30 lakh, inventory of Tk 20 lakh, and accounts payable of Tk 14 lakh. It is offered a cash credit facility at 14 per cent per annum.

*Task:* Calculate the CCC and estimate the working capital requirement. Advise whether the firm should accept the facility.

*Model solution:*
- **DSO** = (30,00,000 ÷ 2,00,00,000) × 365 = **54.75 days**.
- **DIO** = (20,00,000 ÷ 1,40,00,000) × 365 = **52.14 days**.
- **DPO** = (14,00,000 ÷ 1,40,00,000) × 365 = **36.5 days**.
- **CCC** = 54.75 + 52.14 − 36.5 = **70.39 days**.
- **Working capital requirement:** Approximately 70 days of operating costs. Daily COGS = 1,40,00,000 ÷ 365 = Tk 38,356. Working capital gap ≈ 70 × 38,356 = **Tk 26.85 lakh**.
- **Advice:** The firm needs approximately Tk 27 lakh to finance its CCC. A cash credit facility at 14 per cent would cost approximately Tk 3.76 lakh annually in interest. The firm should accept the facility but simultaneously work to shorten the CCC—reducing DSO through stricter collection and DIO through leaner inventory—to reduce the financing requirement and interest cost.

## 5.9 Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the financing of small enterprises from the planning of financial needs through the sources of finance, working capital management, and the institutional loan process.

**Planning financial needs** requires distinguishing CapEx (long-lived assets, financed with long-term capital) from operating working capital (day-to-day expenses, financed with short-term capital), and fixed capital from circulating capital. The **matching principle**—matching the maturity of finance to the maturity of the asset—is foundational. The choice between **equity and debt** involves trade-offs across ownership dilution, cash-flow burden, financial risk, tax shielding, and control.

The **sources of finance** form a spectrum. Internal and informal sources—bootstrapping, savings, family and friends, and trade credit—are fast and non-dilutive but limited in scale. External and formal sources—commercial bank loans (overdrafts, cash credit, term loans), MFIs (microcredit and microenterprise loans), NBFIs and leasing, VC and angel investment, and government concessionary schemes (refinance, credit guarantees, SME Foundation credit wholesaling)—offer larger amounts but impose documentation, collateral, or equity requirements. In Bangladesh, mainstream SME lending costs 12–16 per cent, while concessional windows price at 4–8 per cent; credit guarantees enable collateral-free lending up to Tk 50 lakh, and the SME Foundation–Prime Bank programme caps rates at 8 per cent with no collateral up to Tk 10 lakh.

**Working capital management** is the discipline of survival. The **Cash Conversion Cycle** (DSO + DIO − DPO) measures how long cash is tied up; the **cash budget** identifies the cash trough and prevents "growing broke"; **receivables control** and **inventory management** (EOQ, ROL) keep working capital lean.

**Bank loan proposals** are structured around the **5 Cs of Credit**—Character (CIB), Capacity (DSCR), Capital (equity contribution), Collateral (security), and Conditions (environment)—and processed through a defined workflow of application, CIB verification, site inspection, credit committee appraisal, sanction, documentation, disbursement, and monitoring. The Gazipur plastic-moulding vignette illustrated the process in practice, and the worked problems demonstrated the calculation of CCC, DSCR, and a six-month cash budget.

## 5.10 Key Terms Glossary

1. **Capital expenditure (CapEx):** Investment in long-lived assets—machinery, vehicles, premises—used over multiple years and financed with long-term capital.
2. **Operating working capital:** The cash required to fund day-to-day operations—inventory, payroll, rent—until cash inflows stabilize; financed with short-term capital.
3. **Fixed capital:** Capital invested in long-term assets that determine productive capacity and are not consumed within a single operating cycle.
4. **Circulating (working) capital:** Capital invested in assets that circulate through the operating cycle—inventory, receivables, cash—and determine liquidity.
5. **Bootstrapping:** Launching and growing a venture with minimal external capital, using tactics such as leasing, shared space, lean inventory, and sweat equity.
6. **Trade credit:** The practice of purchasing goods on open account terms—receiving goods now and paying later, typically in 30–120 days.
7. **Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC):** DSO + DIO − DPO; the number of days cash is tied up in the operating cycle.
8. **Overtrading ("growing broke"):** Expanding sales faster than working capital can support, causing cash shortages despite profitability.
9. **Cash budget:** A monthly projection of cash receipts and payments that identifies the cash trough and the financing need.
10. **Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR):** Net operating income divided by total debt service; measures the capacity to repay debt from operating cash flow.
11. **5 Cs of Credit:** Character, Capacity, Capital, Collateral, and Conditions—the framework used by lenders to appraise loan applications.
12. **Credit Information Bureau (CIB):** Bangladesh Bank's repository of borrowers' credit histories, used by banks to verify creditworthiness and existing exposure.
