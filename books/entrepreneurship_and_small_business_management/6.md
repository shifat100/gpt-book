**Prerequisite Knowledge:** Completion of Chapters 1–5, including opportunity identification, business planning, and financing. Familiarity with the marketing mix concept and basic financial calculations (margins, break-even). ## 6.1 Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning (STP) for Small Firms

### 6.1.1 The Unique Nature of Small Business Marketing

Small-business marketing is not a scaled-down version of corporate marketing. It operates under a different logic, shaped by **resource constraints** that would be disabling if they were not also, paradoxically, a source of advantage.

**Resource constraints.** A small firm typically cannot afford mass-media advertising, a dedicated marketing department, or expensive market-research studies. Its marketing budget is a fraction of a large competitor's, and every taka spent must be justified by a measurable return. This forces discipline: small firms cannot afford to market to everyone, so they must choose their targets carefully.

**Lack of mass advertising budget.** Where a large firm can build brand awareness through television, billboards, and national campaigns, a small firm must rely on **targeted, low-cost, and relational** channels. The rise of digital platforms has partially levelled the playing field—Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok allow a small brand to reach millions at low cost—but it has also intensified competition, because every other small brand can do the same.

**Reliance on personal relationships and agility.** The small firm's owner-manager interacts directly with customers, suppliers, and the community. This proximity generates **customer intimacy**—fine-grained knowledge of preferences and pain points—that large firms struggle to replicate. It also enables **rapid responsiveness**: a small firm can adjust its offer, price, or message within days, while a large firm's marketing machinery grinds through approval layers.

**Table 6.1: Traditional Corporate Marketing vs. Entrepreneurial Small Business Marketing**

| Dimension | Traditional Corporate Marketing | Entrepreneurial Small Business Marketing |
|---|---|---|
| **Budget** | Large, allocated across mass media | Small, concentrated on high-ROI channels |
| **Decision structure** | Formal, layered, research-driven | Informal, owner-driven, intuition-informed |
| **Market scope** | Broad, mass-market or multi-segment | Narrow, niche or concentrated |
| **Primary channel** | Mass advertising, national distribution | Digital, direct, relational, community-based |
| **Customer knowledge** | Aggregated through research and data analytics | Direct, personal, qualitative, embedded |
| **Speed of response** | Slow—campaign cycles, approval layers | Fast—days or weeks, owner decides |
| **Brand building** | Long-term, media-reinforced | Reputation-based, experience-driven, WOM |
| **Risk tolerance** | Diversified across segments | Concentrated—betting on a niche |

### 6.1.2 Market Segmentation for Small Budgets

**Market segmentation** is the process of dividing a heterogeneous market into smaller, more homogeneous groups of buyers who share similar needs, characteristics, or behaviours. For a small firm, segmentation is not an academic exercise; it is a **survival strategy**. By focusing on a segment that is too small or too specialised to attract large competitors, the firm can achieve a dominant position without competing head-on against corporate giants.

The four conventional bases of segmentation are:

- **Geographic segmentation** divides the market by location—neighbourhood, city, district, region. For a small firm with limited distribution reach, geographic focus is often the first and most natural segmentation decision. A Dhaka-based cloud kitchen, for example, may serve only specific neighbourhoods where delivery times remain acceptable.
- **Demographic segmentation** divides by age, gender, income, education, occupation, and family status. Demographic segments are easy to identify and measure, but they are also the most contested, because large firms target them intensively.
- **Psychographic segmentation** divides by values, interests, lifestyles, and personality traits. A brand selling organic, chemical-free food is targeting a psychographic segment defined by health consciousness and environmental values, not merely by income.
- **Behavioural segmentation** divides by purchasing behaviour—usage rate, occasion, benefits sought, loyalty status, and readiness to buy. A firm might target heavy users of a product, or customers who buy for specific occasions (gifts, festivals), or customers who seek a specific benefit (convenience, durability, status).

**The small-budget principle.** A small firm cannot afford to segment on every dimension. The practical approach is to select **two or three bases** that jointly define a segment small enough to dominate but large enough to sustain the venture. A boutique cosmetics brand might segment on **gender (female), income (middle to upper-middle), and psychographic (ingredient-conscious, cruelty-free values)** —a combination that defines a manageable, reachable, and underserved niche.

### 6.1.3 Target Market Selection: The Concentrated (Niche) Strategy

Once segments are identified, the firm must choose which to serve. Three broad targeting strategies are available:

- **Undifferentiated (mass) marketing:** The firm ignores segment differences and offers one product to the whole market. This is rarely viable for a small firm, because it requires mass distribution and mass advertising.
- **Differentiated marketing:** The firm targets several segments with distinct offers. This requires more resources than a small firm typically possesses.
- **Concentrated (niche) marketing:** The firm focuses all its resources on **one well-defined segment**, aiming to dominate it rather than compete broadly. This is the **characteristic small-business strategy**, because it concentrates limited resources where they can have maximum impact.

**Why niche dominance works.** A small firm cannot outspend a corporate rival, but it can **out-understand** one. By serving a narrow segment intensively, the firm acquires deep knowledge of customer needs, builds strong relationships, and develops a reputation for expertise. The niche may be too small to interest a large firm—a market segment worth Tk 20 crore is trivial to a conglomerate but can sustain a focused small enterprise. The niche also provides a **defensible position**: the firm's specialisation is a barrier to generalist competitors who lack the knowledge and relationships the niche requires.

**The risk of niche concentration.** Concentration is a double-edged sword. If the niche shrinks, or if a larger competitor decides to enter, the firm has no fallback. The entrepreneur must monitor the niche continuously and be prepared to **migrate** to an adjacent niche if the core segment deteriorates—a capability that depends on the agility that small firms possess.

### 6.1.4 Positioning Strategy: Crafting a USP and Positioning Statement

**Positioning** is the process of shaping how the target customer perceives the firm's offer relative to competitors. It is not what the firm says about itself; it is what the customer believes. A small firm's positioning must be **distinctive, credible, and relevant** to the chosen segment.

**The Unique Selling Proposition (USP).** The USP is the single, clear reason why a customer should choose this firm over alternatives. A strong USP answers three questions: *What benefit do we offer? To whom? Why is it different from what competitors offer?* Examples from small-business contexts include: "The only organic baby-food brand in Bangladesh with a farm-to-jar traceability certificate"; "Same-day repair service for smartphones in Dhaka, with a 90-day warranty"; "Handwoven jute home décor, sourced directly from women artisans in Bogura, at prices below imported alternatives."

A weak USP—"quality products at affordable prices"—is not a USP at all, because every competitor claims the same thing. The discipline of crafting a USP forces the entrepreneur to identify what the firm does **genuinely differently** and to make that difference the centre of the marketing message.

**The positioning statement.** A positioning statement is an internal document that codifies the positioning logic:

> *For [target segment], [brand] is the [category] that [key benefit], because [reason to believe].*

For a boutique cosmetics brand: *"For ingredient-conscious urban women aged 22–35, GlowBotanica is the skincare brand that delivers visible results without synthetic chemicals, because every product is formulated with locally sourced, dermatologist-tested botanical ingredients and backed by transparent ingredient labelling."*

The positioning statement is not the advertising slogan; it is the **strategic anchor** from which all marketing messages, product decisions, and channel choices should flow.

## 6.2 Challenges and Opportunities in Marketing Diverse Entities

Small firms market a remarkable diversity of offerings—physical goods, professional services, digital products, experiences, and B2B subcontracting—and each category presents distinct marketing challenges and opportunities.

### 6.2.1 Marketing Diverse Offerings

**Tangible physical goods.** Goods can be inspected, stored, and transported; their quality can be assessed before purchase. Marketing goods emphasises **product features, packaging, and distribution availability**. The challenge is differentiation in crowded categories; the opportunity is tangibility—customers can see and touch the product, and the firm can use physical evidence (packaging, samples, demonstrations) to build trust.

**Professional services (legal, accounting, IT).** Services are **intangible, inseparable, perishable, and variable** in quality. A customer cannot inspect a legal opinion before purchasing it, cannot separate the service from the professional who delivers it, cannot store it for later use, and cannot assume that the quality of one engagement will be replicated in the next. Marketing professional services therefore relies on **credentials, reputation, case studies, and personal trust** rather than on product features.

**Digital products.** Software, online courses, and digital templates have near-zero marginal cost and can scale rapidly, but they face **credibility hurdles** (customers cannot easily assess quality before purchase), **piracy risk**, and **platform dependence**. Marketing digital products emphasises **free trials, freemium models, user reviews, and demonstrable outcomes**.

**Experiences.** Restaurants, tourism, events, and hospitality sell experiences—memorable, emotionally engaging, and inherently variable. Marketing experiences emphasises **storytelling, atmosphere, and social proof** (photographs, reviews, influencer visits). The challenge is **perishability**: an unsold table or room generates no revenue, creating pressure for dynamic pricing and promotional filling.

**B2B subcontracting.** Small firms that supply larger firms—garment subcontractors, light-engineering workshops, IT outsourcing providers—operate in **business-to-business markets** where the buying process is rational, multi-person, and relationship-driven. Marketing B2B subcontracting emphasises **capability demonstration, certifications, reliability, and references**, rather than consumer-style advertising.

### 6.2.2 Overcoming Specific Challenges

**Intangibility.** Because services cannot be seen or touched, the firm must provide **tangible cues**: professional premises, documented processes, certifications, testimonials, and case studies. A small IT consultancy, for example, can display client logos, project outcomes, and team credentials to make its capability concrete.

**Inseparability.** Because the service is produced and consumed simultaneously, the **person delivering the service is the product**. The firm must invest in staff selection, training, and service standards, and must manage capacity carefully—a solo consultant cannot serve unlimited clients.

**Perishability.** Unsold inventory of services cannot be stored. The firm must manage demand through **reservations, off-peak pricing, and promotional timing**, and must manage capacity through **flexible staffing and subcontracting**.

**Credibility hurdles for unknown startup brands.** New brands lack the track record that customers use to assess quality. The remedies are **social proof** (reviews, testimonials, influencer endorsements), **risk reversal** (money-back guarantees, free trials, sample sizes), **partnerships with trusted institutions** (incubators, banks, certification bodies), and **visible expertise** (content marketing that demonstrates knowledge).

**Price competition from established players.** Large competitors can undercut on price because they enjoy economies of scale. A small firm cannot win a price war; it must **compete on dimensions where scale is irrelevant**—customisation, responsiveness, personal service, niche expertise, and community embeddedness. The strategic response is to **avoid commodity positioning** and to build switching costs through relationships and specialisation.

### 6.2.3 Capitalising on Small Business Opportunities

**Highly customised customer service.** A small firm can tailor its offer to individual customers in ways that large firms, with standardised processes, cannot. Customisation creates **emotional switching costs**: the customer values the relationship and the fit, not merely the product.

**Direct community engagement.** Small firms are embedded in communities—physical or digital—and can build loyalty through **local sponsorships, community events, and reciprocal relationships**. A neighbourhood bakery that sponsors a school cricket team, or a skincare brand that hosts free ingredient-education workshops, builds goodwill that advertising cannot buy.

**Rapid responsiveness.** Small firms can respond to customer requests, complaints, and market shifts within hours or days. This responsiveness is a **marketing asset**: customers who receive a same-day reply or a rapid customisation feel valued, and they tell others.

## 6.3 Innovation Process in Small Business

### 6.3.1 The Nature of Innovation: Incremental, Frugal, and Disruptive

Innovation is not confined to breakthrough technologies. Three forms are relevant to small firms.

**Incremental innovation** improves existing products, processes, or business models in small, cumulative steps—a better packaging format, a faster delivery route, a simplified ordering process. Incremental innovation is the **most common and most accessible** form for small firms, because it requires modest resources and can be pursued continuously.

**Frugal innovation** strips products and services to their essential functions and engineers them for **affordability and accessibility** in resource-constrained markets. Frugal innovation does not mean low quality; it means **ruthless prioritisation**—removing features that add cost without adding value to the target customer. A solar-powered lantern designed for rural Bangladesh, or a low-cost water filter using locally available materials, are examples.

**Disruptive innovation** introduces a simpler, cheaper, or more accessible alternative that initially serves customers underserved by incumbents, then improves over time until it displaces the incumbent. Disruption is rare and difficult to engineer deliberately, but small firms are often its source, because they have nothing to lose from cannibalising existing products.

### 6.3.2 Managing the Innovation Pipeline on a Lean Budget

A small firm cannot run a corporate R&D laboratory. It must innovate through a **lean, customer-centred, iterative process**:

**Step 1: Customer problem discovery.** Before developing a solution, the firm must understand the **problem** from the customer's perspective: what pain are they experiencing, in what context, and what workarounds are they currently using? Discovery methods include interviews, observation, and immersion in the customer's environment. The output is a validated problem statement, not a product idea.

**Step 2: Minimum Viable Product (MVP) development.** The MVP is the smallest version of the product that allows the firm to test its core hypothesis with real users. It is not a prototype in the engineering sense; it is a **learning instrument**. A boutique cosmetics brand might launch with three products rather than a full line; a digital platform might launch with a single feature rather than a complete suite.

**Step 3: Gathering user feedback.** The MVP is released to a small group of target users, and their behaviour is observed—not merely their opinions. What do they actually do? Where do they get stuck? What do they ask for? Feedback should be **behavioural and specific**, not general expressions of approval.

**Step 4: Agile iteration.** Based on feedback, the firm revises the product, releases an improved version, and repeats the cycle. Each iteration is fast and inexpensive; the goal is to converge on product-market fit before committing substantial resources. The lean approach accepts that **most initial assumptions will be wrong** and builds the capacity to correct them quickly.

## 6.4 Small Business Branding

### 6.4.1 Demystifying Branding for Startups

**Branding** is often misunderstood as a logo or a colour scheme. It is neither. A brand is the **sum of perceptions, associations, and expectations** that customers hold about a firm. It is built through every interaction—the product experience, the customer service conversation, the packaging, the social media post, the delivery delay, and the way the firm handles a complaint. A logo is a **symbol** of the brand; it is not the brand itself.

For a startup, branding serves three functions:

- **Trust-building.** An unknown brand faces a credibility deficit. A coherent, professional brand identity signals that the firm is serious, competent, and likely to be around tomorrow.
- **Reputation formation.** Branding shapes the stories customers tell about the firm. A brand that consistently delivers on its promise generates positive word-of-mouth; a brand that overpromises and underdelivers generates the opposite.
- **Identity and differentiation.** In crowded markets, a clear brand identity helps customers distinguish the firm from competitors and remember it when they next need the product.

### 6.4.2 Developing Brand Identity on a Limited Budget

**Naming.** The name should be memorable, pronounceable, relevant to the category, and available (not already trademarked or used by a competitor). For Bangladeshi brands, names may be in Bangla, English, or a blend; the choice should reflect the target customer's language and aspirations. A name that is easy to spell and search online reduces friction in digital discovery.

**Visual identity.** This includes the logo, colour palette, typography, and imagery. A small firm does not need an expensive agency; it needs **consistency**. A simple, clean logo applied consistently across packaging, social media, signage, and invoices builds recognition more effectively than a complex, inconsistent identity.

**Packaging.** For physical products, packaging is both functional (protection, information) and communicative. It is a **tangible brand touchpoint** that the customer holds in their hands. Packaging should reflect the brand's positioning: a premium organic brand uses different materials, colours, and typography than a budget convenience brand.

**Emotional storytelling.** A brand story explains **why the firm exists**, what it stands for, and how it connects to the customer's values. A skincare brand founded by a dermatologist frustrated with harsh chemicals tells a different story than a brand founded by a farmer's daughter using family recipes. The story should be authentic—customers detect and punish inauthenticity—and it should be repeated consistently across channels.

**Customer experience consistency.** The brand promise must be matched by the **actual experience** at every touchpoint: the ease of ordering, the speed of delivery, the quality of the product, the responsiveness of customer service, and the handling of returns. Inconsistency between promise and experience is the fastest way to destroy a brand.

## 6.5 Setting the Price in Small Business

### 6.5.1 The Pricing Dilemma

Pricing is the most consequential and least understood marketing decision for small firms. Price too high, and the venture loses volume; price too low, and it loses margin—and may signal low quality. The dilemma is to find the price that **covers costs, reflects perceived value, and is competitive** in the target segment.

### 6.5.2 Specific Pricing Strategies

**Cost-plus (markup) pricing.** The firm calculates the full unit cost and adds a desired markup. The formula for a **markup on cost** is:

\[
\text{Selling Price} = \text{Unit Total Cost} \times (1 + \text{Markup \%})
\]

The formula for a **desired margin on selling price** (a different and often confused approach) is:

\[
\text{Selling Price} = \frac{\text{Unit Total Cost}}{1 - \text{Desired Margin \%}}
\]

For example, if a boutique cosmetics unit costs Tk 200 to produce and the founder wants a 40 per cent margin on the selling price:

\[
\text{Selling Price} = \frac{200}{1 - 0.40} = \frac{200}{0.60} = \text{Tk } 333.33
\]

Cost-plus pricing is simple, ensures cost coverage, and is common in manufacturing and retail. Its weakness is that it **ignores customer willingness to pay** and competitor pricing; a cost-plus price may be too high for the market or too low relative to the value delivered.

**Value-based pricing.** Price is set according to the **perceived value** the customer receives—the economic utility, emotional benefit, or status the product confers—rather than the cost of production. A skincare brand that solves a painful acne problem for a young professional can price at a premium because the customer's willingness to pay is high relative to the cost of the ingredients. Value-based pricing requires deep customer understanding and is harder to implement, but it captures more of the value the firm creates.

**Penetration pricing.** The firm sets a **low initial price** to attract customers rapidly, build market share, and achieve scale economies. Penetration pricing is suitable for price-sensitive markets and for products with network effects (where more users make the product more valuable). The risk is that low prices may signal low quality and may be difficult to raise later without losing customers.

**Price skimming.** The firm sets a **high initial price** to capture customers with the highest willingness to pay, then lowers the price over time as competitors enter or the segment saturates. Skimming is suitable for innovative products with a temporary monopoly and for prestige brands. The risk is that high prices attract competitors and may limit adoption.

**Competitive (going-rate) pricing.** The firm sets its price at or near the prevailing market rate, or slightly below it. This is common in commodity markets and in B2B subcontracting, where buyers compare quotes. It is simple and low-risk, but it offers little differentiation and can trigger price wars.

**Psychological pricing.** Prices are set to exploit cognitive biases: **charm pricing** (Tk 199 instead of Tk 200), **prestige pricing** (high prices signalling quality and exclusivity), and **bundle pricing** (combining products at a combined price lower than the sum of individual prices, increasing perceived value and average transaction value).

**Table 6.2: Pricing Strategies Comparison**

| Strategy | Method / Logic | Best Used When | Inherent Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost-plus** | Unit cost × (1 + markup %) or Unit cost ÷ (1 − margin %) | Manufacturing, retail, cost transparency | Ignores demand and competition; may over- or under-price |
| **Value-based** | Price reflects perceived customer value and economic utility | Differentiated products, strong brand, deep customer knowledge | Requires accurate value assessment; hard to execute |
| **Penetration** | Low initial price to build share and scale | Price-sensitive markets, network effects, new categories | May signal low quality; hard to raise later |
| **Skimming** | High initial price, lowered over time | Innovative products, prestige brands, temporary monopoly | Attracts competitors; limits adoption |
| **Competitive** | Price at or near market rate | Commodity markets, B2B subcontracting | Little differentiation; price war risk |
| **Psychological** | Charm, prestige, or bundle pricing | Consumer goods, gifting, premium segments | Can feel manipulative if overused; margins may suffer |

## 6.6 Designing Marketing Channels

### 6.6.1 Channel Architecture: Direct vs. Indirect

A **marketing channel** is the path through which a product or service reaches the customer. The choice of channel shapes cost structure, customer experience, and control.

**Direct channels (zero-level)** involve no intermediaries: the producer sells directly to the consumer. This includes a factory outlet, a farm shop, a direct sales force, an e-commerce website, or a social media page. Direct channels give the firm **maximum control** over pricing, presentation, and customer data, and they capture the full margin. They require the firm to bear the costs of distribution, however—logistics, payment processing, customer service—and they limit reach.

**Indirect channels** involve intermediaries: retailers, wholesalers, commission agents, or distributors. A **one-level channel** has one intermediary (producer → retailer → consumer); a **two-level channel** has two (producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer). Indirect channels extend reach and reduce the firm's distribution burden, but they dilute margin and reduce control over the customer experience.

**Table 6.3: Direct vs. Indirect Distribution Channels**

| Dimension | Direct (Zero-Level) | Indirect (Retailer, Wholesaler, Agent) |
|---|---|---|
| **Control over pricing and presentation** | High | Low to moderate |
| **Margin retained** | Full | Shared with intermediaries |
| **Reach** | Limited by firm's own capacity | Extended through intermediary networks |
| **Customer data** | Directly captured | Often mediated or unavailable |
| **Distribution cost** | Borne by the firm | Shared or borne by intermediaries |
| **Best suited to** | High-margin, differentiated, or digital products | Mass-market, low-margin, or geographically dispersed products |

### 6.6.2 Digital and Hybrid Distribution

Digital channels have transformed small-business distribution, particularly in Bangladesh.

**E-commerce websites.** A dedicated website (built on platforms such as Shopify, WooCommerce, or nopCommerce) gives the firm full control over branding, customer data, and pricing. It requires investment in development, payment integration, and digital marketing to drive traffic. The **Ladily** case illustrates the transition: a skincare brand that began as a Facebook-based, message-driven business moved to a structured nopCommerce store to manage inventory, pre-orders, nationwide delivery, and bKash payments as order volume outgrew manual chat handling.

**Third-party marketplaces.** Platforms such as **Daraz** and **Bikroy** provide built-in audiences and logistics infrastructure, reducing the firm's customer-acquisition burden. The trade-off is **platform dependence**: the marketplace controls the customer relationship, charges commissions, and may compete with the seller through its own private-label products.

**F-commerce (Facebook/Social Commerce).** Bangladesh's social media landscape is overwhelmingly Facebook-dependent. The country recorded approximately **71.95 million Facebook users in September 2026**, representing 39.7 per cent of the population, with young adults aged 18–24 forming the largest group. There are **over 300,000 active seller pages** on Facebook, with reported annual sales above **Tk 1,000 crore**. For many small entrepreneurs, a Facebook page is not merely a marketing tool; it is **the entire business**—the shop, the advertisement, the customer-service desk, and the payment gateway.

F-commerce's advantages are **low entry barriers, zero platform fees, and direct customer conversation**. Its risks are **platform dependence** and **algorithmic vulnerability**. Facebook's organic reach has collapsed: a page with 10,000 followers now reaches approximately **100 to 200 people per post organically**, down from an average of 16 per cent reach in 2012 to between 1.0 and 2.0 per cent in 2025. The platform increasingly rewards paid promotion and frequent Reels output, disadvantaging capital-constrained solo entrepreneurs. The strategic response is **channel diversification**: using F-commerce for discovery and conversation, but building owned assets (a website, an email or SMS list, a customer database) that are not subject to algorithmic whim.

**Logistics infrastructure.** Bangladesh's courier ecosystem—**Pathao Courier, RedX, Steadfast, eCourier, Paperfly**, and others—provides nationwide delivery with cash-on-delivery, tracking, and webhook integration, enabling small sellers to reach customers outside Dhaka without building their own logistics. Steadfast offers flat-rate pricing and 48-hour payouts; Pathao is fastest in Dhaka metro; RedX provides nationwide coverage with detailed tracking. The availability of these services has been a **critical enabler** of the F-commerce and D2C boom.

### 6.6.3 Selecting the Optimal Channel

Channel selection should be driven by four considerations:

- **Product type:** Perishable goods require short, controlled channels; digital products can be distributed directly at near-zero cost; bulky goods favour local or intermediary channels.
- **Margins:** High-margin products can support direct channels with their higher per-unit distribution costs; low-margin products require intermediary leverage.
- **Logistics:** The firm's capacity to store, ship, and deliver determines how much channel control it can sustain.
- **Customer convenience:** The channel must fit the customer's buying habits—where they search, how they prefer to pay, and how quickly they expect delivery.

Most small firms adopt a **hybrid channel**—for example, F-commerce for discovery, a website for transactions, and a courier for delivery—rather than relying on a single channel.

## 6.7 Marketing Communications in Small Business

### 6.7.1 Low-Cost Promotional Mix

A small firm cannot outspend competitors on advertising, but it can **out-communicate** them through targeted, low-cost, and high-credibility channels.

**Digital marketing.** Digital channels offer the small firm a reach and measurability that traditional media cannot match at comparable cost.

- **Content marketing** builds authority and trust by publishing useful, relevant material—how-to guides, ingredient explanations, customer stories, and behind-the-scenes content. A skincare brand that educates customers about ingredients builds credibility that advertising cannot buy.
- **Search engine optimization (SEO)** improves the firm's visibility when customers search for products or solutions. For a small Bangladeshi firm, SEO is often a **second-stage** investment after social media validation; the choice between SEO and Facebook boosting depends on whether the target customer searches on Google or scrolls on Facebook.
- **Social media marketing** on Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, and TikTok allows the firm to reach specific demographics with organic content and paid promotion. Facebook remains the widest-reach platform in Bangladesh, with **64–75 million users** across 2025–2026; TikTok has grown rapidly, with **56.2 million users aged 18 and above** in late 2025.

**Guerrilla marketing.** Guerrilla marketing uses **unconventional, high-impact, low-cost tactics** to generate attention and viral word-of-mouth. Examples include flash mobs, street stunts, creative public installations, humorous or provocative social media content, and partnerships with unexpected allies. The logic is to achieve disproportionate visibility through creativity rather than expenditure.

**Word-of-mouth (WOM) and referral programmes.** Word-of-mouth is the most credible form of marketing, because it comes from people the customer trusts. Small firms can **engineer** WOM through exceptional service (creating stories worth telling), referral incentives (discounts or gifts for customers who bring new customers), and **micro-influencer partnerships** (collaborating with credible, niche content creators rather than expensive celebrities). In Bangladesh, micro-influencers are particularly effective for beauty, food, and lifestyle brands, because their recommendations are perceived as authentic.

**Localized PR, community sponsorships, networking, and personal selling.** Small firms can generate publicity through local media, sponsor community events (school sports, cultural festivals, charity drives), and build networks through trade associations and business chambers. **Personal selling**—the owner-manager's direct engagement with customers and prospects—remains the most effective channel for high-value, relationship-driven products, particularly in B2B subcontracting and professional services.

## 6.8 Bangladesh Market Vignette: From F-Commerce Page to Omnichannel Skincare Brand

**The venture.** **Ladily** is a Bangladeshi skincare brand that began as a Facebook-based, message-driven business. Orders were confirmed through chat, inventory was updated manually, and customer conversations were handled one message at a time. As order volume grew, this model became unmanageable: tracking gaps emerged, inventory accuracy depended on manual updates, and pre-orders and outside-Dhaka deliveries increased cancellation and return risk.

**The transition.** Ladily migrated to a structured **nopCommerce e-commerce platform**, implementing a mobile-first one-page checkout, location-based advance-payment rules for outside-Dhaka orders, Facebook order capture inside the site (so Facebook remained a sales channel while the website became the single source of truth for inventory and reporting), pre-order inventory management, and **bKash payment gateway integration**. The platform was deployed on cost-efficient infrastructure, avoiding the recurring cost of public cloud services.

**The marketing engine.** Ladily's growth was driven by a combination of:
- **Targeted social media marketing** on Facebook and Instagram, where the target customer—young, urban, ingredient-conscious women—was already spending time.
- **Micro-influencer reviews**, which provided social proof and authenticity at a fraction of the cost of celebrity endorsements.
- **Local courier logistics** through RedX, Steadfast, and Pathao, enabling nationwide delivery with cash-on-delivery and tracking.
- **Content-led trust-building**: ingredient education, before-and-after content, and responsive customer service that converted first-time buyers into repeat customers.

**The outcome.** The transition transformed Ladily from a chat-based operation into a structured omnichannel business. Recent analytics showed approximately **18,000 unique visitors and over 2 million total requests in a 30-day window**, confirming reliable performance under production load.

**The lesson.** Ladily's trajectory illustrates the **F-commerce-to-omnichannel pathway** that many Bangladeshi small brands follow: start on Facebook because it is free, fast, and where the customers are; build traction through targeted content and micro-influencer credibility; then, as volume grows, migrate to owned infrastructure to gain control over inventory, data, and customer experience—while **retaining Facebook as a discovery channel** rather than abandoning it. The pathway is not inevitable—many brands remain on Facebook and stagnate or collapse when organic reach declines—but it is the most viable route from survival to scale in Bangladesh's platform-dependent market.

## 6.9 Practical Numerical Examples

### 6.9.1 Cost-Plus and Desired-Margin Pricing

**Problem.** A boutique cosmetics brand produces a face serum with the following costs: ingredients Tk 120 per unit, packaging Tk 30 per unit, direct labour Tk 25 per unit, and allocated fixed overhead Tk 25 per unit. Total unit cost = **Tk 200**.

(a) Calculate the selling price using a **50 per cent markup on cost**.
(b) Calculate the selling price using a **40 per cent desired margin on selling price**.

**Solution.**

(a) \(200 \times (1 + 0.50) = 200 \times 1.50 = \text{Tk } 300\)

(b) \(200 \div (1 - 0.40) = 200 \div 0.60 = \text{Tk } 333.33\)

**Interpretation.** A 50 per cent markup on cost yields a 33.3 per cent margin on price; a 40 per cent margin on price requires a 66.7 per cent markup on cost. Confusing markup with margin is a common and costly error: a firm that targets a "40 per cent margin" but applies a 40 per cent markup will actually earn only 28.6 per cent.

### 6.9.2 Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) and Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)

**Problem.** A skincare brand spends Tk 50,000 on Facebook advertising in a month and acquires 200 new customers. Each customer spends an average of Tk 800 per order, makes 3 orders per year, and remains a customer for 2 years. The contribution margin is 40 per cent.

(a) Calculate **CAC**.
(b) Calculate **CLV**.
(c) Calculate the **CLV:CAC ratio** and assess whether the marketing is sustainable.

**Solution.**

(a) \(\text{CAC} = \frac{50,000}{200} = \text{Tk } 250 \text{ per customer}\)

(b) \(\text{Annual revenue per customer} = 800 \times 3 = \text{Tk } 2,400\)
\(\text{Annual contribution} = 2,400 \times 0.40 = \text{Tk } 960\)
\(\text{CLV over 2 years} = 960 \times 2 = \text{Tk } 1,920\)

(c) \(\text{CLV:CAC} = \frac{1,920}{250} = \mathbf{7.68}\)

**Interpretation.** A CLV:CAC ratio of 7.68 is strong—well above the commonly cited threshold of 3:1 for sustainable growth. The brand recovers its acquisition cost within the first year and generates substantial lifetime value thereafter. However, the ratio should be monitored as the brand scales: acquisition costs typically rise as the easiest audiences are exhausted, and retention assumptions may prove optimistic.

## 6.10 End-of-Chapter Exam Preparation Pack

### 6.10.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Question 1: Why is concentrated (niche) marketing the characteristic strategy of small firms?**

**Model Answer:** Small firms lack the resources for mass advertising and mass distribution, so they cannot compete broadly against corporate giants. Concentrated marketing focuses all resources on one well-defined segment, allowing the firm to out-understand rather than outspend competitors. The niche may be too small to interest large firms but large enough to sustain a focused enterprise. The firm builds deep customer knowledge, strong relationships, and a reputation for expertise, creating a defensible position that generalist competitors cannot easily replicate. The risk is concentration: if the niche deteriorates, the firm has no fallback, so it must monitor the segment and be prepared to migrate.

**Question 2: What is a USP, and why is "quality products at affordable prices" not a USP?**

**Model Answer:** A Unique Selling Proposition is the single, clear reason why a customer should choose a firm over alternatives. It must specify the benefit, the target customer, and the difference from competitors. "Quality products at affordable prices" is not a USP because every competitor claims the same thing—it is a generic aspiration, not a distinctive advantage. A genuine USP identifies what the firm does **genuinely differently**: a traceability certificate, a same-day service guarantee, a direct-from-artisan sourcing model, or a formulation free of a specific harmful ingredient.

**Question 3: Explain the four characteristics of services (intangibility, inseparability, perishability, variability) and their marketing implications.**

**Model Answer:** **Intangibility** means services cannot be inspected before purchase, so the firm must provide tangible cues—credentials, testimonials, case studies, professional premises. **Inseparability** means the service is produced and consumed simultaneously, so the person delivering it is the product; the firm must invest in staff selection, training, and service standards. **Perishability** means unsold service capacity cannot be stored, so the firm must manage demand through reservations and off-peak pricing, and capacity through flexible staffing. **Variability** means quality differs across engagements, so the firm must standardise processes, train staff, and solicit feedback to ensure consistency.

**Question 4: What is an MVP, and how does it support lean innovation?**

**Model Answer:** A Minimum Viable Product is the smallest version of a product that allows the firm to test its core hypothesis with real users. It is a learning instrument, not a finished product. The MVP supports lean innovation by reducing the cost of experimentation: the firm releases a basic version, observes actual user behaviour, gathers specific feedback, and iterates rapidly—revising the product based on evidence rather than assumption. This approach accepts that initial assumptions will often be wrong and builds the capacity to correct them before committing substantial resources.

**Question 5: Distinguish between penetration pricing and price skimming, and identify the risk of each.**

**Model Answer:** Penetration pricing sets a low initial price to attract customers rapidly, build market share, and achieve scale economies. It suits price-sensitive markets and products with network effects. The risk is that low prices may signal low quality and may be difficult to raise later without losing customers. Price skimming sets a high initial price to capture customers with the highest willingness to pay, then lowers the price over time. It suits innovative products with a temporary monopoly and prestige brands. The risk is that high prices attract competitors and may limit adoption.

**Question 6: Why is F-commerce both an opportunity and a vulnerability for Bangladeshi small firms?**

**Model Answer:** F-commerce is an opportunity because it offers low entry barriers, zero platform fees, direct customer conversation, and access to a massive audience—approximately 72 million Facebook users in Bangladesh. It is a vulnerability because it creates **platform dependence**: the firm's entire business—shop, advertising, customer service, and payment—rests on a platform it does not control. Facebook's organic reach has collapsed to 1–2 per cent, forcing firms to pay for promotion, and algorithmic changes can destroy a business overnight. The strategic response is channel diversification: use F-commerce for discovery but build owned assets (a website, a customer database, an email/SMS list) that are not subject to algorithmic whim.

### 6.10.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions with Detailed Structural Outlines

**Essay Question 1: "Small firms cannot compete on price; they must compete on difference." Critically evaluate this statement with reference to segmentation, positioning, and pricing strategy.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Price competition favours firms with scale economies; small firms that compete on price enter a war they cannot win. Differentiation is the viable alternative.
- **The logic of price competition:** Large firms spread fixed costs over larger volumes, negotiate bulk discounts, and can sustain losses longer. A small firm matching prices may destroy its margins.
- **Segmentation as the foundation of difference:** By choosing a niche, the small firm identifies customers whose needs are not fully met by mass-market offerings.
- **Positioning and the USP:** Differentiation must be **perceived** by the customer. A USP that is not communicated is not a competitive advantage.
- **Pricing as a signal of difference:** Value-based and prestige pricing allow the small firm to capture the value of differentiation rather than competing on cost.
- **The counterargument:** In commodity markets and B2B subcontracting, price is the primary decision criterion; differentiation may be limited to reliability and service.
- **Conclusion:** The statement is broadly correct: small firms should avoid head-on price competition and compete on dimensions where scale is irrelevant—customisation, responsiveness, expertise, and community embeddedness.

**Essay Question 2: "Branding is a luxury that small firms cannot afford." Discuss.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** The statement reflects a common misconception that branding equals expensive advertising. Branding is the sum of customer perceptions, built through every interaction.
- **The cost misconception:** A logo and visual identity can be developed inexpensively; consistency costs nothing but discipline. Expensive agencies are not required.
- **Why branding matters for startups:** Trust-building (signals competence and longevity), reputation formation (shapes word-of-mouth), and differentiation (helps customers remember and choose the firm).
- **Low-cost branding tactics:** Consistent visual identity across touchpoints, authentic storytelling, packaging as a brand touchpoint, and experience consistency.
- **The risk of neglect:** Inconsistent branding creates confusion; broken promises destroy trust; without a brand, the firm competes only on price.
- **Conclusion:** Branding is not a luxury; it is a **discipline of consistency and trust-building** that costs little but yields compounding returns.

**Essay Question 3: Evaluate the role of digital platforms in enabling small-business marketing in Bangladesh, and assess the risks of platform dependence.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Digital platforms—Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, Daraz, Pathao, RedX—have lowered the cost of reaching customers, processing payments, and delivering products. They have enabled an F-commerce and D2C boom.
- **The opportunities:** Low entry barriers, access to mass audiences, direct customer conversation, built-in logistics and payments, and the ability to test and iterate cheaply.
- **The risks:** Platform dependence (the firm does not control the platform), algorithmic vulnerability (organic reach collapsed to 1–2 per cent), rising paid-acquisition costs, data limitations, and the risk of sudden policy changes.
- **The evidence:** Bangladesh's 300,000+ F-commerce seller pages and Tk 1,000 crore in annual sales illustrate the scale of opportunity; the collapse of organic reach and the stories of businesses destroyed by algorithm changes illustrate the vulnerability.
- **The strategic response:** Channel diversification—use platforms for discovery, but build owned assets (website, customer database, email/SMS list) and multiple distribution channels.
- **Conclusion:** Platforms are indispensable enablers but unreliable foundations. The resilient small firm treats them as **channels to be used**, not **infrastructure to be depended upon**.

**Essay Question 4: Design a low-cost marketing campaign for a new organic food brand in Dhaka, integrating STP, branding, pricing, channels, and communications.**

**Outline:**
- **Situation:** A new organic food brand targeting health-conscious urban households, with a limited budget of Tk 2 lakh for the first six months.
- **STP:** Segment on geography (Dhaka neighbourhoods), income (middle to upper-middle), and psychographic (health and sustainability values). Target a concentrated niche: families with young children. Position as "safe, traceable, chemical-free food for your family, delivered to your door."
- **Branding:** Name, logo, and packaging reflecting purity and transparency; storytelling about farm partnerships and testing protocols; consistent experience across delivery and customer service.
- **Pricing:** Value-based pricing (premium to conventional alternatives, justified by certification and traceability); introductory bundle pricing to encourage trial.
- **Channels:** Hybrid—F-commerce for discovery and community-building, a simple website for orders, and a courier partnership (Pathao/RedX/Steadfast) for delivery.
- **Communications:** Content marketing (nutrition education, farm stories), micro-influencer partnerships (parenting and health influencers), referral programme (discount for customers who bring new customers), community engagement (school events, farmers' markets), and guerrilla tactics (sampling at high-traffic locations).
- **Metrics:** CAC, CLV, repeat purchase rate, and referral rate; adjust the mix based on evidence after three months.
- **Conclusion:** The campaign integrates the marketing mix around a coherent positioning, using low-cost, high-credibility channels suited to the target segment.

### 6.10.4 Practical Marketing Strategy Problems

**Problem 1: Pricing and Break-Even for a Boutique Cosmetics Brand**

*Situation:* A boutique cosmetics brand produces a moisturiser with a unit total cost of Tk 250. The founder wants a 35 per cent margin on the selling price. Fixed costs are Tk 3,00,000 per month. The target market is willing to pay up to Tk 450 for a comparable premium product.

*Task:* (a) Calculate the cost-plus price using the desired margin formula. (b) Calculate the break-even volume at that price. (c) Assess whether value-based pricing could justify a higher price, and recommend a pricing strategy.

*Model solution:*
(a) \(\text{Price} = 250 \div (1 - 0.35) = 250 \div 0.65 = \text{Tk } 384.62\).
(b) Contribution per unit = 384.62 − 250 = Tk 134.62. Break-even volume = 3,00,000 ÷ 134.62 = **2,229 units per month**.
(c) The target market is willing to pay up to Tk 450, which is above the cost-plus price of Tk 384.62. Value-based pricing could justify a price of Tk 420–450 if the brand can demonstrate superior ingredients, dermatological testing, or ethical sourcing. **Recommendation:** Adopt a value-based price of Tk 425, which captures more of the perceived value while remaining below the customer's ceiling. At Tk 425, contribution is Tk 175 per unit, and break-even falls to 1,715 units—a 23 per cent lower volume requirement.

## 6.11 Chapter Summary

This chapter has developed a marketing framework tailored to the resource constraints and relational advantages of small firms.

**Small-business marketing** differs from corporate marketing in budget, decision structure, market scope, channel reliance, and speed. Its characteristic strategy is **concentrated (niche) marketing**: focusing resources on a well-defined segment to achieve dominance rather than competing broadly. **Segmentation** may be geographic, demographic, psychographic, or behavioural, but the small firm must select a manageable combination; **positioning** requires a distinctive, credible USP and a clear positioning statement.

Small firms market **diverse offerings**—goods, services, digital products, experiences, and B2B subcontracting—each with distinct challenges. Services face intangibility, inseparability, perishability, and variability; startups face credibility hurdles; and all small firms face price competition from established players. The response is to **compete on difference**—customisation, responsiveness, community engagement, and expertise—rather than on price.

**Innovation** may be incremental, frugal, or disruptive. Lean innovation proceeds through customer problem discovery, MVP development, user feedback, and agile iteration, allowing the firm to test assumptions cheaply and converge on product-market fit.

**Branding** is the sum of customer perceptions, built through consistent naming, visual identity, packaging, storytelling, and experience. It is not a luxury; it is a discipline of trust-building that costs little but compounds over time.

**Pricing** strategies include cost-plus (with markup on cost or margin on price), value-based, penetration, skimming, competitive, and psychological pricing. Each has a context in which it is appropriate and a risk that must be managed.

**Channels** may be direct, indirect, or hybrid. Digital channels—e-commerce websites, marketplaces, F-commerce, and courier logistics—have transformed small-business distribution in Bangladesh, but they also create platform dependence. The strategic response is **channel diversification** and the building of owned assets.

**Communications** rely on low-cost, high-credibility channels: content marketing, SEO, social media, guerrilla tactics, word-of-mouth, referral programmes, local PR, community engagement, and personal selling.

The **Ladily vignette** illustrated the F-commerce-to-omnichannel pathway: start on Facebook, build traction through targeted content and micro-influencer credibility, then migrate to owned infrastructure to gain control over inventory, data, and customer experience. The **numerical examples** demonstrated cost-plus and margin pricing, CAC and CLV calculation, and the interpretation of the CLV:CAC ratio.

## 6.12 Key Terms Glossary

1. **Market segmentation:** The process of dividing a heterogeneous market into smaller, more homogeneous groups of buyers who share similar needs or behaviours.
2. **Concentrated (niche) marketing:** A targeting strategy that focuses all resources on one well-defined segment to achieve dominance rather than competing broadly.
3. **Unique Selling Proposition (USP):** The single, clear reason why a customer should choose a firm over alternatives, specifying the benefit, target customer, and difference from competitors.
4. **Positioning statement:** An internal document codifying the target segment, brand category, key benefit, and reason to believe.
5. **Intangibility:** The characteristic of services that they cannot be inspected, touched, or tested before purchase.
6. **Minimum Viable Product (MVP):** The smallest version of a product that allows the firm to test its core hypothesis with real users.
7. **Frugal innovation:** Stripping products to essential functions and engineering them for affordability and accessibility in resource-constrained markets.
8. **Cost-plus pricing:** Setting price by adding a markup or desired margin to the unit total cost.
9. **Value-based pricing:** Setting price according to the perceived value the customer receives rather than the cost of production.
10. **F-commerce:** Facebook-based social commerce, a dominant small-business channel in Bangladesh.
11. **Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC):** Total marketing and sales spend divided by the number of new customers acquired.
12. **Customer Lifetime Value (CLV):** The total contribution a customer generates over their relationship with the firm.
