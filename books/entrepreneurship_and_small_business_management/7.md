## 7.1 The Small and Medium Enterprise Foundation (SME Foundation)

### 7.1.1 Historical Background and Institutional Mandate

The **Small and Medium Enterprise Foundation (SME Foundation)** was established by the Government of Bangladesh in **2006** under the Ministry of Industries. Its creation reflected a policy recognition that the CMSME sector—despite constituting over 99 per cent of enterprises and employing the overwhelming majority of the industrial workforce—lacked a dedicated institutional champion. Existing bodies, principally BSCIC, were oriented toward cottage and small industry infrastructure; the SME Foundation was designed to fill a different niche: **cluster development, credit wholesaling, women entrepreneurship promotion, and business support services** for the broader SME segment.

The Foundation operates as a **catalyst and wholesaler**, not a direct retail lender. It does not disburse loans to individual entrepreneurs from its own balance sheet; instead, it channels funds through **partner banks and financial institutions** and provides the technical assistance, cluster diagnostics, and market linkages that make those enterprises bankable. This wholesaling model allows the Foundation to leverage commercial bank branch networks while concentrating its own resources on the interventions that banks cannot provide.

Its programmes are aligned with the **National Industrial Policy 2022**, the **SME Policy 2019**, and the **Sustainable Development Goals 2030**. Since its establishment, the Foundation reports having supported nearly **2 million entrepreneurs**, approximately **60 per cent of them women**.

### 7.1.2 Core Functions and Strategic Interventions

**Credit Wholesaling Program.** The credit wholesaling programme is the Foundation's flagship financing intervention. Under this model, the SME Foundation provides funds to partner banks and NBFIs at concessional rates, and those institutions on-lend to SME entrepreneurs—particularly in clusters, rural areas, and women-led enterprises—at capped interest rates. The programme was initially piloted with the Foundation's own fund, offering **collateral-free loans at a 9 per cent interest rate** to technology-based and agro-based SME manufacturing industries. It has since been expanded under a **Bangladesh Bank Fund**, with dedicated windows for cluster financing, women entrepreneurs, and rural entrepreneurs. As of the most recent reporting, nearly **2,800 entrepreneurs have received approximately Tk 185 crore** in loans under the credit wholesaling programme and incentive packages.

The wholesaling model is significant because it addresses the **collateral bottleneck** that excludes most CMSMEs from direct bank lending. By absorbing a portion of the credit risk and providing a concessional funding line, the Foundation lowers the barrier for partner banks to lend to borrowers they would otherwise reject.

**Cluster Development Approach.** The SME Foundation has been the principal institutional driver of **cluster-based industrial development** in Bangladesh. A **cluster** is a geographic concentration of enterprises producing similar or related products, sharing suppliers, skilled labour, and market channels. Clusters generate **external economies**—specialised labour pools, supplier networks, knowledge spillovers, and collective marketing opportunities—that individual enterprises cannot create in isolation.

The Foundation's cluster mapping exercise identified **177 clusters in 2013**, involving nearly **2 million workers** (74 per cent men, 26 per cent women), spread across **51 districts**, and generating an annual turnover of approximately **Tk 30,000 crore**. A further **20 clusters** have since been identified, spanning light engineering, small garments, and other sectors. Development needs have been assessed in **95 clusters**, with regular interventions ongoing in **40**.

The Foundation's cluster interventions include infrastructure development, modern technology integration, design diversification, quality assurance training, raw-material recycling, policy advocacy for industrial recognition and **Geographical Indication (GI) certification**, cluster-based loans, ICT support, and the establishment of **Common Facility Centres (CFCs)**—shared facilities that give small enterprises access to expensive machinery, testing laboratories, or design services that no single firm could afford. Notable clusters include light engineering in **Dholaikhal and Bogura**, handlooms in **Sirajganj**, leather goods in **Hazaribagh and Bhairab**, and footwear in **Rajshahi**.

**Women Entrepreneurship Development.** Bringing women entrepreneurs into the mainstream is a prioritised activity. The Foundation's interventions include institutional capacity building for women's chambers and trade bodies, gender action plans, advocacy with bankers to increase finance to women entrepreneurs, studies on women's entrepreneurial constraints, women entrepreneur conferences, the **National SME Women Entrepreneurship Award**, and dedicated **SME product fairs for women entrepreneurs**. It has also operated a **One-Stop Business Advisory Services (OSS) platform** in partnership with other organisations, providing women entrepreneurs with support on bankability, loan products, financial literacy, and project-profile preparation, with a target of facilitating **1,500 women SMEs annually**. **Women Entrepreneur Advisory Desks** have been established at bank branches to provide dedicated guidance on loan applications and documentation.

**Capacity Building and Skill Upgradation.** The Foundation organises training programmes in public-private partnership modules, signing memoranda of understanding with training institutes and SME associations. Course content includes entrepreneurship development, cluster-wise skill training, technology and ICT-based training, training of trainers (TOT), productivity and quality improvement, marketing, management, and financial management. It also organises **national and regional SME product fairs**—the National SME Product Fair and the National SME Entrepreneur Award are annual flagship events—and **buyer-seller conferences** that connect cluster enterprises with commercial buyers. A two-day buyer-seller conference in Dhaka, organised with World Bank support, brought together entrepreneurs from **25 SME clusters and 46 enterprises** representing leather, light engineering, handloom, hosiery, fashion and home textiles, nakshi kantha, wood and bamboo crafts, pottery, and jewellery.

## 7.2 Bangladesh Small and Cottage Industries Corporation (BSCIC)

### 7.2.1 Historical Background and Organizational Mandate

The **Bangladesh Small and Cottage Industries Corporation (BSCIC)** is the oldest and most institutionally entrenched agency in the small-industry ecosystem. Its origins lie in the **East Pakistan Small Industries Corporation (EPSC)**, established in **1957** under the initiative of **Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujibur Rahman**, who was then the Industries Minister of the United Front government of East Pakistan. The legislation was designed to promote small and cottage industries based on local raw materials, with the objective of rural development through industrial decentralisation.

Following independence, the corporation was reorganised as the **Bangladesh Small Industries Corporation (BSIC)** in **1971**, and in **1976** it merged with the **Cottage Industries Corporation (CIC)** to become BSCIC in its present form. It operates under the **Ministry of Industries** and is governed by the **BSCIC Act** (the legal framework consolidated through the 1957 ordinance and subsequent amendments).

BSCIC's mandate spans **infrastructure development** (industrial estates and their operation), **private business investment promotion**, **technical and managerial services** (counselling and extension), **financial advisory**, **technical and managerial training**, **market development**, **design and prototype development**, and **pilot and demonstration projects**. Unlike the SME Foundation, which is a wholesaler and catalyst, BSCIC is an **infrastructure provider and direct service delivery agency**: it builds and manages industrial parks, operates training institutes, and provides extension services to enterprises located within its estates and beyond.

### 7.2.2 Core Programs and Operational Infrastructure

**BSCIC Industrial Estates.** BSCIC is the principal developer of planned industrial parks for small and cottage industries. Since its inception, it has built **76 industrial estates** across the country, generating more than **six lakh jobs**. The estates provide developed plots with internal roads, drainage, electricity, gas, and water connections, allowing entrepreneurs to establish production units without the cost and complexity of independent land development.

**Plot allotment criteria.** For newly established estates, the **District Industries Service Centre (ISC)** of BSCIC advertises plots in national and local newspapers. Applicants submit documents including the project profile, building layout, environmental clearance, and evidence of financial capacity. Plots are allotted according to guidelines that specify land prices (for example, **Tk 2,103 per square foot** at a recent engineering industrial park) and eligibility criteria. In practice, allotment has been complicated by infrastructure delays: at the Sirajganj Industrial Park, roads, drainage, and utility connections were incomplete when plots were handed over, and banks refused to lend against land for which BSCIC had not issued a lease document.

**Specialised estates.** In addition to general estates, BSCIC has established specialised estates: **Jamdani Palli** and **Hosiery** in Narayanganj, **Leather** in Savar, and **Electronic** in Mirpur, Dhaka. The **Tannery Industrial Estate at Savar** is the most consequential and most contested. Established on **200 acres** with **155 tannery industries** and a **Central Effluent Treatment Plant (CETP)**, it was designed to relocate tanneries from Hazaribagh and to provide modern environmental infrastructure. The estate has faced persistent delays in CETP commissioning and compliance, and there have been proposals to transfer its management from BSCIC to BEPZA or BEZA. The estate nevertheless illustrates BSCIC's role in hosting environmentally sensitive industries that require collective infrastructure.

**Technical Support and Extension Services: SCITI.** BSCIC delivers training through the **Small and Cottage Industries Training Institute (SCITI)**, established in **1985** and located at Uttara in Dhaka, with field-level training conducted through **64 industrial service centres** across the country. SCITI's courses include **Entrepreneurship Development** (two weeks), **Starting a Small Enterprise** (three weeks), **Non-Farm Employment for Rural Women** (three days), **Office Management** (one week), **Human Resource Management** (two weeks), **Project Management** (two weeks), and **Bookkeeping and Accounting** (one week). Field-level courses run for **2–5 days**. SCITI focuses on practical skills—food processing, handicrafts, small-scale trading, and basic manufacturing techniques—aimed at enhancing productivity and business viability.

**Credit and Marketing Assistance.** BSCIC operates **joint credit programmes** with banks and financial institutions, provides **financial advisory** services, and monitors **salt iodization** and **traditional handicraft and cottage industry promotion**. It prepares **industrial profiles**—detailed project reports covering product description, manufacturing process, market, location, production capacity, machinery requirements, and profitability estimates—to guide prospective entrepreneurs. These profiles are particularly valuable for first-time entrepreneurs who lack technical backgrounds.

**Agro-industrial and economic zones.** BSCIC has been involved in the development of **agro-industrial economic zones** and special industrial parks, including a planned **200-acre processing and backward-linkage zone** beside the Savar Tannery Estate. Its estate model has been replicated in specialised sectors, though the operational performance of individual estates has varied widely.

## 7.3 Other Institutional Support Organizations and Programs

### 7.3.1 Bangladesh Investment Development Authority (BIDA)

**BIDA** is the apex investment promotion agency, created in **2016** by merging the former Board of Investment (BOI) with other investment bodies. It operates under the Prime Minister's Office and is responsible for **investment registration**, **foreign joint venture facilitation**, **industrial project approval**, and **One-Stop Service (OSS)** delivery.

BIDA's **OSS platform** integrates approvals from multiple agencies—name clearance, company incorporation, e-TIN, trade licence, environmental clearance, VAT registration, factory and fire licences, and IRC/ERC—into a single digital workflow. The **BanglaBiz** portal, launched in **February 2026**, initially integrated five key approvals (name clearance, temporary bank account, incorporation, e-TIN, and trade licence) into a single application, enabling business registration within **three working days**—down from procedures that previously took two to three years. BIDA's royalty payment service has been fully digitised, with approval time falling from two months to **seven to ten working days**, and outward remittance repatriation approval is now delivered online within **seven working days**. For small enterprises, BIDA's OSS reduces the **transaction cost of formalisation**, though the platform's coverage of sector-specific approvals (BSTI, drug administration, etc.) remains incomplete.

### 7.3.2 Bangladesh Bank CMSME Refinance Schemes and Credit Guarantee Scheme

Bangladesh Bank operates the principal **concessional refinance windows** for CMSMEs, channeling funds through participating banks at rates below the market.

**CMSME Working Capital Refinance Fund (2026).** A **Tk 5,000 crore** revolving fund provides refinance to banks at **4 per cent**, with the customer-level rate capped at **9 per cent**. Eligibility is restricted to **operational CMSMEs** that are unable to utilise full production or service capacity due to working-capital shortages; **CIB-classified borrowers are not eligible**. The fund is designed to prevent viable enterprises from failing for want of liquidity rather than solvency.

**CMSE New Entrepreneurs Refinance Scheme.** Expanded from **Tk 100 crore to Tk 500 crore**, this scheme provides **collateral-free loans of up to Tk 10 lakh at a maximum of 7 per cent** to new entrepreneurs, with **collateral-backed loans up to Tk 35 lakh** available under the same window.

**Startup Fund.** A separate **Tk 500 crore** refinance fund provides loans to startup entrepreneurs at **4 per cent**—a nominal rate designed to make early-stage financing accessible to technology-driven ventures. The government has also signalled an intention to create a **Tk 1,000 crore fund** to support approximately **5,000 new entrepreneurs** with collateral-free loans at **4–6 per cent**.

**Credit Guarantee Scheme (CGS).** Bangladesh Bank's **Tk 2,000 crore** CGS enables banks to issue **collateral-free loans to cottage, micro, and small enterprises** ranging from **Tk 2 lakh to Tk 50 lakh**. The scheme provides guarantees against **up to 80 per cent of the loan principal** if the borrower defaults, and it covers working capital loans across multiple schemes. The CGS is the single most important instrument for addressing the **collateral bottleneck** that excludes most CMSMEs from formal credit.

### 7.3.3 Palli Karma-Sahayak Foundation (PKSF)

**PKSF** is the apex development finance institution for microenterprise funding. It does not lend directly to entrepreneurs; it provides **wholesale funds and credit guarantees to Partner Organizations (POs)**—microfinance institutions and NGOs—which then on-lend to microenterprises at the grassroots.

PKSF's **Credit Enhancement Scheme (CES)**, launched in **July 2025**, is the country's first credit guarantee scheme for microenterprise financing. Under the CES, PKSF signs guarantee agreements with commercial banks, which then lend to PKSF's POs; the POs disburse to microentrepreneurs. In its first year, **BDT 1,082 crore** was disbursed through **26 Partner Organizations**, and PKSF has signed agreements with **11 banks and 1 NBFI**, including BRAC Bank, City Bank, Prime Bank, NCC Bank, Trust Bank, Southeast Bank, and Mercantile Bank. PKSF reports a **99.9 per cent loan recovery rate**, a testament to the viability of group-based and relationship-based lending at the microenterprise level.

### 7.3.4 Commercial Banks: SME Divisions and Women Entrepreneur Desks

Every scheduled commercial bank in Bangladesh operates a dedicated **SME Banking division** or unit, mandated by Bangladesh Bank's CMSME financing targets. These divisions design SME loan products, manage the CGS and refinance portfolios, and conduct cluster-based lending. Banks have also established **Women Entrepreneur Desks** at branches to provide dedicated advisory support, and they participate in the SME Foundation's women entrepreneurship programmes. The **Bangladesh Bank CMSME loan target** requires banks to allocate a defined share of their loan portfolio to the sector, though actual achievement has lagged—less than 17 per cent of total bank loans went to CMSMEs by December 2025, against a 25 per cent target.

### 7.3.5 Startup Bangladesh Limited

**Startup Bangladesh Limited (SBL)** is the government's flagship **venture capital fund**, operating under the **ICT Division**. It is not a lender; its mandate is **equity and equity-linked investment** in promising technology startups. As of mid-2026, SBL had invested approximately **Tk 109–111 crore in 36 approved startups**, with a portfolio including Pathao, Chaldal, 10 Minute School, Shohoz, Shikho, and MedEasy. The investments span ride-sharing, edtech, fintech, logistics, healthtech, and e-commerce. SBL operates a combined funding structure that includes a proposed **Tk 400 crore Fund of Funds** and a **Tk 300 crore Co-Investment Fund**, with a stated intention to increase the total fund size to **Tk 1,000 crore** in phases. Portfolio companies have created more than **7,000 direct jobs**, achieved average revenue growth of nearly **five times**, and paid over **Tk 180 crore in taxes**—approximately 1.7 times the total amount invested.

### 7.3.6 Non-Government Organizations (NGOs)

The large NGOs—**BRAC, ASA, and TMSS**—operate microcredit, microenterprise lending, business development training, and market linkage programmes that reach segments beyond the formal banking system. BRAC is the largest, with **11.4 million people** accessing microfinance services and **8 million** using microloans totaling $6.6 billion. ASA ranks third with **6.77 million active members** and **5.43 million outstanding borrowers**. TMSS combines microcredit with handicraft and food-processing enterprises (TMSS Utpadon Foods, TMSS Handicraft), providing both finance and market channels for rural producers. The NGOs' strength lies in **last-mile reach** and **relationship-based lending**, but their loan sizes are small and interest rates are higher than concessional bank schemes.

## 7.4 Regulatory and Institutional Procedures for Starting a Small Business in Bangladesh

The formalisation pathway for a small enterprise involves a sequence of licences and registrations, each issued by a different authority. The sequence is not arbitrary: the trade licence is foundational, and most subsequent registrations require it.

**Step 1: Trade Licence.** Issued by the **City Corporation** (for urban areas), **Municipality**, or **Union Parishad** (for rural areas) where the business is located. Requirements include proof of premises (rent agreement or ownership documents), taxpayer identification, and payment of the prescribed fee. The trade licence is typically issued for **one year** and must be **renewed annually**.

**Step 2: Electronic Tax Identification Number (e-TIN).** Issued by the **National Board of Revenue (NBR)**. The e-TIN is required for opening a bank account, obtaining a trade licence in many jurisdictions, and filing income tax returns. It is obtained online through the NBR portal and is a prerequisite for VAT registration.

**Step 3: Business Identification Number (BIN) / VAT Registration.** Issued by the **NBR**. VAT registration is mandatory for businesses with annual turnover above the threshold (currently BDT 3 crore for companies, with lower thresholds for某些 categories). The BIN is the business's VAT identity and is required for issuing VAT invoices and claiming input credits.

**Step 4: Fire Safety Certificate.** Issued by the **Fire Service and Civil Defence**. Required for most public-facing and industrial premises under the Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting Act, 2003. The certificate requires a fire-fighting and safety plan, evidence of equipment installation, and inspection. It is **renewed annually**.

**Step 5: Environmental Clearance Certificate (ECC).** Issued by the **Department of Environment (DoE)** under the Bangladesh Environment Conservation Act. The category of clearance (Green, Yellow, Orange, Red) depends on the industry's pollution potential. Manufacturing units, food processing, and tanneries require ECC; the certificate is a prerequisite for factory licensing and bank financing.

**Step 6: BSTI Quality Certification.** Issued by the **Bangladesh Standards and Testing Institution (BSTI)**. Mandatory for specified products in food, cosmetics, chemicals, and certain industrial categories. Certification involves product testing, factory inspection, and issuance of a **Certification Mark (CM)** licence. It is renewed periodically and is essential for retail and export market access.

**Step 7: Factory Licence.** Issued by the **Department of Inspection for Factories and Establishments (DIFE)** under the Ministry of Labour. Required for manufacturing units employing the threshold number of workers. The licence requires compliance with building safety, worker welfare, and occupational health standards. It is **renewed annually**.

**Step 8: Import Registration Certificate (IRC) and Export Registration Certificate (ERC).** Issued by the **Office of the Chief Controller of Imports and Exports (CCI&E)**. Required for any business engaged in import or export. Applications are submitted online, with fees paid through a Treasury Challan. Both certificates must be **renewed annually**.

| Document / Licence | Issuing Authority | Core Requirements | Typical Renewal Cycle |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade Licence** | City Corporation / Municipality / Union Parishad | Premises proof, TIN, fee | Annual |
| **e-TIN** | National Board of Revenue (NBR) | Online application, identity documents | Perpetual (updated with tax filing) |
| **VAT / BIN** | NBR | Trade licence, e-TIN, turnover threshold | Annual (with VAT returns) |
| **Fire Safety Certificate** | Fire Service and Civil Defence | Fire plan, equipment, inspection | Annual |
| **Environmental Clearance (ECC)** | Department of Environment (DoE) | Industry category, environmental management plan | Periodic (category-dependent) |

| **Factory Licence** | DIFE (Ministry of Labour) | Building safety, worker welfare compliance | Annual |
| **IRC / ERC** | CCI&E | Trade licence, TIN, chamber membership, fee | Annual |

## 7.5 Contemporary Challenges, Policy Reforms, and Future Roadmap

### 7.5.1 Systemic Bottlenecks

Despite the institutional architecture described above, CMSMEs continue to face structural constraints that limit their contribution to output and employment.

**Collateral bias and credit rationing.** SME lending in Bangladesh is shaped by **conservative bank behaviour, high collateral dependence, and weak credit-risk infrastructure**, resulting in credit rationing. Lending terms are often determined by **collateral-based assessment rather than cash-flow viability**, and weaker borrower documentation and limited credit histories translate into higher risk premia. **68.6 per cent of small enterprises and 44.7 per cent of medium enterprises** report being finance-constrained, and over **one-third of economic units lack access to easy loans** because they are deemed non-bankable in the absence of necessary documents.

**Complex documentation and loan procedures.** Traditional bank borrowing models require **collateral, extensive documentation, and guarantees**, and the procedures are sufficiently complex that many small entrepreneurs—particularly those with limited financial literacy—are deterred from applying. Several refinance schemes remain **under-disbursed** because of collateral-related complications and procedural bottlenecks.

**High cost of doing business and infrastructure deficits.** Power interruptions, inadequate gas connections, poor road access, and incomplete utility infrastructure in industrial estates raise operating costs and reduce competitiveness. The Sirajganj Industrial Park case—where plots were handed over before roads, drainage, and utility connections were complete—illustrates the gap between estate allocation and operational readiness.

**Skills shortages and technology gaps.** CMSMEs report difficulties in recruiting skilled technical labour, and technology adoption is constrained by capital scarcity and limited access to extension services. Industry 4.0 technologies—automation, data analytics, digital platforms—remain largely inaccessible to micro and small enterprises.

**Market-access limitations.** Cluster enterprises often lack the design capability, quality certification, branding, and buyer relationships required to access modern retail or export markets. The SME Foundation's buyer-seller conferences and market linkage programmes are responses to this gap, but their scale is small relative to the need.

### 7.5.2 The National SME Policy 2019

The **SME Policy 2019**, gazetted in **December 2019**, provides the strategic framework for CMSME development. It is built on **six foundational pillars**: (1) **access to finance**; (2) **access to technology and innovation**; (3) **access to markets**; (4) **access to education and training**; (5) **access to business support services**; and (6) **access to information**. From these pillars, the policy articulates **eleven strategic goals**, including improving the investment and business environment, expanding institutional finance, enhancing competitive capability and market access, supporting startup businesses, and institutionalising SME statistics and research.

The policy's **cluster-based growth strategy** is its most distinctive operational feature. It directs implementing agencies—the SME Foundation, BSCIC, and the Ministry of Industries—to prioritise cluster infrastructure, common facility centres, technology upgradation, and collective marketing. It also commits to establishing an **SME Bank** and strengthening refinance schemes to provide loans at reduced interest rates. Implementation, however, has been uneven: the SME Bank has not been established, and coordination among the multiple agencies remains a persistent challenge.

### 7.5.3 Future Horizons

**Industry 4.0 and digitalisation.** The transition to **Industry 4.0**—automation, the Internet of Things, data analytics, and digital platforms—presents both an opportunity and a threat for CMSMEs. The opportunity lies in productivity gains, market access through e-commerce, and supply-chain integration. The threat is that capital-constrained small firms will be left behind as larger competitors adopt technologies that require investment and skills they lack. Government and donor programmes are increasingly oriented toward **digital literacy, e-commerce onboarding, and technology upgradation** for CMSMEs.

**Alternative capital markets: DSE SME Board.** The **Dhaka Stock Exchange SME Board**, introduced in **2021**, was intended to provide small manufacturers and startups with an alternative financing channel to bank loans. The board allows SMEs with **minimum paid-up capital of Tk 5 crore** to list and raise equity from qualified investors. Its market capitalisation grew from **Tk 3.6 billion in 2021 to Tk 24.6 billion by 2023**, but trading volumes remain thin and the listing pipeline is limited. The SME Board is a **long-term institutional building block**, not yet a transformative source of finance.

**Smart Bangladesh 2041.** The **Smart Bangladesh 2041** vision commits the country to a high-income, innovation-driven economy in which technology, digital governance, and entrepreneurship are central. The CMSME sector is identified as a **driving force**, comprising **95 per cent of total industry**, and the vision's action plans include digital transformation of CMSMEs, startup ecosystem development, and the establishment of SME centres in underserved regions. Achieving this vision requires closing the **financing gap**, upgrading **infrastructure**, building **digital and technical skills**, and ensuring that the institutional architecture—SME Foundation, BSCIC, Bangladesh Bank, BIDA, PKSF, and the private banks—operates as a coordinated system rather than a collection of disconnected programmes.

## 7.6 Field Vignette: Cluster Interventions in Bogura's Light Engineering Sector

**The cluster.** Bogura is the historic hub of Bangladesh's **foundry and light-engineering industry**. The **Bogura cluster** comprises approximately **700 engineering workshops, 500 agricultural-machinery and parts manufacturers, and 75 foundries**, producing **2,000–3,000 types of finished products** worth more than **Tk 500 crore annually**. The cluster meets roughly **80 per cent of the country's agricultural machinery needs** and employs thousands of skilled and semi-skilled workers. The **BSCIC industrial estate in Bogura** hosts foundries and parts-manufacturing workshops and employs approximately **3,755 people, including 700 women**.

**The enterprise.** Consider **Bogura Foundry & Engineering (BFE)**, a representative enterprise. The founder, a second-generation foundry operator, began as a small workshop producing spare parts for power tillers and irrigation pumps. The enterprise operated from a rented shed, used manual moulding and a cupola furnace, and sold through local traders. Its constraints were typical of the cluster: **limited space for expansion, unreliable power, lack of testing facilities, and no access to formal bank credit** because it lacked audited accounts and acceptable collateral.

**The BSCIC intervention: industrial estate plot.** BFE applied for a plot in the **BSCIC Bogura Industrial Estate** when BSCIC advertised allotments. The application included the project profile, building layout, environmental clearance, and financial statements. BFE was allotted a plot with **internal roads, drainage, electricity, gas, and water connections**, allowing it to construct a purpose-built foundry shed and install a larger furnace and moulding line. The estate location provided **shared infrastructure**—power substation, effluent drainage, and road access—that no individual small foundry could have financed. The **BSCIC extension services** provided technical counselling on furnace efficiency and quality control, and **SCITI** training upgraded the skills of supervisory staff.

**The SME Foundation intervention: cluster training and credit wholesaling.** BFE participated in **SME Foundation cluster training** on productivity, quality improvement, and marketing, delivered through a partnership with a local industry association. The training introduced **design diversification**—moving from generic spare parts to precision components for agro-machinery—and **quality assurance** protocols that allowed BFE to approach larger buyers. Simultaneously, BFE accessed a **credit wholesaling loan** through a partner bank under the SME Foundation's programme, at a **9 per cent interest rate and without conventional collateral**. The loan financed a **spectrometer and testing equipment**, enabling BFE to certify the metallurgical composition of its castings—a requirement for supplying original-equipment manufacturers.

**The outcome.** Within two years of the combined interventions, BFE had:

- **Expanded production capacity** by approximately 40 per cent, using the estate plot and upgraded furnace.
- **Replaced imported components** for agro-machinery assemblers, reducing their foreign-exchange costs and shortening lead times.
- **Secured supply contracts** with two agricultural-machinery brands that had previously imported precision castings.
- **Increased employment** from 18 to 34 workers, including 6 women in finishing and inspection roles.
- **Became bankable**: with audited accounts, a formal factory licence, and the estate lease as partial security, BFE subsequently accessed a **CMSME term loan** from a commercial bank to add a CNC machining centre.

**The lesson.** The Bogura case illustrates the **complementarity of institutional interventions**. BSCIC provided the **physical infrastructure**—the estate plot and utilities—that removed the space and power constraints. The SME Foundation provided the **soft infrastructure**—cluster training, design support, and concessional credit—that removed the knowledge and finance constraints. Neither intervention alone would have been sufficient: an estate plot without credit could not finance equipment; credit without space and power could not be productively deployed. The **cluster approach** worked because it coordinated infrastructure, finance, and capability development around a geographically concentrated group of enterprises with shared needs.

## 7.7 Step-by-Step Guide: How to Legally Register and Launch a Small Enterprise in Bangladesh

The following sequence reflects current practice, with the caveat that requirements vary by sector and location, and that BIDA's **BanglaBiz** platform is progressively integrating several of these steps.

```
STEP 1: Choose the legal form (sole proprietorship, partnership, private limited company, or OPC).
        → If incorporating, complete name clearance and incorporation at RJSC.

STEP 2: Obtain a Trade Licence from the City Corporation / Municipality / Union Parishad.
        → Requires premises proof and fee payment. Valid one year; renew annually.

STEP 3: Obtain an e-TIN from the National Board of Revenue (online).
        → Required for bank account opening, tax filing, and VAT registration.

STEP 4: Obtain VAT/BIN registration from NBR (if turnover exceeds threshold).
        → Enables VAT invoicing and input credit claims.

STEP 5: Obtain a Fire Safety Certificate from Fire Service and Civil Defence.
        → Requires fire plan, equipment, and inspection. Renew annually.

STEP 6: Obtain Environmental Clearance (ECC) from the Department of Environment.
        → Category depends on industry pollution potential. Prerequisite for factory licence.

STEP 7: Obtain BSTI Certification (for food, cosmetics, and specified industrial products).
        → Product testing and factory inspection. Certification Mark licence.

STEP 8: Obtain a Factory Licence from DIFE (for manufacturing units).
        → Building safety and worker welfare compliance. Renew annually.

STEP 9: Obtain IRC/ERC from CCI&E (if importing or exporting).
        → Online application, Treasury Challan fee. Renew annually.

STEP 10: Open a bank account in the enterprise's name.
         → Requires trade licence, e-TIN, and incorporation documents (if a company).

STEP 11: Register for any sector-specific licences (drug administration, BIDA registration for foreign investment, etc.).
```

**Practical note on sequencing.** The trade licence, e-TIN, and bank account are the **minimum viable formalisation triad** for a micro enterprise. Environmental clearance, factory licence, and BSTI certification become binding as the enterprise scales or enters regulated product categories. The **BanglaBiz Business Starter Package** now allows name clearance, temporary bank account, incorporation, e-TIN, and trade licence to be filed together within **three working days**, reducing the initial formalisation burden.

### 7.8.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions with Model Answers

**Question 1: Distinguish between the institutional roles of the SME Foundation and BSCIC.**

**Model Answer:** The SME Foundation (established 2006) is a **catalyst and wholesaler**: it channels credit through partner banks, develops clusters through training and common facility centres, promotes women entrepreneurship, and organises trade fairs and buyer-seller conferences. It does not build industrial infrastructure. BSCIC (established 1957) is an **infrastructure provider and direct service agency**: it develops and manages industrial estates, operates the SCITI training institute, provides extension services, and hosts specialised estates such as the Savar Tannery Estate. The Foundation addresses **finance and capability**; BSCIC addresses **land, utilities, and technical extension**.

**Question 2: What is the Credit Guarantee Scheme, and why is it important for CMSMEs?**

**Model Answer:** Bangladesh Bank's **Tk 2,000 crore Credit Guarantee Scheme** enables banks to issue **collateral-free loans to cottage, micro, and small enterprises** ranging from **Tk 2 lakh to Tk 50 lakh**. The scheme provides guarantees against **up to 80 per cent of the loan principal** if the borrower defaults. It is important because the **collateral bottleneck** is the binding constraint on CMSME access to formal credit: most small firms lack acceptable land or building collateral, and banks are unwilling to lend without it. The CGS transfers a portion of the default risk from the bank to the guarantor, making collateral-free lending viable.

**Question 3: Explain the six pillars of the SME Policy 2019.**

**Model Answer:** The SME Policy 2019 is built on six pillars: (1) **access to finance**—expanding institutional credit, refinance schemes, and an SME Bank; (2) **access to technology and innovation**—technology upgradation, reverse engineering, and certification; (3) **access to markets**—buyer-seller linkages, fairs, and GI certification; (4) **access to education and training**—skill development through SCITI and other institutes; (5) **access to business support services**—incubation, advisory services, and common facility centres; and (6) **access to information**—data banks and web portals. The policy articulates **eleven strategic goals** from these pillars, including cluster-based development and institutionalising SME statistics.

**Question 4: What are the main documents required to start a small manufacturing business in Bangladesh?**

**Model Answer:** The core documents are: **Trade Licence** (City Corporation/Municipality/Union Parishad), **e-TIN** (NBR), **VAT/BIN** (NBR), **Fire Safety Certificate** (Fire Service and Civil Defence), **Environmental Clearance Certificate** (Department of Environment), **BSTI Certification** (for regulated products), **Factory Licence** (DIFE), and **IRC/ERC** (CCI&E) if importing or exporting. For incorporated entities, **RJSC incorporation documents** (MOA/AOA) are also required. The trade licence is foundational; most other registrations require it.

**Question 5: Why do many CMSMEs remain excluded from formal bank credit despite refinance schemes?**

**Model Answer:** Despite refinance schemes, many CMSMEs remain excluded because the **lending technology** is mismatched to the **borrower population**. Banks require **collateral, audited financial statements, and formal documentation**; most CMSMEs are informal, lack audited accounts, and cannot provide acceptable collateral. Lending terms are shaped by **collateral-based assessment rather than cash-flow viability**, and weaker documentation translates into higher risk premia. Several refinance schemes remain under-disbursed because of collateral-related complications and procedural complexity. The **CGS and credit wholesaling** are partial responses, but their scale is small relative to the financing gap.

**Question 6: What is the DSE SME Board, and what are its limitations?**

**Model Answer:** The **DSE SME Board**, introduced in **2021**, is a dedicated platform on the Dhaka Stock Exchange that allows small and medium enterprises to raise equity capital from qualified investors. It requires a **minimum paid-up capital of Tk 5 crore**. Its limitations are: **thin trading volumes**, a **small listing pipeline**, and **eligibility thresholds** that exclude most micro and small enterprises. It is a **long-term institutional building block** for alternative finance, not yet a transformative source of CMSME funding.

### 7.8.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions with Detailed Structural Outlines

**Essay Question 1: "The institutional architecture for CMSME development in Bangladesh is comprehensive on paper but fragmented in practice." Critically evaluate.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Multiple institutions—SME Foundation, BSCIC, Bangladesh Bank, BIDA, PKSF, Startup Bangladesh, commercial banks, NGOs—cover the CMSME space. The architecture is comprehensive, but coordination is weak.
- **Coverage:** Each institution addresses a distinct gap: BSCIC (infrastructure), SME Foundation (cluster finance and capability), Bangladesh Bank (refinance and guarantees), PKSF (microenterprise wholesale), BIDA (formalisation and investment), Startup Bangladesh (equity), banks (retail credit), NGOs (last-mile reach).
- **Fragmentation:** Overlapping mandates, weak inter-agency coordination, and inconsistent eligibility criteria create confusion for entrepreneurs. The SME Policy 2019 commits to coordination, but implementation has been uneven. The proposed SME Bank has not been established.
- **Under-disbursement:** Refinance schemes remain under-subscribed because of collateral and documentation barriers; the CGS is underutilised; the credit wholesaling programme reaches only 2,800 entrepreneurs out of millions.
- **The Bogura case:** Successful outcomes occur where interventions are **coordinated**—BSCIC plot plus SME Foundation training plus credit wholesaling. Where coordination fails, interventions are siloed.
- **Conclusion:** The architecture is comprehensive in **coverage** but fragmented in **delivery**. The policy challenge is not creating new institutions but **integrating** existing ones around cluster-level and enterprise-level needs.

**Essay Question 2: Evaluate the effectiveness of Bangladesh Bank's refinance and credit guarantee schemes in closing the CMSME financing gap.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** The financing gap is estimated at $2.8 billion (IFC), and 77 per cent of CMSMEs cannot access bank loans. Bangladesh Bank operates multiple schemes to close the gap.
- **Refinance schemes:** The Tk 5,000 crore working capital fund (9 per cent), the Tk 500 crore new entrepreneurs scheme (7 per cent, Tk 10 lakh collateral-free), and the Tk 500 crore startup fund (4 per cent) reduce the **cost** of credit.
- **Credit Guarantee Scheme:** Tk 2,000 crore, guarantees up to 80 per cent, loans Tk 2–50 lakh collateral-free. It addresses the **collateral** barrier.
- **Effectiveness:** The schemes have expanded the **supply** of concessional funds, but **uptake** is constrained by bank risk aversion, documentation requirements, and borrower awareness. Many schemes remain under-disbursed.
- **Limitations:** Concessional pricing does not solve the **information asymmetry** problem; banks still lack the capacity to assess cash-flow-based lending to informal firms. The CGS covers only a fraction of the gap.
- **Conclusion:** The schemes are **necessary but insufficient**. They reduce price and collateral barriers but do not build the **credit infrastructure**—alternative data, cash-flow underwriting, formalisation support—that would make CMSMEs bankable on commercial terms.

**Essay Question 3: "Cluster-based industrial development is the most effective route to CMSME growth in Bangladesh." Discuss with reference to the Bogura and Sirajganj clusters.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Clusters generate external economies—specialised labour, supplier networks, knowledge spillovers, and collective marketing. The SME Foundation has identified 177 clusters with Tk 30,000 crore in annual turnover.
- **Bogura light engineering:** Approximately 700 workshops, 500 machinery manufacturers, and 75 foundries; meets 80 per cent of agricultural machinery needs. BSCIC estate provides infrastructure; SME Foundation provides training and credit. The cluster approach coordinated interventions.
- **Sirajganj handloom:** A traditional cluster with limited technical support, weak trade associations, and low formalisation (90 per cent trade licence, 30 per cent TIN, no VAT registration). Recent interventions include a weaving training centre and basic centre.
- **Effectiveness:** Clusters allow **shared infrastructure** (common facility centres), **collective bargaining** (supplier and buyer linkages), and **targeted interventions** (training, credit) at lower cost per enterprise than dispersed lending.
- **Limitations:** Not all clusters have the same absorptive capacity; Sirajganj's weak formalisation constrains access to formal credit. Cluster development requires **sustained coordination**, not one-off interventions.
- **Conclusion:** Clusters are the **most cost-effective route** to CMSME growth because they exploit agglomeration economies and allow interventions to be delivered at scale. But they require **institutional coordination** and **long-term commitment**.

**Essay Question 4: Assess the future of CMSME development in Bangladesh in light of Industry 4.0, digitalisation, and the Smart Bangladesh 2041 vision.**

**Outline:**
- **Introduction:** Smart Bangladesh 2041 envisions a high-income, innovation-driven economy. CMSMEs constitute 95 per cent of industry and must be central to this transition.
- **Opportunities:** Digital platforms (e-commerce, F-commerce, digital payments) lower market-entry costs; Industry 4.0 technologies (automation, data analytics) can raise productivity; alternative finance (DSE SME Board, fintech) can diversify funding.
- **Threats:** Capital-constrained micro and small firms risk being left behind; digital divides (rural connectivity, digital literacy) persist; automation may displace labour in labour-intensive sectors.
- **Policy requirements:** Digital literacy and e-commerce onboarding; technology upgradation funds; credit infrastructure reform (cash-flow lending, CGS expansion); coordination among SME Foundation, BSCIC, Bangladesh Bank, and BIDA.
- **The DSE SME Board:** A long-term building block, currently thin and exclusionary, but capable of growth if listing requirements are calibrated to smaller firms.
- **Conclusion:** The transition is **necessary but risky**. CMSMEs can be the engine of Smart Bangladesh 2041 only if policy addresses **finance, skills, infrastructure, and coordination** simultaneously.

## 7.9 Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the institutional architecture, regulatory procedures, and policy framework for entrepreneurship and small business development in Bangladesh.

The **SME Foundation** (2006) is the principal catalyst and wholesaler: its **credit wholesaling programme** has disbursed approximately **Tk 185 crore to 2,800 entrepreneurs** at concessional rates; its **cluster development approach** has identified **177 clusters** and supports **common facility centres**; its **women entrepreneurship** programmes include advisory desks, awards, and financial-literacy support; and its **capacity-building** programmes reach entrepreneurs through training, trade fairs, and buyer-seller conferences.

**BSCIC** (1957) is the infrastructure and extension agency: it has built **76 industrial estates**, operates the **SCITI** training institute, manages specialised estates including the **200-acre Savar Tannery Estate**, and provides technical counselling and industrial profiles.

The **supporting institutional architecture** includes **BIDA** (OSS and BanglaBiz three-day registration), **Bangladesh Bank** (Tk 5,000 crore working capital refinance at 9 per cent, Tk 500 crore new entrepreneurs scheme at 7 per cent with Tk 10 lakh collateral-free, Tk 500 crore startup fund at 4 per cent, and the Tk 2,000 crore CGS covering up to Tk 50 lakh), **PKSF** (Credit Enhancement Scheme with partner organizations), **commercial bank SME divisions and Women Entrepreneur Desks**, **Startup Bangladesh Limited** (Tk 109 crore invested in 36 startups), and **NGOs** (BRAC, ASA, TMSS).

The **regulatory pathway** runs from trade licence through e-TIN, VAT/BIN, fire safety, environmental clearance, BSTI certification, factory licence, and IRC/ERC, each with a defined issuing authority and renewal cycle.

**Systemic bottlenecks** persist: collateral bias, documentation complexity, infrastructure deficits, skills shortages, and market-access limitations. The **SME Policy 2019** addresses these through six pillars and eleven strategic goals, with a cluster-based growth strategy. The **future roadmap**—Industry 4.0, digitalisation, the DSE SME Board, and Smart Bangladesh 2041—requires coordinated action across finance, skills, infrastructure, and institutional integration.

## 7.10 Key Terms Glossary

1. **SME Foundation:** A government-established catalyst and wholesaler (2006) that channels credit through partner banks and provides cluster development, women entrepreneurship, and capacity-building services.
2. **BSCIC:** The Bangladesh Small and Cottage Industries Corporation (1957), the principal developer of industrial estates and provider of technical extension and training for small and cottage industries.
3. **Credit wholesaling:** A model in which a catalytic institution provides funds to partner banks at concessional rates for on-lending to target borrowers, rather than lending directly.
4. **Industrial estate:** A planned industrial park developed by BSCIC with internal roads, drainage, electricity, gas, and water connections for small and cottage enterprises.
5. **SCITI:** The Small and Cottage Industries Training Institute, BSCIC's training arm at Uttara, Dhaka, offering entrepreneurship development and skill-upgradation courses.
6. **Credit Guarantee Scheme (CGS):** Bangladesh Bank's Tk 2,000 crore scheme guaranteeing up to 80 per cent of collateral-free CMSME loans ranging from Tk 2 lakh to Tk 50 lakh.
7. **PKSF:** The Palli Karma-Sahayak Foundation, the apex wholesale funder for microenterprise financing through Partner Organizations.
8. **Startup Bangladesh Limited:** The government's venture capital fund under the ICT Division, investing equity in technology-driven startups.
9. **BanglaBiz:** BIDA's digital one-stop service platform integrating business registration and regulatory approvals, enabling registration within three working days.
10. **SME Policy 2019:** The national policy framework built on six pillars (finance, technology, markets, education, business support, information) and eleven strategic goals.
11. **DSE SME Board:** The Dhaka Stock Exchange's dedicated platform (2021) for SME equity listings, requiring minimum paid-up capital of Tk 5 crore.
12. **Smart Bangladesh 2041:** The national vision for a high-income, innovation-driven economy, with CMSME digitalisation and startup development as central pillars.
