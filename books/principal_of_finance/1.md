## 1.1 What is Finance?

Imagine you want to open a small coffee shop. You need money to rent a space, buy an espresso machine, and purchase beans. Once the shop is running, money flows in from customers and flows out to pay suppliers and employees. Some months you have extra cash; other months you need a short‑term loan. Eventually you may want to open a second location, requiring a much larger sum. Every one of these activities—raising money, spending it wisely, managing daily cash, and planning for the future—is the essence of **finance**.

**Finance** is the art and science of managing money. At the personal level, it is about budgeting, saving, and investing. At the business level, it is about making decisions that increase the value of the firm. At the government level, it is about collecting taxes and funding public projects. In all cases, finance deals with how money is **raised** (financing), **allocated** (investing), and **managed** (cash and risk management) over time.

> **Analogy:** Finance is the plumbing system of the economy. Just as pipes carry water to where it is needed, financial systems channel funds from those who have surplus money (savers) to those who need it for productive use (borrowers).

---

## 1.2 Functions of Finance

Within a business, finance serves four fundamental functions:

- **Financing (Raising Capital):** Deciding how to obtain the funds needed to start and grow. This could mean borrowing from a bank (debt), selling ownership shares (equity), or using internally generated profits.
- **Investing (Capital Budgeting):** Choosing which long‑term projects to undertake, such as buying new machinery, launching a new product, or acquiring another company. The goal is to invest in assets that are worth more than they cost.
- **Liquidity Management (Working Capital):** Ensuring the firm has enough cash to pay its bills on time. This involves managing the timing of cash inflows from customers and outflows to suppliers.
- **Risk Management:** Identifying, measuring, and reducing the financial risks the firm faces—for example, the risk that interest rates rise, that a foreign currency moves against the firm, or that a key customer fails to pay.

These functions are not isolated; they constantly interact. A decision to build a new factory (investing) requires a decision on whether to raise the money by issuing bonds or by selling new shares (financing), and it will affect the firm’s daily cash needs for years (liquidity).

## 1.3 The Goal of a Firm: More Than Just Profit

When you ask someone “What is the goal of a business?”, a common answer is “to make as much profit as possible.” In finance, however, we draw a sharp distinction between **profit maximization** and **wealth maximization**.

### Profit Maximization: A Flawed Target

**Profit maximization** aims to make the yearly accounting profit as large as possible. While this sounds appealing, it suffers from three serious defects:

1. **It ignores the timing of returns.**  
   A project that generates a ₹100 crore profit next year is more valuable than one that generates the same profit ten years from now. Accounting profit treats both as equal.
2. **It ignores risk.**  
   Two projects may both promise a ₹50 crore profit, but if one is a safe government contract and the other is a speculative venture, a rational investor prefers the safer one. Profit numbers alone do not show risk.
3. **It ignores the size of the initial investment.**  
   A ₹10 crore profit on a ₹100 crore investment is far less attractive than the same profit on a ₹10 crore investment. Focusing purely on profit ignores how much capital was required.

> **Analogy:** If your only goal on a road trip is to maximize speed, you might race ahead recklessly, ignore fuel warnings, and crash. Profit maximization is like staring only at the speedometer—it ignores the destination, the road conditions, and whether you even have enough fuel to arrive.

### Wealth Maximization: The Smarter Goal

**Wealth maximization** (also called **value maximization**) means maximising the current market value of the firm’s equity shares. It focuses on the long‑term wealth of the owners—the shareholders. This goal automatically accounts for the size, timing, and risk of all future cash flows the firm can generate.

- It looks at **cash flows**, not accounting profits, because cash is what ultimately pays bills and rewards shareholders.
- It considers **when** cash flows occur—a rupee earned today is worth more than a rupee earned later.
- It discounts cash flows for **risk**—riskier cash flows are worth less today.

Thus, under wealth maximization, a manager should undertake any investment that increases the share price. This aligns perfectly with the owners’ interests: a higher share price means a wealthier shareholder.

### The Agency Problem: When Managers and Owners Clash

In many large firms, the people who own the company (shareholders/principals) are not the same as the people who run it day‑to‑day (managers/agents). This separation creates an **agency problem**—a conflict of interest where managers may act in their own self‑interest rather than in the best interest of shareholders.

Examples of agency conflicts:
- Managers may pursue personal prestige projects (a lavish headquarters, an acquisition that boosts their ego) that do not increase shareholder wealth.
- They may avoid risky but positive‑value investments to protect their job security.
- They may consume excessive perks at company expense.

If left unchecked, the agency problem destroys shareholder value and weakens the very purpose of a firm.

**Resolving the Agency Problem**  
Several mechanisms help align managers’ interests with those of shareholders:
- **Performance‑based compensation:** Linking managerial pay to the company’s share price via stock options or restricted stock grants. This turns managers into shareholders.
- **Monitoring by the board of directors:** An independent board can question decisions and, if necessary, replace underperforming managers.
- **Threat of takeover:** If a firm’s share price falls because of poor management, another company may acquire it, often replacing the management team. This fear keeps managers focused.
- **Shareholder activism:** Large institutional investors can demand changes in strategy or governance.
- **Proper disclosure and audit:** Transparent financial reporting reduces the ability of managers to hide selfish actions.

Together, these tools reduce agency costs and help ensure the firm pursues wealth maximization.

## 1.4 Why Do We Need Financial Markets?

Think of a village where some people have surplus grain and others need grain to plant their fields. Without a marketplace, the surplus might rot while farmers starve. A farmers’ market connects the two groups, setting a fair price and moving grain from where it is plentiful to where it is needed.

**Financial markets** perform exactly this role for money. They are mechanisms that bring together **surplus units** (households, firms, governments that have extra funds) and **deficit units** (those that need funds for consumption or investment). Without well‑functioning financial markets, savings would sit idle under mattresses, and promising business ideas would never be funded.

Key purposes of financial markets:
- **Facilitate the flow of funds:** Channel savings into productive investments.
- **Provide liquidity:** Allow investors to quickly convert assets into cash without a significant loss of value.
- **Price discovery:** Through the interaction of buyers and sellers, markets determine the fair price of financial assets (shares, bonds, etc.).
- **Risk sharing:** They enable the transfer of risk from those unwilling to bear it to those willing, for a price (e.g., via insurance or derivatives).

## 1.5 Classifying Financial Markets

Financial markets can be sliced in several ways. The two most important classifications for a beginner are **money vs. capital markets** and **primary vs. secondary markets**.

### Money Market vs. Capital Market

This distinction is based on the **maturity** of the instruments traded.

| Feature | **Money Market** | **Capital Market** |
|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Maturity | Short‑term (less than one year) | Long‑term (more than one year) |
| Purpose | Meet temporary cash shortages or park surplus cash | Finance long‑term investments |
| Instruments | Treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit | Shares (equity), debentures, bonds |
| Risk & Liquidity | Very low risk, highly liquid | Higher risk, less liquid (but still tradeable) |
| Participants | Banks, mutual funds, central banks, large corporations | Individual investors, institutions, companies, governments |

> **Analogy:** The money market is like a revolving line of credit you use for daily working capital; the capital market is the 20‑year mortgage you take out to buy a house.

### Primary Market vs. Secondary Market

This classification is about whether the securities are **newly issued** or **already existing**.

- **Primary Market:** The market where companies and governments **raise fresh capital** by issuing new securities to investors. Example: when a company launches an **Initial Public Offering (IPO)** to sell its shares to the public for the first time. The money flows directly from investors to the issuing firm.
- **Secondary Market:** The market where **already‑issued securities** are traded among investors. The company that originally issued the shares does not receive any money from these trades. Example: buying shares of a listed company on the stock exchange from another investor. The secondary market provides **liquidity**—making it easy to sell the shares whenever you need cash. Without a liquid secondary market, few people would be willing to buy shares in the primary market.

> **Analogy:** The primary market is like buying a new car directly from the factory. The secondary market is the used‑car market, where owners trade cars among themselves; the factory gets no money from these resales, but the existence of a used‑car market makes you more confident to buy a new car in the first place.

## 1.6 The Middlemen: Financial Intermediaries and Brokers

Funds can move from savers to borrowers in two ways:

1. **Direct transfer:** The saver buys the borrower’s securities directly (e.g., buying shares through a stock exchange). Here, a **broker** merely brings the buyer and seller together and charges a commission, without taking ownership of the securities.
2. **Indirect transfer through a financial intermediary:** The saver deposits money with a bank or buys units of a mutual fund; the intermediary then lends or invests that pool of funds. **Financial intermediaries** issue their own claims to savers (e.g., a savings account) and purchase claims from borrowers (e.g., a mortgage loan).

**Financial Intermediaries** (banks, insurance companies, mutual funds, pension funds) perform critical roles:
- **Maturity transformation:** They convert short‑term deposits into long‑term loans, allowing savers to access their money quickly while borrowers get long‑term financing.
- **Risk reduction:** By pooling thousands of small deposits, they can diversify their loan portfolios, greatly reducing the risk that a single default will wipe out a saver.
- **Cost reduction:** They achieve economies of scale in evaluating creditworthiness, processing payments, and enforcing contracts.

**Brokers**, on the other hand, simply match buyers and sellers. They do not take deposits, do not create new financial products, and do not bear the risk of the instruments they trade. Their value lies in reducing search costs and facilitating transactions.

Without these institutions, lending would be a cumbersome, risky, face‑to‑face affair, severely limiting economic growth.

## 1.7 The Time Value of Money: A Dollar Today is Worth More

If someone offers you ₹10,000 today or ₹10,000 one year from now, you would instantly choose today. Why? Because you can invest today’s money and earn interest, making it grow to more than ₹10,000 in a year. This fundamental idea is the **time value of money** (TVM): a rupee received today is worth more than a rupee received in the future because of its potential earning capacity.

TVM is the bedrock of all financial decisions—valuing stocks, choosing between projects, pricing loans. Let’s build it step by step.

### Simple vs. Compound Interest

- **Simple interest** is calculated only on the original principal.
  - Example: ₹1,000 invested at 6% p.a. simple interest for 3 years yields ₹60 each year. Total amount = ₹1,000 + (3 × ₹60) = ₹1,180.
- **Compound interest** is interest earned on both the original principal **and** on the interest that has been added to it. It makes money grow faster.
  - Same ₹1,000 at 6% compounded annually:
    - Year 1: ₹1,000 × 1.06 = ₹1,060
    - Year 2: ₹1,060 × 1.06 = ₹1,123.60
    - Year 3: ₹1,123.60 × 1.06 = ₹1,191.02
  - The extra ₹11.02 over simple interest is the “interest on interest.”

The power of compounding is why Einstein reportedly called it the eighth wonder of the world. In finance, we always use compound interest.

### Future Value and Present Value

- **Future Value (FV)** tells you what an amount invested today will grow to at a given interest rate after a certain time.
  
  \[
  FV = PV \times (1 + r)^n
  \]
  where \(PV\) = present value (today’s sum), \(r\) = interest rate per period, \(n\) = number of periods.

- **Present Value (PV)** works in reverse: it determines how much a future sum is worth today.
  
  \[
  PV = \frac{FV}{(1 + r)^n}
  \]
  
  This process of calculating present value is called **discounting**, and the interest rate \(r\) used is the **discount rate**. The discount rate reflects both the time value and the risk of the future cash flow.

> **Analogy:** If you think of money as a living tree, future value is seeing how tall the tree will grow after *n* years of sunshine (interest). Present value is figuring out how big the seed must be today to grow into a tree of a certain size in the future.

### Annuities: When Cash Flows are Equal

An **annuity** is a series of equal payments made at regular intervals—like a monthly loan instalment, yearly insurance premium, or bond interest coupon. There are two main types:

1. **Ordinary Annuity:** Payments occur at the **end** of each period. Example: a loan repayment where you make a payment at the end of each month.
2. **Annuity Due:** Payments occur at the **beginning** of each period. Example: rent paid on the first of the month.

Because money has time value, an annuity due is always worth more than an identical ordinary annuity: each payment comes one period earlier.

- **Present Value of an Ordinary Annuity** (what a stream of equal end‑of‑period payments is worth today):
  
  \[
  PV_{\text{ordinary}} = PMT \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}}{r} \right]
  \]
  
- **Future Value of an Ordinary Annuity** (what the stream grows to after the last payment):
  
  \[
  FV_{\text{ordinary}} = PMT \times \left[ \frac{(1 + r)^n - 1}{r} \right]
  \]

For an **annuity due**, simply multiply the corresponding ordinary annuity formula by \((1 + r)\):
\[
PV_{\text{due}} = PV_{\text{ordinary}} \times (1 + r)
\]
\[
FV_{\text{due}} = FV_{\text{ordinary}} \times (1 + r)
\]

*Example:* You plan to deposit ₹5,000 at the end of each year for 4 years in an account earning 7% p.a.  
FV (ordinary annuity) = \(5{,}000 \times \frac{(1.07)^4 - 1}{0.07} = 5{,}000 \times 4.4399 \approx ₹22{,}200\).  
If deposits were made at the *beginning* of each year, FV = \(22{,}200 \times 1.07 \approx ₹23{,}754\).

### Compounding More Than Once a Year: Effective Interest Rate

So far we assumed interest compounds once a year. But many loans and investments compound quarterly, monthly, or even daily. If a bank advertises a **nominal annual rate** of 12% compounded quarterly, the true return is higher than 12% because interest gets added to the principal four times a year.

When compounding occurs \(m\) times a year:
- The periodic rate = \(r_{\text{nominal}} / m\)
- Total number of periods = \(n \times m\)

The **Future Value** formula becomes:
\[
FV = PV \times \left(1 + \frac{r_{\text{nom}}}{m}\right)^{n \times m}
\]

The real annual return is captured by the **Effective Annual Rate (EAR)**:
\[
EAR = \left(1 + \frac{r_{\text{nom}}}{m}\right)^m - 1
\]

*Example:* For a nominal rate of 12% compounded quarterly, \(m = 4\):
\[
EAR = \left(1 + \frac{0.12}{4}\right)^4 - 1 = (1.03)^4 - 1 = 1.1255 - 1 = 0.1255 \text{ or } 12.55\%.
\]

Thus, a loan quoting 12% compounded quarterly actually costs 12.55% per year. Regulators often require lenders to disclose the EAR (or equivalent) so borrowers can compare offers on a like‑for‑like basis. This idea links directly to wealth maximization: valuing an investment requires using the correct, often effective, discount rate to reflect the true opportunity cost of funds.

## Chapter Summary

- **Finance** is the management of money—raising, allocating, and controlling funds—in a way that maximises economic value.
- Firms perform four core financial functions: financing, investing, liquidity management, and risk management.
- The proper goal of a firm is **wealth maximization** (maximising the market value of shareholders’ equity), not profit maximization. Wealth maximization accounts for the size, timing, and risk of all future cash flows.
- The separation of ownership and management creates an **agency problem**, which can be mitigated through performance‑based pay, board oversight, takeover threats, and transparent reporting.
- **Financial markets** channel funds from savers to borrowers, provide liquidity, and facilitate price discovery and risk sharing.
- **Money markets** deal in short‑term (<1 year) instruments; **capital markets** deal in long‑term securities.
- **Primary markets** issue new securities; **secondary markets** trade existing ones among investors.
- **Financial intermediaries** (banks, mutual funds) transform maturity, reduce risk, and lower costs, while **brokers** simply match buyers and sellers.
- The **time value of money** states that a rupee today is worth more than a rupee later because it can be invested to earn interest. **Compound interest** generates growth on both principal and accumulated interest.
- **Future Value** compounds a present sum forward; **Present Value** discounts a future sum back to today. **Annuities** are equal periodic payments; an ordinary annuity pays at period‑end, an annuity due at period‑beginning.
- When compounding occurs more than once a year, the **Effective Annual Rate (EAR)** captures the true annual cost or return, and must be used for accurate valuation.
