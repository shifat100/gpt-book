## Risk and Return

### The Two Sides of Every Investment
In Chapter 1, you learned that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. But there is a catch: not every future dollar is equally certain. A government bond might promise a near-guaranteed payment, while a new tech start-up could either double your money or vanish entirely. This chapter is about the relationship between **risk** and **return**—the fundamental trade-off that lies at the heart of every financial decision. You will learn how to measure risk, why some people shy away from it while others embrace it, how diversification can reduce risk without sacrificing return, and how financial economists price risky assets using the famous Capital Asset Pricing Model. Let’s begin.

---

## 1. What Are Risk and Return?

- **Return** is the reward you earn for investing your money. It is usually expressed as a percentage of the amount invested. The simplest measure is the **holding period return (HPR)**, which captures both income (dividends, interest) and capital gains (price change) over a period.

  $$HPR = \frac{(\text{Ending Price} - \text{Beginning Price}) + \text{Income}}{\text{Beginning Price}}$$

  *Example:* You buy a stock for $50, receive a $2 dividend, and sell it for $55 after one year.  
  $HPR = \frac{(55 - 50) + 2}{50} = \frac{7}{50} = 0.14 = 14\%$.

- **Risk** is the uncertainty that the actual return will differ from what you expected. In finance, risk is not just the possibility of loss—it is the *variability* of possible outcomes. An investment whose returns can swing wildly carries more risk than one that delivers a steady, predictable return.

Think of return as the speed of a car and risk as the bumpiness of the road. A highway might get you to your destination at a steady 60 mph (low risk, moderate return), while an off-road trail may occasionally rocket you forward but also jolt you to a crawl—or send you into a ditch. As an investor, you must decide how bumpy a ride you can tolerate.

## 2. Measuring Risk with Probability Distributions

To make risk tangible, we use **probability distributions**. A probability distribution lists all possible outcomes and their likelihoods. From this, we calculate two key numbers:
- **Expected Return** ($\bar{r}$ or $E(r)$): the probability-weighted average of all possible returns.
- **Standard Deviation** ($\sigma$): a measure of how much the actual returns can deviate from the expected return—the higher the standard deviation, the greater the risk.

The expected return is given by:
$$E(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i \times r_i)$$
- $p_i$ = probability that outcome $i$ occurs.
- $r_i$ = return in outcome $i$.
- $n$ = total number of possible outcomes.

The variance ($\sigma^2$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$) are:
$$\sigma^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \times (r_i - E(r))^2$$
$$\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$$

**Step‑by‑Step Example 2.1 – Calculating Expected Return and Risk**
You are considering investing in Company X. After analyzing the economy, you estimate three possible scenarios for the coming year:

| Scenario | Probability ($p_i$) | Return ($r_i$) |
|----------|---------------------|----------------|
| Boom     | 0.20                | 30%            |
| Normal   | 0.50                | 10%            |
| Recession| 0.30                | -10%           |

First, calculate the expected return:
$E(r) = (0.20 \times 0.30) + (0.50 \times 0.10) + (0.30 \times (-0.10))$  
$E(r) = 0.06 + 0.05 - 0.03 = 0.08 = 8\%$

Now, compute the variance and standard deviation:
1. For Boom: $(0.30 - 0.08)^2 = (0.22)^2 = 0.0484$. Multiply by $0.20$ → $0.00968$.
2. For Normal: $(0.10 - 0.08)^2 = (0.02)^2 = 0.0004$. × $0.50$ → $0.00020$.
3. For Recession: $(-0.10 - 0.08)^2 = (-0.18)^2 = 0.0324$. × $0.30$ → $0.00972$.

Sum: $\sigma^2 = 0.00968 + 0.00020 + 0.00972 = 0.0196$.  
$\sigma = \sqrt{0.0196} = 0.14 = 14\%$.

Interpretation: Company X offers an expected return of 8%, but the typical deviation from that average is 14 percentage points. There’s a substantial chance of a negative return, making this a risky investment.

## 3. Attitudes Toward Risk

Not all investors view risk the same way. Broadly, people fall into three categories:

- **Risk‑averse** – The typical investor. A risk‑averse person requires a higher expected return to accept additional risk. For the same return, they prefer a certain outcome over an uncertain one. Most of us are risk‑averse: you would demand a premium to invest in a shaky start‑up rather than in a safe government bond.
- **Risk‑neutral** – Indifferent to risk. Such an investor judges an opportunity solely by its expected return, completely ignoring the variability. They would accept a fair coin flip for $100 just as happily as a certain $50, because the expected payoff is the same.
- **Risk‑seeking** – Enjoys risk for its own sake, sometimes accepting a lower expected return for the thrill of gambling. Casino gamblers and some speculative traders exhibit risk‑seeking behavior.

Finance theory assumes that investors are **risk‑averse** because it matches reality: people pay for insurance, diversify, and require higher yields on risky bonds. This demand for compensation is the **risk premium**—the extra return above a “risk‑free rate” that an investor expects for bearing risk. Understanding risk aversion is key to the models that follow.

## 4. The Magic of Diversification

“Don’t put all your eggs in one basket.” This ancient wisdom is the essence of **diversification**—spreading your investments across different assets to reduce overall risk without necessarily lowering expected return. Diversification works because the prices of different stocks (or asset classes) do not move perfectly together; when one zigs, another may zag.

### How Diversification Reduces Risk
Total risk of an individual stock consists of two parts:
- **Unsystematic (diversifiable) risk** – Risk unique to a specific company or industry (e.g., a strike, a product recall). You can eliminate it by holding a portfolio of many stocks.
- **Systematic (market) risk** – Risk that affects the entire market (e.g., interest rate changes, wars, recessions). Even the most diversified portfolio retains this risk.

A well‑diversified portfolio of 20–30 randomly selected stocks eliminates almost all unsystematic risk, leaving only systematic risk.

**Measuring Portfolio Risk for Two Assets**  
When you combine two stocks, A and B, the expected return of the portfolio is simply the weighted average of the individual expected returns:
$$E(r_p) = w_A E(r_A) + w_B E(r_B)$$
where $w_A, w_B$ are the portfolio weights (proportions) summing to 1.

The portfolio variance, however, depends critically on the **correlation** ($\rho_{AB}$) between the two assets’ returns. The formula:
$$\sigma_p^2 = w_A^2 \sigma_A^2 + w_B^2 \sigma_B^2 + 2 w_A w_B \rho_{AB} \sigma_A \sigma_B$$
- $\sigma_A, \sigma_B$ = standard deviations of A and B.
- $\rho_{AB}$ = correlation coefficient between returns on A and B, ranging from –1 to +1.

If $\rho$ is less than +1, the portfolio standard deviation is *less* than the weighted average of the individual standard deviations. The lower the correlation, the greater the risk reduction. At $\rho = -1$, you could theoretically build a risk‑free portfolio.

**Step‑by‑Step Example 4.1 – Portfolio Risk and Diversification**  
You invest 60% in Stock A and 40% in Stock B.  
Stock A: expected return = 12%, standard deviation = 20%.  
Stock B: expected return = 8%, standard deviation = 15%.  
The correlation coefficient between their returns is 0.30.

First, portfolio expected return:  
$E(r_p) = (0.60 \times 0.12) + (0.40 \times 0.08) = 0.072 + 0.032 = 0.104 = 10.4\%$.

Now, portfolio variance:  
$\sigma_p^2 = (0.60)^2(0.20)^2 + (0.40)^2(0.15)^2 + 2(0.60)(0.40)(0.30)(0.20)(0.15)$  
$= (0.36 \times 0.04) + (0.16 \times 0.0225) + 2(0.60)(0.40)(0.30)(0.03)$  
$= 0.0144 + 0.0036 + 2 \times 0.60 \times 0.40 \times 0.30 \times 0.03$  
First compute the product inside: $0.60 \times 0.40 = 0.24$, then $0.24 \times 0.30 = 0.072$, then $0.072 \times 0.03 = 0.00216$. Twice that is $0.00432$.  
So $\sigma_p^2 = 0.0144 + 0.0036 + 0.00432 = 0.02232$.  
$\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.02232} \approx 0.1494 = 14.94\%$.

Notice: The weighted average of the individual standard deviations would be $0.60 \times 20\% + 0.40 \times 15\% = 12\% + 6\% = 18\%$, but the actual portfolio risk is only 14.94%. Diversification reduced risk due to the imperfect correlation.

## 5. The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

Now we arrive at the most famous model linking risk and return. The **Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)** tells us what return a risky asset *should* offer, given its systematic risk and the prevailing risk‑free rate.

### The Big Idea
Because unsystematic risk can be eliminated by diversification, the market does not reward investors for bearing it. The only risk that matters for an individual asset’s required return is **systematic risk**, measured by **beta ($\beta$)**.

- **Beta** measures an asset’s sensitivity to movements in the overall market portfolio (often the S&P 500).  
  - $\beta = 1$: The asset moves in sync with the market.  
  - $\beta > 1$: The asset is more volatile than the market (aggressive stocks).  
  - $\beta < 1$: The asset is less volatile (defensive stocks).  
  - $\beta = 0$: The asset is risk‑free (no correlation with the market).

### The CAPM Equation (Security Market Line)
$$E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i \times \bigl(E(R_m) - R_f\bigr)$$
- $E(R_i)$ = required (expected) return on asset $i$.
- $R_f$ = risk‑free rate (e.g., yield on 3‑month Treasury bills).
- $\beta_i$ = beta of asset $i$.
- $E(R_m)$ = expected return on the market portfolio.
- $E(R_m) - R_f$ = **market risk premium**—the extra return investors demand for taking on average market risk.

The equation draws a straight line called the **Security Market Line (SML)**. Any asset priced correctly will lie on this line.

**Step‑by‑Step Example 5.1 – Using CAPM**  
Suppose the risk‑free rate is 3%, the expected market return is 10%, and Stock ABC has a beta of 1.4. What is the required return on ABC?

Market risk premium = $10\% - 3\% = 7\%$.  
$E(R_{ABC}) = 3\% + 1.4 \times 7\% = 3\% + 9.8\% = 12.8\%$.

Interpretation: Because ABC is 40% more volatile than the market, investors need a 12.8% return to compensate for that extra systematic risk.

### What CAPM Teaches Us
- There is a linear relationship between systematic risk (beta) and expected return. Higher beta = higher required return.
- The risk premium of an asset depends only on beta, not on its total standard deviation.
- In equilibrium, all assets and portfolios plot on the SML. If an asset plots above the line (offering too high a return for its beta), investors will buy it, pushing its price up and its expected return down, until it rests on the line. This is the market’s self‑correcting mechanism.

## 6. Efficient Financial Markets

Can you beat the market by picking undervalued stocks or forecasting trends? The **efficient market hypothesis (EMH)** says: not consistently.

An **efficient financial market** is one in which security prices fully reflect all available information at any point in time. If markets are efficient, no amount of analysis—technical or fundamental—can systematically generate returns above the risk‑adjusted market average. Price changes are random because new information is, by definition, unpredictable.

### Three Forms of Market Efficiency
1. **Weak‑form efficiency** – Current prices reflect all past trading information (historical prices, volume). Therefore, technical analysis (looking at charts and patterns) cannot consistently beat the market.
2. **Semi‑strong form efficiency** – Prices instantly reflect all publicly available information (financial statements, economic reports, news). Neither technical analysis nor fundamental analysis can yield excess returns.
3. **Strong‑form efficiency** – Prices reflect *all* information, including private or insider information. Even corporate insiders cannot earn abnormal profits.

Most financial economists accept that developed markets like the NYSE are reasonably semi‑strong efficient, though not perfectly so. The practical implication: for most investors, a low‑cost, passively managed index fund that simply tracks the market is the best strategy. Trying to time the market or pick “hot stocks” tends to be a losing game after fees and taxes.

### The Random Walk
If markets are efficient, stock prices follow a **random walk**—tomorrow’s price change is unrelated to today’s. A coin flip is just as good at predicting short‑term movements. This is why financial news often feels like noise: by the time you hear the news, it is already baked into the price.

Of course, active managers exist, and some outperform in any given year. But the EMH argues that such performance is mostly luck, not skill, and rarely persists. The debate continues, but the evidence strongly suggests that consistent above‑market returns are extremely rare.

## Chapter Summary
- **Return** is the percentage gain (or loss) from an investment. **Risk** is the uncertainty of those returns, measured by standard deviation.
- Using **probability distributions**, we calculate expected return $E(r)$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. Standard deviation captures total risk.
- Investors are generally **risk‑averse**, requiring a **risk premium** above the risk‑free rate to bear risk.
- **Diversification** reduces risk by combining assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated. Unsystematic risk can be eliminated; only **systematic risk** (market risk) remains in a well‑diversified portfolio.
- **Beta ($\beta$)** measures an asset’s systematic risk relative to the market.
- The **Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)** defines the required return: $E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i [E(R_m) - R_f]$. All assets should plot on the Security Market Line.
- **Efficient market hypothesis (EMH)** states that prices reflect all available information, making it nearly impossible to consistently beat the market. Three forms: weak, semi‑strong, and strong.
