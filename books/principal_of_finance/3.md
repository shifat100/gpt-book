# Chapter 3  
## Capital Budgeting

### The Art of Choosing Tomorrow’s Wealth
Imagine you are the captain of a ship setting out to discover new trade routes. You have limited gold to build and outfit one vessel, but many possible islands to explore—some rumored to be rich with spices, others barren. Which voyage do you fund? Firms face the same challenge when they commit millions to a new factory, a product line, or a research project. **Capital budgeting** is the process of deciding which long‑term investments are worth undertaking. It is the compass that guides a company’s strategic direction, ensuring scarce capital is allocated to projects that create the most value for shareholders. In this chapter, you’ll learn how to estimate a project’s cash flows, avoid common traps like sunk costs, and use four powerful tools—Payback, Net Present Value, Internal Rate of Return, and Profitability Index—to make smart investment choices. Let’s set sail.

---

## 1. What Is Capital Budgeting?

**Capital budgeting** is the systematic process of identifying, evaluating, and selecting long‑term investment projects that are expected to generate cash flows over several years. These projects typically require a large upfront cash outlay (the *capital expenditure*, or CAPEX) and promise returns well into the future. Examples include:
- Purchasing a new piece of machinery
- Opening a new store or factory
- Developing a new software platform
- Launching a major marketing campaign
- Acquiring another company

Because these decisions involve huge sums and are often irreversible, getting them right—or wrong—can make or break a firm. A good capital budgeting system asks two questions: **“How much cash will this project generate, and when?”** and **“Is that enough to compensate for the risk and the money tied up?”**

---

## 2. The Capital Budgeting Process

Formal capital budgeting follows a logical sequence of steps:

1. **Idea Generation** – Investment proposals originate from all parts of the organization: the plant manager spotting a worn‑out machine, the R&D team devising a new product, the marketing department pushing for geographic expansion, or even a government mandate to reduce emissions. A culture that encourages innovation fuels this pipeline.

2. **Project Screening and Proposal** – Raw ideas are filtered for strategic fit and minimum size. Those that survive are developed into formal **project proposals** that spell out the rationale, estimated costs, expected cash flows, and key risks. A proposal might be a one‑page summary for a small equipment replacement or a thick document for a multi‑billion‑dollar acquisition.

3. **Financial Analysis and Evaluation** – This is where the number‑crunching happens. The financial manager applies the techniques you’ll learn in this chapter (NPV, IRR, etc.) to determine whether the project earns more than the firm’s cost of capital.

4. **Decision and Approval** – Based on the analysis, senior management or the board gives the green light (or red light). Sometimes projects must compete for a limited capital budget, and only the best are funded.

5. **Implementation and Monitoring** – Once approved, the project is executed, and its actual performance is tracked against the original projections. This feedback loop improves future estimates and holds managers accountable.

Throughout this process, the quality of the cash‑flow forecast is the single most important ingredient. “Garbage in, garbage out” is especially true in capital budgeting.

---

## 3. Generating Investment Ideas and Project Proposals

Where do good investment ideas come from? Broadly, projects can be classified into a few categories:
- **Replacement projects** – Replacing aging or obsolete equipment to maintain current operations or reduce costs. These are the most routine and least risky.
- **Expansion projects** – Expanding existing product lines or markets to grow revenues. Moderate risk.
- **New products or markets** – Entering uncharted territory. High potential reward, but high risk.
- **Mandatory or regulatory projects** – Safety, environmental, or legal compliance investments. They may not be profitable but are necessary to continue operating.

Firms often set up a cross‑functional capital expenditure committee to solicit, review, and prioritize proposals. In large companies, a **project proposal** typically contains:
- A clear description and strategic justification
- Estimated initial investment (including installation, training, and working capital needs)
- Projected incremental cash flows over the project’s life
- Sensitivity analysis (what if sales are 10% lower?)
- The recommended decision based on NPV/IRR.

The quality of the proposal determines whether the idea ever sees the light of day. As future managers, your ability to construct and critique such proposals will be indispensable.

## 4. Determining Relevant Cash Flows: After‑Tax, Incremental, and Nothing Else

The hardest part of capital budgeting is not the math—it’s estimating the right numbers. The golden rule is: **only incremental cash flows matter**. An incremental cash flow is one that occurs if and only if the project is accepted; it is the difference between the firm’s cash flows with the project and without it.

### 4.1 Sunk Costs and Opportunity Costs
Two special cost concepts trip up beginners:

- **Sunk Cost** – A cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of whether the project goes ahead. *Sunk costs are irrelevant.*  
  *Example:* Last year you spent $500,000 on market research for a potential new product. That money is gone. When deciding whether to launch the product today, do not include that $500,000. It will not change whether you go forward or not.

- **Opportunity Cost** – The cash that an asset could generate if it were used in its next best alternative. *Opportunity costs are very relevant.*  
  *Example:* Your firm owns a vacant factory that could be sold for $2 million. If you use it for a new project, you forgo the $2 million sale proceeds. The $2 million is an opportunity cost that should be charged to the project, even though no cash is actually spent.

**Analogy:** Imagine you have a non‑refundable movie ticket (sunk cost). If a better‑paying job shift comes up for that evening, you should ignore the ticket price when deciding whether to work—the money is already spent. But you must consider the foregone wage (opportunity cost) if you go to the movie.

### 4.2 Building the After‑Tax Incremental Operating Cash Flow
Project cash flows have three components:
- **Initial investment** (CAPEX plus change in net working capital)
- **Operating cash flows** over the project’s life
- **Terminal cash flow** (salvage value, recovery of working capital)

The annual **after‑tax operating cash flow (OCF)** can be calculated with this widely used formula:

$$OCF = (\Delta S - \Delta C - \Delta D)(1 - T) + \Delta D$$

or equivalently:

$$OCF = (\Delta S - \Delta C)(1 - T) + T \times \Delta D$$

Where:
- $\Delta S$ = change in sales revenue (revenues from the project)
- $\Delta C$ = change in cash operating costs (excluding depreciation)
- $\Delta D$ = change in depreciation expense (a non‑cash charge that affects taxes)
- $T$ = marginal corporate tax rate
- $T \times \Delta D$ = the **depreciation tax shield**—the tax saving created by the depreciation deduction.

The second form is especially intuitive: the project’s after‑tax operating profit **plus** the tax shield on depreciation. Since depreciation itself is not a cash outflow, we add it back after computing the tax effect.

### 4.3 Comprehensive Example: Estimating Cash Flows for a New Machine

**Step‑by‑Step Example 4.1 – OCF and Terminal Cash Flow**  
*Situation:* SuperDrill Inc. is considering buying a new automated drill press for $100,000. It will be depreciated straight‑line to zero over its 5‑year useful life. The machine is expected to boost annual sales by $60,000 and reduce annual cash operating costs by $20,000 (treated here as a net positive cash effect; to match the formula, we’ll say $\Delta S = 60,000$ and $\Delta C = -20,000$, meaning costs drop by $20,000, which is equivalent to an increase in pre‑tax profit). However, it’s simpler to work with the aggregate: incremental revenue of $60,000 and operating costs that are $20,000 lower than before, so net cash operating improvement = $80,000 per year? Wait, the problem statement can be clearer: I’ll set incremental sales = $60,000, incremental cash operating expenses = $20,000 (these are new costs). So the pre‑tax profit before depreciation = $60,000 - $20,000 = $40,000. Straight‑line depreciation = $100,000 / 5 = $20,000 per year. Tax rate = 40%. The machine will be sold at the end of year 5 for an estimated $10,000 (salvage value). There is no additional working capital needed.

Calculate the annual after‑tax operating cash flow for years 1‑5, and the total cash flow in year 5 including the after‑tax salvage value.

**Solution using the tax‑shield approach:**
1. First, $\Delta S - \Delta C = 60,000 - 20,000 = 40,000$ (net cash flow before depreciation and tax).
2. $T = 0.40$, $\Delta D = 20,000$.
3. $OCF = (40,000)(1 - 0.40) + 0.40 \times 20,000 = 40,000 \times 0.60 + 8,000 = 24,000 + 8,000 = \$32,000$ per year for years 1 to 5.

Thus, the machine throws off a steady $32,000 of cash each year from operations.

**Terminal cash flow in year 5:**  
At the end of year 5, the machine is sold for $10,000. Since its book value is zero (fully depreciated), the entire $10,000 is a taxable gain. Tax on gain = $10,000 \times 0.40 = \$4,000$. The after‑tax salvage cash inflow = $10,000 - 4,000 = \$6,000$.  
Therefore, total cash flow in year 5 = $OCF$ + after‑tax salvage = $32,000 + 6,000 = \$38,000$.

The complete cash flow timeline:  
Year 0: –$100,000 (initial investment)  
Year 1‑4: $32,000 each  
Year 5: $38,000

We’ll use this cash flow stream to demonstrate all evaluation methods.

## 5. Project Evaluation and Selection Methods

With the cash flows in hand, we now need tools to decide whether the machine is a good investment. We will cover four methods: Payback Period, Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR), and Profitability Index (PI). All assume the firm’s required rate of return (discount rate) is **10%**.

### 5.1 Payback Period (PBP)
**Definition:** The payback period is the number of years it takes for the project’s cumulative cash inflows to recover the initial investment.

**Decision Rule:** Accept if the payback is less than a predetermined cutoff period (e.g., 3 years). Otherwise reject.

**Calculation** for the drill press:
- Initial investment: $100,000
- Year 1: cumulative = $32,000
- Year 2: cumulative = $64,000
- Year 3: cumulative = $96,000
- Year 4: cumulative = $128,000

The payback occurs during year 4. At the end of year 3, we need $4,000 more. Year 4’s cash flow is $32,000. So:
$$Payback = 3 + \frac{4,000}{32,000} = 3.125 \text{ years}$$

**Pros and cons:** Payback is simple and highlights liquidity risk. But it ignores the time value of money, cash flows after the payback, and the project’s overall profitability. A project could pay back quickly and then fizzle out, or take longer but generate enormous later gains.

### 5.2 Net Present Value (NPV)
**Definition:** NPV is the sum of the present values of all expected cash flows, discounted at the project’s required rate of return ($k$). It measures how much value the project adds to the firm in today’s dollars.

$$NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + k)^t}$$
Where $CF_t$ is the net cash flow at time $t$, $n$ is the project’s life, and $k$ is the discount rate (cost of capital).

**Decision Rule:**  
- If NPV > 0 → Accept (the project earns more than the required return).
- If NPV < 0 → Reject.
- If NPV = 0 → Indifferent.

**Example 5.2 – NPV of the Drill Press (k = 10%)**
We discount each cash flow:
- Year 0: –$100,000 (already in present value)
- Years 1‑4: an annuity of $32,000 for 4 years at 10%
- Year 5: $38,000 discounted back 5 years

Present value of annuity years 1‑4:  
$PV = 32,000 \times \frac{1 - (1.10)^{-4}}{0.10} = 32,000 \times 3.169865 \approx 101,435.68$

Present value of year 5: $38,000 \times (1.10)^{-5} = 38,000 \times 0.620921 \approx 23,595.00$

Total PV of inflows ≈ $101,435.68 + 23,595.00 = \$125,030.68$

NPV = $125,030.68 - 100,000 = \$25,030.68$

Since NPV is strongly positive, the project should be accepted. It creates over $25,000 of extra wealth for shareholders.

### 5.3 Internal Rate of Return (IRR)
**Definition:** IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV of the project exactly zero. It represents the project’s expected annualized rate of return.

$$0 = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + IRR)^t}$$

**Decision Rule:**  
- If IRR > required rate of return ($k$) → Accept.
- If IRR < $k$ → Reject.

Because the IRR equation is a polynomial, solving it by hand often requires trial and error and **linear interpolation**.

**Example 5.3 – Finding IRR by Interpolation for the Drill Press**
We need to find the rate $r$ such that:  
$-100,000 + \frac{32,000}{(1+r)^1} + \frac{32,000}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{32,000}{(1+r)^3} + \frac{32,000}{(1+r)^4} + \frac{38,000}{(1+r)^5} = 0$

**Step 1: Try a lower rate, say 15%.**  
$PVIFA(15\%,4) = \frac{1 - (1.15)^{-4}}{0.15} = 2.854978$  
PV of years 1‑4: $32,000 \times 2.854978 = 91,359.30$  
PV of year 5: $38,000 \times (1.15)^{-5} = 38,000 \times 0.497177 = 18,892.73$  
Total PV = 91,359.30 + 18,892.73 = 110,252.03  
NPV at 15% = 110,252.03 – 100,000 = **+$10,252.03** → positive.

**Step 2: Try a higher rate, say 20%.**  
$PVIFA(20\%,4) = \frac{1 - (1.20)^{-4}}{0.20} = 2.588735$  
PV of years 1‑4: $32,000 \times 2.588735 = 82,839.52$  
PV of year 5: $38,000 \times (1.20)^{-5} = 38,000 \times 0.401878 = 15,271.36$  
Total PV = 82,839.52 + 15,271.36 = 98,110.88  
NPV at 20% = 98,110.88 – 100,000 = **–$1,889.12** → negative.

Since NPV changes sign between 15% and 20%, IRR lies in that interval. We interpolate linearly:

$$IRR \approx r_L + (r_U - r_L) \times \frac{NPV_L}{NPV_L - NPV_U}$$
Where:
- $r_L$ = lower rate (15%), $NPV_L$ = +10,252.03
- $r_U$ = upper rate (20%), $NPV_U$ = –1,889.12

Plug in:  
$IRR = 0.15 + (0.20 - 0.15) \times \frac{10,252.03}{10,252.03 - (-1,889.12)}$  
$= 0.15 + 0.05 \times \frac{10,252.03}{12,141.15}$  
$= 0.15 + 0.05 \times 0.8444$  
$= 0.15 + 0.04222 = 0.19222 \approx 19.22\%$

The IRR of about 19.22% is well above the 10% cost of capital, confirming the project is a good one. **Caution:** Interpolation gives an approximation; the true IRR is 19.15%, but this is close enough for decision‑making. For mutually exclusive projects, always rely on NPV rather than IRR, because IRR can mislead when cash flow patterns differ.

### 5.4 Profitability Index (PI)
**Definition:** The profitability index (also called benefit‑cost ratio) is the ratio of the present value of future cash flows to the initial investment.

$$PI = \frac{PV \text{ of future cash flows}}{\text{Initial Investment}} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+k)^t}}{|CF_0|}$$

**Decision Rule:**  
- If PI > 1 → Accept (each dollar invested returns more than a dollar in present value terms).
- If PI < 1 → Reject.

**Example 5.4 – PI for the Drill Press**  
We already computed the PV of future cash inflows (years 1‑5) at 10%: $125,030.68$.  
Initial investment = $100,000.

$$PI = \frac{125,030.68}{100,000} = 1.2503$$

PI = 1.25, meaning for every $1.00 invested, the project returns $1.25 in present value. This index is particularly helpful when capital is rationed and you need to rank projects by “bang for the buck.”

### Comparing the Methods: A Quick Guide
| Method | Considers TVM? | Uses All Cash Flows? | Decision Rule |
|--------|----------------|----------------------|---------------|
| Payback Period | No | No | Shortest payback below cutoff |
| NPV | Yes | Yes | NPV > 0 → Accept |
| IRR | Yes | Yes | IRR > k → Accept |
| PI | Yes | Yes | PI > 1 → Accept |

For independent projects, NPV, IRR, and PI will generally give the same accept/reject signal. But when projects are mutually exclusive or have unconventional cash flows, **NPV is the gold standard** because it directly measures the wealth added to the firm and never gives conflicting rankings due to scale or timing.

## Chapter Summary
- **Capital budgeting** is the process of evaluating long‑term investment opportunities. It involves generating ideas, screening proposals, performing financial analysis, making decisions, and monitoring outcomes.
- Only **incremental cash flows** matter. **Sunk costs** are ignored; **opportunity costs** must be included.
- **After‑tax operating cash flow** is calculated as $( \Delta S - \Delta C)(1 - T) + T \times \Delta D$. Depreciation provides a tax shield that boosts cash flow.
- Four major evaluation techniques:
  - **Payback period**: Quick and simple, but ignores time value and later cash flows.
  - **Net Present Value (NPV)**: The dollar amount of value created. Accept if NPV > 0.
  - **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**: The discount rate that makes NPV zero. Accept if IRR > cost of capital. Can be approximated by linear interpolation.
  - **Profitability Index (PI)**: Measures value per dollar invested. Accept if PI > 1.
- NPV is theoretically superior because it directly aligns with the goal of maximizing shareholder wealth.
