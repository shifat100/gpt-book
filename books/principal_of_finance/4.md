## Cost of Capital

### The Price Tag on Money
Imagine you are building your dream house. You buy bricks, timber, and glass, each with a different price. To know whether you can afford the house, you need the average cost of all the materials combined. In finance, when a firm builds a new project, it uses money from different sources—loans, preferred shares, and common equity. Each source has a specific cost, and the firm must earn at least the blended, or average, of those costs to keep all its investors happy. This blended rate is the **cost of capital**. It is the minimum return a company must earn on its investments to create value rather than destroy it. In this chapter, you’ll learn how to compute the cost of each financing piece and combine them into the **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)**—the single most important number in corporate finance. Once you know the WACC, you can return to Chapter 3 and use it to discount project cash flows, deciding once and for all which projects are worth pursuing.

---

## 1. Value Creation and the Hurdle Rate
Recall the goal of a firm: maximize shareholder wealth. A project creates wealth if its **return exceeds the return required by those who provided the capital**. The cost of capital is therefore a **hurdle rate**—the minimum acceptable rate of return on an investment. Think of it as the pace a marathon runner must beat to win the race. If the runner goes slower, she loses; if she goes faster, she wins. Similarly, if a project’s internal rate of return (IRR) exceeds the WACC, it generates positive NPV and adds value. If it falls short, it destroys value.

Thus, the cost of capital is the critical link between the firm’s financing decisions and its investment decisions. Estimating it correctly is essential—overestimate it and you’ll reject good projects; underestimate it and you’ll accept bad ones. We now dissect the three building blocks: debt, preferred stock, and equity.

---

## 2. The Cost of Debt (K<sub>d</sub>)
Debt comes in many forms—bank loans, bonds, notes—but the principle is the same: lenders provide money today in exchange for promised interest payments and repayment of principal. The **before‑tax cost of debt** is the yield to maturity (YTM) on new debt the firm would issue today. It reflects the current market interest rate for the firm’s risk level.

However, interest expenses are tax‑deductible. This creates a tax shield that reduces the true cost to the firm. What matters is the **after‑tax cost of debt**:

$$K_{d,\text{after-tax}} = K_d \times (1 - T)$$

Where:
- $K_d$ = before‑tax cost of debt (YTM on new bonds, expressed as a decimal)
- $T$ = marginal corporate tax rate

**Why the tax adjustment?**  
For every dollar of interest paid, the firm saves $T in taxes. The net cost of borrowing one dollar is therefore the interest minus the tax saving. This makes debt the cheapest source of capital—a benefit often called the **interest tax shield**.

**Example 2.1 – After‑Tax Cost of Debt**  
EcoFabrics Ltd. can issue 10‑year bonds with an 8% coupon rate, sold at their face value of $1,000. Since the bonds are sold at par, the coupon rate equals the YTM, so $K_d = 8\%$. The firm’s tax rate is 30%.  
After‑tax cost of debt:  
$K_{d,\text{after-tax}} = 0.08 \times (1 - 0.30) = 0.08 \times 0.70 = 0.056 = 5.6\%$

EcoFabrics effectively pays only 5.6% for its borrowed money. This low cost explains why companies with stable cash flows often use a lot of debt.

---

## 3. The Cost of Preferred Stock (K<sub>p</sub>)
**Preferred stock** is a hybrid—it carries a fixed dividend but has no maturity date, much like a perpetual bond. Preferred dividends are not tax‑deductible, so there is no tax adjustment. The cost of preferred stock is simply the annual dividend divided by the net proceeds from selling a share:

$$K_p = \frac{D_p}{P_p}$$

Where:
- $D_p$ = annual preferred dividend per share
- $P_p$ = net market price per share (current market price minus any flotation costs, i.e., the cash the company actually receives when issuing a new preferred share)

Because preferred dividends are typically fixed, the formula is a straightforward perpetuity.

**Example 3.1 – Cost of Preferred Stock**  
GlobalTech’s preferred shares pay a $5 annual dividend and are currently trading at $80 per share. If the company issues new preferred shares, it expects flotation costs of $2 per share, so net proceeds are $78.  
$K_p = \frac{5}{78} \approx 0.0641 = 6.41\%$

If GlobalTech were not issuing new shares and simply using existing preferred stock, flotation costs are irrelevant, and the cost would be $5 / 80 = 6.25\%$. In most WACC calculations, we use current market prices without flotation costs because the WACC represents the ongoing cost of the firm’s capital structure, not the cost of raising a marginal dollar. But when evaluating a specific new project that requires issuing new preferred shares, the flotation‑adjusted cost is the right one to use. For simplicity, we’ll stick to market prices without flotation costs in the WACC examples that follow.

## 4. The Cost of Common Equity (K<sub>e</sub>) – The CAPM Approach
Common shareholders are the true owners of the firm. They bear the highest risk because they are paid last—after lenders and preferred shareholders—and their returns depend on the firm’s residual profits. Consequently, equity is the most expensive source of capital. Two common approaches exist to estimate the cost of equity: the **Dividend Growth Model** and the **Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)**. We will focus on the CAPM, as introduced in Chapter 2.

The CAPM tells us that the required return on a stock equals the risk‑free rate plus a risk premium that depends on the stock’s systematic risk (beta):

$$K_e = R_f + \beta \times (E(R_m) - R_f)$$

Where:
- $R_f$ = risk‑free rate
- $\beta$ = beta, the measure of the stock’s systematic risk
- $E(R_m)$ = expected return on the market portfolio
- $E(R_m) - R_f$ = market risk premium (MRP)

We now examine each input in detail.

### 4.1 The Risk‑Free Rate (R<sub>f</sub>)
The risk‑free rate is the return on an investment considered to have zero default risk. In practice, we use the yield on long‑term government bonds—usually the 10‑year Treasury bond—because the time horizon for corporate projects is long‑term. This rate sets the baseline: even the safest firm must offer at least this return to attract equity investors. As market interest rates change, the risk‑free rate changes, directly affecting the cost of capital.

*Example:* If the 10‑year U.S. Treasury bond yields 4.0%, then $R_f = 4\%$.

### 4.2 The Market Risk Premium (MRP)
The market risk premium, $E(R_m) - R_f$, is the extra return investors demand for taking on the risk of the stock market as a whole, over and above the risk‑free rate. It is not directly observable; we estimate it from historical data. In the United States, the long‑run average market risk premium has been around 5% to 7%. Many practitioners use 5.5% or 6% as a forward‑looking estimate.

For our examples, we will assume an MRP of **6%**. So, if $R_f = 4\%$ and MRP = 6%, the expected market return $E(R_m) = 10\%$.

### 4.3 Beta ($\beta$) – The Measure of Systematic Risk
As you learned in Chapter 2, beta gauges how much a stock moves with the overall market.
- $\beta = 1$: The stock moves in lockstep with the market.
- $\beta > 1$: The stock is more volatile than the market (e.g., technology firms).
- $\beta < 1$: The stock is less volatile (e.g., utility companies).

Betas are typically estimated by regressing a stock’s historical returns against market returns. For our purposes, we can use published betas from financial data services. A firm’s beta reflects its business risk and financial leverage.

### 4.4 Putting It All Together: Cost of Equity Example
**Example 4.1 – Cost of Equity for Apex Manufacturing**  
Apex Manufacturing has a beta of 1.3. The 10‑year government bond yield is 3.8%, and the market risk premium is estimated at 5.5%. What is Apex’s cost of equity?

$K_e = 3.8\% + 1.3 \times (5.5\%) = 3.8\% + 7.15\% = 10.95\%$

Apex must earn at least 10.95% on its equity‑financed projects to compensate its shareholders for the risk they bear. Note that this is higher than the cost of debt or preferred stock—equity investors demand a premium for being the first to absorb losses.

## 5. The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)
A firm typically uses a mix of debt, preferred stock, and equity to finance its operations. The **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)** is the overall, blended cost of that mix. It represents the minimum return the firm must generate on its existing assets to satisfy all its investors. The WACC is the appropriate discount rate for projects that have the same risk as the firm’s overall operations.

The formula for WACC is:

$$WACC = w_d \times K_{d}(1 - T) + w_p \times K_p + w_e \times K_e$$

Where:
- $w_d$ = proportion of debt in the capital structure (market value weight)
- $w_p$ = proportion of preferred stock
- $w_e$ = proportion of common equity
- $w_d + w_p + w_e = 1$
- $K_{d}(1-T)$ = after‑tax cost of debt
- $K_p$ = cost of preferred stock
- $K_e$ = cost of equity (CAPM)

**Important: Use market values, not book values.** Book values are historical and may differ greatly from current market realities. The weights should reflect the firm’s target capital structure—the mix it plans to maintain over the long run.

### Step‑by‑Step Comprehensive Example 5.1 – Calculating WACC

**Situation:** ClearView Optics has the following capital structure, based on current market values:
- **Debt:** $15 million in bonds, currently yielding 7% to maturity.
- **Preferred Stock:** $5 million in preferred shares, paying a $4 annual dividend, trading at $50 per share.
- **Common Equity:** $30 million. ClearView’s beta is 1.1. The risk‑free rate is 4.2%, and the market risk premium is 5.8%.
- Corporate tax rate: 25%.

Let’s compute the WACC step by step.

**Step 1: Calculate the cost of each component.**
- After‑tax cost of debt: $K_{d}(1-T) = 7\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 7\% \times 0.75 = 5.25\%$.
- Cost of preferred stock: $K_p = \frac{D_p}{P_p} = \frac{4}{50} = 0.08 = 8\%$.
- Cost of equity using CAPM: $K_e = 4.2\% + 1.1 \times 5.8\% = 4.2\% + 6.38\% = 10.58\%$.

**Step 2: Determine market value weights.**
Total market value = Debt + Preferred + Equity = $15 + $5 + $30 = $50 million.
- $w_d = \frac{15}{50} = 0.30$ (30%)
- $w_p = \frac{5}{50} = 0.10$ (10%)
- $w_e = \frac{30}{50} = 0.60$ (60%)

**Step 3: Compute WACC.**
$$WACC = 0.30 \times 5.25\% + 0.10 \times 8\% + 0.60 \times 10.58\%$$
$$= 1.575\% + 0.80\% + 6.348\% = 8.723\%$$

ClearView Optics’ WACC is approximately **8.72%**. This means that to create value, any new project of average risk must earn a return greater than 8.72%. If the firm uses this rate to discount the project cash flows from Chapter 3, a positive NPV tells management that the project beats the cost of capital and adds to shareholder wealth.

### What the WACC Tells Us
- If a firm earns exactly its WACC on all assets, the stock price remains unchanged; all investors get their required return, but no extra value is created.
- A return above WACC increases the stock price; a return below WACC decreases it.
- WACC is also a benchmark for evaluating divisional performance and for setting the hurdle rate in incentive compensation.

### A Note on Taxes and Capital Structure
Notice how debt’s after‑tax cost is lower due to the tax shield. This seems to suggest that using more debt lowers WACC, which is true up to a point. However, as debt increases, financial risk rises, equity beta climbs, and the cost of equity goes up, eventually offsetting the tax benefit. The optimal capital structure balances these forces—a topic for an advanced finance course. For now, we take the firm’s target weights as given.

## Chapter Summary
- The **cost of capital** is the minimum return a firm must earn to satisfy its debt holders, preferred shareholders, and common shareholders. It is the hurdle rate used in capital budgeting.
- **Value creation** occurs when an investment earns more than the cost of capital (NPV > 0).
- The **after‑tax cost of debt** equals the yield to maturity times $(1 - T)$, reflecting the interest tax shield.
- **Cost of preferred stock** is the dividend yield: $K_p = D_p / P_p$, with no tax adjustment.
- **Cost of equity** via CAPM: $K_e = R_f + \beta \times (E(R_m) - R_f)$. The risk‑free rate, beta, and market risk premium are essential inputs.
- **WACC** blends the component costs using market value weights: $$WACC = w_d K_d (1-T) + w_p K_p + w_e K_e$$
- WACC is the appropriate discount rate for projects of average risk and ties directly to the goal of maximizing shareholder wealth.
