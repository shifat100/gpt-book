# Chapter 5  
## Short‑Term Financing

### The Bridge Between Paying and Getting Paid
Imagine you own a small bakery. You buy flour and sugar today, pay your bakers on Friday, but your corporate clients pay their invoices 30 days later. There is a gap—a stretch of time when cash flows out before it flows in. How do you keep the ovens running? **Short‑term financing** is the set of tools that bridges that gap. It provides the cash needed to meet day‑to‑day obligations until customer payments arrive. Some sources of short‑term money arise automatically as you do business; others require a trip to the bank or a negotiation with an investor. This chapter explores the full menu: trade credit, bank loans, commercial paper, and factoring. By the end, you will be able to compare their true costs and choose the cheapest, most sensible way to keep your business humming.

---

## 1. Spontaneous vs. Negotiated Sources
Short‑term financing falls into two broad buckets:

- **Spontaneous sources** – These arise naturally from the firm’s ordinary operations. You don’t negotiate a separate contract; they simply appear as you buy, hire, and sell. The two main types are **trade credit** (from suppliers) and **accruals** (unpaid wages and taxes).
- **Negotiated sources** – These require a specific agreement with a lender or financial institution. Examples include **bank loans**, **commercial paper**, and **factoring**. They are used when spontaneous financing is not enough to cover the cash gap.

We start with spontaneous sources because they are the cheapest and easiest to obtain—and often the most misunderstood.

---

## 2. Spontaneous Sources of Short‑Term Financing

### 2.1 Trade Credit: The Silent Lifeline
**Trade credit** is the credit extended by a supplier to a buyer when goods or services are delivered but payment is deferred. It appears on the buyer’s balance sheet as **accounts payable**. For most small and medium‑sized businesses, trade credit is the single largest source of short‑term funds.

When you receive an invoice that says “Net 30,” the supplier is lending you the purchase price for 30 days—interest‑free, as long as you pay on time. Think of it as a free, short‑term loan bundled with every order. But not all trade credit terms are created equal.

#### 2.1.1 Cash on Delivery (COD) and Cash Before Delivery (CBD)
These are the strictest terms a supplier can impose. They offer zero financing.

- **Cash Before Delivery (CBD)** – The buyer must pay the full amount before the goods are shipped. This eliminates the seller’s credit risk entirely. It is common in international trade with unknown buyers or for customized products that cannot be resold.
- **Cash on Delivery (COD)** – The buyer pays when the goods arrive, often to the carrier. The seller still receives cash quickly but bears the risk that the buyer might refuse delivery. Financing is limited to the transit time.

From the buyer’s perspective, COD and CBD provide no breathing room. They are essentially cash purchases. As a financial manager, you would always prefer to negotiate credit terms if possible, freeing up cash for other uses.

#### 2.1.2 Credit Terms: With or Without a Cash Discount
Most business‑to‑business transactions use open‑account credit with standard terms. These terms specify two things: a **cash discount** (if any) and a **net period**.

A typical term is **2/10, net 30**. It reads:
- **2% discount** if the invoice is paid within **10 days**.
- Otherwise, the **full amount is due in 30 days**.

**Why offer a discount?** The supplier wants cash sooner. To incentivize early payment, they dangle a price reduction. From the buyer’s perspective, skipping the discount is a deliberate choice—and an expensive one. Let’s quantify exactly how expensive.

**The Cost of Forgoing the Cash Discount**
When you decide not to pay within the discount period, you effectively borrow the discounted price for the extra days. The cost can be annualized with this formula:

$$\text{Cost of forgoing discount} = \frac{D}{100 - D} \times \frac{365}{N_d - D_d}$$

Where:
- $D$ = discount percentage (e.g., 2 for 2%)
- $N_d$ = net period in days (final due date)
- $D_d$ = discount period in days

The fraction $\frac{D}{100 - D}$ is the interest paid for each dollar of “loan,” and $\frac{365}{N_d - D_d}$ annualizes it.

**Example 2.1 – Cost of Skipping a Discount**
Your supplier offers terms of 2/10, net 30. You forgo the discount and pay on day 30. What is the effective annual cost?

- $D = 2$, so $\frac{2}{100 - 2} = \frac{2}{98} \approx 0.020408$.
- Extra credit days = $30 - 10 = 20$ days. Annualization factor = $365 / 20 = 18.25$.

$$\text{Cost} = 0.020408 \times 18.25 = 0.3724 = 37.24\%$$

That is a staggering **37.24%** annual interest rate! If your firm has cash available or can borrow from a bank at, say, 8%, it is vastly better to take the discount. The message: never skip a cash discount unless you have no alternative—the implicit interest is usually astronomical.

**When no discount is offered** (e.g., “Net 30”), the supplier is providing interest‑free financing for the full credit period. There is no penalty for paying on the final due date, so you should take the full 30 days.

#### 2.1.3 Seasonal Dating
Some industries, like toys, swimwear, or agricultural equipment, are highly seasonal. A manufacturer might want to produce year‑round even though sales happen in a concentrated selling season. **Seasonal dating** is a special trade credit arrangement that shifts the net period and discount period to coincide with the buyer’s selling season.

For example, a toy retailer might receive terms of **2/10, net 30, dating 120 days**. This means:
- The 10‑day discount period and the 30‑day net period do **not** start on the invoice date.
- They start **120 days after the invoice date**.

So, the retailer can take the 2% discount if it pays by day 130, or pay the full amount by day 150. This allows the retailer to stock up for the holiday season in August, sell the goods in November and December, and pay the supplier in January—after cash has come in from customers.

Seasonal dating is a powerful form of spontaneous financing that aligns cash outflows with cash inflows. The supplier effectively finances the buyer’s inventory build‑up. For the buyer, it reduces the need for bank loans during the pre‑season period.

#### 2.1.4 Trade Acceptance
Sometimes a supplier wants a more formal promise than an open‑account invoice. A **trade acceptance** is a draft (a written order) drawn by the seller on the buyer, requiring the buyer to pay a specified sum at a future date. The buyer acknowledges the obligation by signing the draft, converting it into a negotiable instrument. The seller may then hold it until maturity or, if they need cash sooner, sell (discount) it at a bank.

Trade acceptances are particularly common in international trade and in transactions where the buyer’s creditworthiness is less certain. By accepting the draft, the buyer legally commits to pay, giving the supplier a stronger claim than a simple accounts payable entry. However, for the buyer, a trade acceptance is still a form of trade credit—the payment is deferred, but the formal nature may impose slightly higher administrative costs.

### 2.2 Accruals: The Free, Automatic Loan
**Accruals** are expenses that have been incurred but not yet paid. The two largest categories are:
- **Wages** – Employees work for a week or a month before receiving their paycheck. During that period, the firm owes them wages but still holds the cash.
- **Taxes** – Income taxes, sales taxes, and payroll taxes are paid periodically (quarterly, monthly). Between the time the liability is recorded and the payment is made, the firm uses the government’s money.

Accruals are completely interest‑free and require no negotiation. They expand automatically as the firm grows. However, they are not fully controllable—labor laws and tax deadlines impose strict payment dates. Still, they provide a permanent cushion of cost‑free financing that every firm should factor into its cash planning.

## 3. Negotiated Sources of Short‑Term Financing

When spontaneous financing is exhausted, a firm must actively seek external funds. These negotiated sources involve explicit contracts, interest costs, and sometimes collateral.

### 3.1 Bank Loans
Bank lending is the most common negotiated source. Short‑term bank loans typically mature in one year or less and come in three main forms:

- **Line of credit** – An informal, revocable agreement allowing the firm to borrow up to a specified limit at any time. Interest is charged only on the amount actually borrowed. Lines of credit must usually be “cleaned up” (paid down to zero) for some period each year to prove the borrowing is genuinely short‑term.
- **Revolving credit agreement** – A legally binding commitment from the bank to lend up to a limit, often for a fee (a commitment fee) on the unused portion. Unlike a line of credit, the bank cannot cancel the agreement if the firm’s condition deteriorates.
- **Transaction loan** – A one‑time, fixed‑amount loan for a specific purpose, such as financing a large inventory purchase. Terms are negotiated individually.

**The Cost of a Bank Loan**
The cost depends on the quoted interest rate and any additional requirements, especially **compensating balances**.

A compensating balance is a minimum deposit that the borrower must maintain at the bank, usually expressed as a percentage of the loan amount. It reduces the cash the borrower can actually use. If the required balance is higher than what the firm would normally hold, the effective cost of borrowing rises.

**Effective annual rate with a compensating balance (assuming simple interest):**

$$EAR = \frac{\text{Interest}}{\text{Usable funds}} = \frac{r \times L}{L \times (1 - CB)} = \frac{r}{1 - CB}$$

Where:
- $r$ = stated annual interest rate (decimal)
- $L$ = loan amount
- $CB$ = compensating balance requirement (as a decimal fraction of the loan)

**Example 3.1 – Cost with Compensating Balance**
Precision Tools borrows $200,000 for one year at a stated rate of 7%, with a 15% compensating balance requirement. Precision Tools normally maintains a zero balance at the bank. What is the effective annual rate?

- Interest = $200,000 \times 0.07 = \$14,000$.
- Usable funds = $200,000 \times (1 - 0.15) = 200,000 \times 0.85 = \$170,000$.
- $EAR = \frac{14,000}{170,000} = 0.08235 = 8.24\%$.

The 7% stated rate becomes an 8.24% effective cost because the firm cannot touch $30,000 of the loan. If the firm already kept a positive balance at the bank, only the *additional* balance above normal would be considered a restriction.

**Discount loans** are even more expensive. If the bank deducts the interest in advance (a “discounted loan”), the usable funds are reduced by the interest itself:

$$EAR = \frac{r}{1 - r}$$
for a one‑year loan with no other fees. Example: $100,000 loan at 8% discounted, usable = $92,000, effective rate = $8,000 / 92,000 = 8.70%.

### 3.2 Commercial Paper
**Commercial paper** is an unsecured, short‑term promissory note issued by large, financially strong corporations. Maturities range from a few days to a maximum of 270 days (to avoid SEC registration requirements). It is sold at a discount from face value in the open market, directly to investors such as money market mutual funds.

Think of commercial paper as an IOU from a blue‑chip company. Because the issuers have excellent credit ratings, commercial paper is generally cheaper than bank loans. However, the market is impersonal: if a firm’s credit quality slips, it may not be able to roll over (reissue) its paper, leaving it suddenly without funds.

**Cost of Commercial Paper**
The cost is calculated similarly to a discount loan, annualized:

$$\text{Effective cost} = \frac{\text{Interest}}{\text{Net proceeds}} \times \frac{365}{\text{Days to maturity}}$$
Where Interest = Face value – Net proceeds.

**Example 3.2 – Commercial Paper Cost**
MegaFirm needs $980,000 in cash for 90 days. It issues commercial paper with a face value of $1,000,000, to be paid at maturity. (Thus net proceeds today are $980,000, and the discount is $20,000.)

- Interest cost (discount) = $20,000.
- Net proceeds = $980,000.
- Period = 90 days.

$$\text{Effective annual rate} = \frac{20,000}{980,000} \times \frac{365}{90} = 0.020408 \times 4.0556 \approx 0.0828 = 8.28\%$$

MegaFirm effectively pays 8.28% per annum for this short‑term financing. If a bank loan would cost 9%, commercial paper is the better choice—provided the firm can access the market.

Note: Some commercial paper is interest‑bearing, but the discount form is most common.

### 3.3 Factoring
**Factoring** is not a loan; it is the sale of a firm’s accounts receivable to a specialized financial institution called a **factor**. The factor buys the invoices at a discount and assumes the responsibility for collecting from the customers. Factoring thus provides immediate cash and, in some arrangements, transfers the credit risk of customer default.

Factoring can be **with recourse** (the seller must reimburse the factor for any uncollectible receivables) or **without recourse** (the factor absorbs the loss). Non‑recourse factoring is more expensive because the factor bears the credit risk.

The factor typically performs three services:
- Credit checking on potential customers.
- Bookkeeping and collection of receivables.
- Financing by advancing cash before maturity.

**Cost of Factoring**
The cost has two components: a **factoring fee** (usually a percentage of the invoice value, e.g., 1–3%) and **interest on advances** (if the factor provides cash before collection). The overall effective annual cost can be substantial.

**Example 3.3 – Factoring Cost**
Textile Designs Ltd. sells $150,000 of its receivables to a factor. The terms are:
- Factoring fee: 2.5% of invoice value.
- Reserve (holdback): 10% of invoice value (to cover returns and disputes).
- The factor advances the remaining funds immediately and charges 12% annual interest on the advance until the receivables are collected. Average collection period is 60 days.

1. Invoice value = $150,000.
2. Factoring fee = $150,000 \times 0.025 = \$3,750 (paid upfront or netted).
3. Reserve = $150,000 \times 0.10 = \$15,000 (held by factor, returned later net of any adjustments).
4. Advance = $150,000 – Reserve – Fee = $150,000 – $15,000 – $3,750 = \$131,250. (Alternatively, fee might be deducted from advance; we assume fee is settled separately or the advance is the amount after fee. Usually the advance is based on the face value minus reserve, and the fee is a separate cost. Let's compute step by step.)

A common structure: Factor pays upfront a percentage of the invoice (say 80% to 90%), then the rest (reserve) upon collection, minus fee. Let's use a simpler approach: The factor pays $131,250 now, and in 60 days, after collecting $150,000, will remit the remaining reserve less any charge‑offs, but here we’ll ignore charge‑offs. The interest on the advance is applied to the $131,250 for 60 days at 12%.

Interest on advance = $131,250 \times 0.12 \times \frac{60}{365} = 131,250 \times 0.12 \times 0.16438 \approx 131,250 \times 0.019726 = \$2,589.

Total cost to the seller = Factoring fee + Interest = $3,750 + $2,589 = \$6,339.

Net cash received today (advance) = $131,250 (we can think of this as the usable funds). The amount that will be received later (reserve) is $15,000, but that's tied up. The total financing provided is essentially the advance. To compute effective annualized cost of financing, we can relate total cost to the advance:

$$\text{Effective rate} = \frac{\text{Total cost}}{\text{Advance}} \times \frac{365}{60} = \frac{6,339}{131,250} \times 6.0833 \approx 0.0483 \times 6.0833 \approx 0.2938 = 29.38\%$$

That's a very high cost, reflecting the credit risk and collection services bundled into factoring. Factoring is generally more expensive than a bank line of credit, but it provides immediate cash and outsources credit management. Firms with weak internal collection systems or that value the credit protection of non‑recourse factoring often accept the higher cost.

## 4. Choosing the Right Short‑Term Financing Mix

A smart financial manager builds a layered strategy:
- Use **accruals** as perpetual, free financing.
- Stretch **trade credit** to the net date when no discount is offered, but always seize cash discounts if cheaper than bank borrowing.
- Maintain a reliable **bank line of credit** for predictable seasonal needs.
- Issue **commercial paper** if the firm is large and highly rated, to lower borrowing costs.
- Consider **factoring** when credit management becomes a burden or when immediate cash is critical.

The guiding principle remains the same as in previous chapters: find the lowest‑cost source that matches the timing and risk of the cash need. Every dollar saved in financing cost is a dollar that drops straight to the bottom line.

## Chapter Summary
- **Short‑term financing** bridges the gap between cash outflows and inflows. It arises either **spontaneously** from operations or is **negotiated** with lenders.
- **Trade credit** (accounts payable) is the largest spontaneous source. Terms may require payment before delivery (CBD), upon delivery (COD), or after delivery with possible cash discounts (e.g., 2/10, net 30).
- **Forgoing a cash discount** incurs an extremely high implicit cost: $\frac{D}{100-D} \times \frac{365}{N_d - D_d}$. Always take the discount if possible.
- **Seasonal dating** shifts the payment clock to align with the buyer’s selling season, providing extra interest‑free financing.
- **Trade acceptances** formalize credit obligations and can be discounted at banks.
- **Accruals** (unpaid wages and taxes) offer cost‑free financing but are limited.
- **Bank loans** include lines of credit, revolving agreements, and transaction loans. **Compensating balances** raise the effective cost: $EAR = \frac{r}{1 - CB}$.
- **Commercial paper** is unsecured, short‑term debt issued by top‑rated firms at a discount. Effective cost: $\frac{\text{Discount}}{\text{Proceeds}} \times \frac{365}{\text{days}}$.
- **Factoring** sells receivables to a factor, providing immediate cash but at a high total cost combining a fee and interest on advances.
- The goal is to blend these sources to minimize the overall cost of short‑term funds.

## Review and Practice Questions
1. **Trade Credit Cost**  
   A supplier offers terms of 3/15, net 45. If you pay on day 45 instead of day 15, what is the effective annual cost of forgoing the discount? (Use a 365‑day year.)

2. **Seasonal Dating**  
   A garden‑equipment manufacturer offers a retailer terms of 2/10, net 30, dating 150 days. The retailer places an order on February 1. By what date must the retailer pay to take the 2% discount? By what date is the full amount due? Explain why such terms benefit the retailer.

3. **Bank Loan Effective Rate**  
   You borrow $50,000 for one year at 9% stated interest, with a 20% compensating balance requirement that you would not normally hold. Compute the effective annual rate. What would the effective rate be if the loan were also discounted (interest deducted in advance)?

4. **Commercial Paper Cost**  
   XYZ Corporation issues $500,000 face value of 180‑day commercial paper for net proceeds of $485,000. Calculate the effective annual cost of this financing.

5. **Factoring Decision**  
   A firm sells $200,000 of receivables to a factor. The factor charges a 2% factoring fee, holds a 10% reserve, and advances the remainder at 10% annual interest. The average collection period is 45 days. Calculate the total dollar cost and approximate effective annual cost. Given that the firm’s bank line of credit costs 7%, would factoring be a good choice? Why might the firm still use it?
