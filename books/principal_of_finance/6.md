# Chapter 6  
## Mid‑Term Financing

### The Middle Path That Builds the Future
Short‑term loans keep the lights on for a few months; long‑term bonds build skyscrapers over decades. But what about the delivery truck that will serve you for five years, or the printing press that pays for itself in three? These assets sit in the middle—too durable for a 90‑day note, yet not quite “forever” like a factory. **Mid‑term financing** fills this gap, providing funds with maturities typically ranging from one to ten years. It is the quiet workhorse behind countless machines, vehicle fleets, and moderate expansions. In this chapter we explore the instruments that make mid‑term finance possible: term loans, chattel mortgages, conditional sales, and the wonderfully flexible world of leasing. By the end, you will know how to structure a loan, compare it to a lease, and recognize which tool fits which need. Let’s step into the middle.

---

## 1. Term Loans: The Installment Backbone

A **term loan** is a debt contract under which a borrower receives a lump sum of cash up front and repays it in a series of scheduled payments (installments) over one to ten years. It is the most common form of mid‑term financing for businesses of all sizes.

### Major Sources of Term Loans
- **Commercial Banks** – The largest source. Banks offer term loans for equipment, working capital, and moderate expansion, usually secured by the asset being purchased.
- **Insurance Companies** – Life insurance firms invest their massive, long‑term pools of premiums in term loans to larger corporations. They often prefer longer maturities (7–10 years) and charge slightly higher rates than banks.
- **Pension Funds** – Like insurers, pension funds seek stable, intermediate‑term returns and sometimes participate in private placements of term debt.
- **Government‑Sponsored Development Banks** – In many countries, development banks (e.g., Small Business Administration in the U.S., KfW in Germany) provide term loans to specific sectors—agriculture, small business, green energy—at subsidized rates.

### Features of a Term Loan
- **Amortization** – Most term loans are fully amortizing, meaning each payment covers both interest and principal, and the balance reaches zero by maturity.
- **Secured or Unsecured** – Banks often require collateral (a lien on equipment or real estate). Very strong firms can borrow unsecured.
- **Fixed or Floating Rate** – The interest rate may be fixed for the entire term or float with a benchmark like SOFR or the prime rate.
- **Restrictive Covenants** – The lender imposes conditions, such as maintaining a minimum current ratio or limiting further borrowing, to protect its position.

### Calculating the Installment Payment
The periodic payment ($PMT$) for a fully amortizing term loan is found using the present value of an annuity formula:

$$PMT = \frac{PV \times r}{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}}$$

Where:
- $PV$ = original loan amount (present value of the payments)
- $r$ = interest rate per payment period
- $n$ = total number of payment periods

**Step‑by‑Step Example 1.1 – Term Loan Amortization**
Precision Gears borrows $80,000 from a bank at 9% annual interest, repayable in equal annual installments over 6 years. Payments are made at the end of each year.

First, compute the annual payment:
$r = 0.09$, $n = 6$.  
Annuity present value factor:  
$PVIFA = \frac{1 - (1.09)^{-6}}{0.09} = \frac{1 - 0.596267}{0.09} = \frac{0.403733}{0.09} \approx 4.4859$  
$PMT = \frac{80,000}{4.4859} \approx \$17,831.63$

Now, let’s build the first two rows of the amortization schedule.

| Year | Beginning Balance | Payment | Interest (9%) | Principal | Ending Balance |
|------|-------------------|---------|---------------|-----------|----------------|
| 1    | $80,000.00        | 17,831.63 | 7,200.00      | 10,631.63 | 69,368.37      |
| 2    | 69,368.37         | 17,831.63 | 6,243.15      | 11,588.48 | 57,779.89      |

*Year 1:* Interest = $80,000 \times 0.09 = 7,200. Principal = 17,831.63 – 7,200 = 10,631.63.  
*Year 2:* Interest = $69,368.37 \times 0.09 = 6,243.15. Principal = 17,831.63 – 6,243.15 = 11,588.48.

Over six years, the company pays a total of $17,831.63 × 6 = $106,989.78, of which $26,989.78 is interest. Term loans give predictable cash outflows and build equity in the asset over time.

## 2. Secured Mid‑Term Financing: Chattel Mortgage and Conditional Sale

When a lender wants extra protection beyond the borrower’s promise, it takes a security interest in a specific asset. Two common structures are the chattel mortgage and the conditional sale.

### 2.1 Chattel Mortgage
A **chattel mortgage** is a loan secured by movable personal property—vehicles, machinery, equipment, or livestock. The borrower takes immediate ownership (title) and possession of the asset, but the lender places a lien on it. If the borrower defaults, the lender can seize and sell the asset to recover the debt.

*Think of a chattel mortgage like a home mortgage, but on a truck instead of a house.* You hold the pink slip, but the bank’s name is on it until the loan is paid off.

### 2.2 Conditional Sale
A **conditional sale** (also called a hire‑purchase agreement) is a contract under which the seller retains legal title to the asset until the buyer makes all payments. The buyer receives possession and use from day one, but ownership transfers only after the final installment is paid. If the buyer stops paying, the seller repossesses the asset. This arrangement is very common in equipment financing, especially for vehicles and industrial machinery.

**Key difference:**  
- *Chattel mortgage:* Title passes to the borrower immediately; lender holds a lien.  
- *Conditional sale:* Title stays with the seller; borrower is essentially a renter‑with‑a‑promise.

**Example 2.1 – Conditional Sale Payment**
A bakery buys a new oven priced at $40,000 under a conditional sale contract. It makes a 10% down payment ($4,000) and finances the remaining $36,000 over 4 years at 10% annual interest, with equal annual payments at year‑end. Title transfers upon the last payment.

Calculate the annual payment:  
$PV = 36,000$, $r = 0.10$, $n = 4$, $PVIFA = \frac{1 - (1.10)^{-4}}{0.10} = 3.169865$.  
$PMT = \frac{36,000}{3.169865} \approx \$11,357.06$.

The bakery’s total cost of the oven is $4,000 + 4 × $11,357.06 = $49,428.24. The conditional sale allowed it to acquire a vital asset without laying out the full $40,000 in cash today.

Both chattel mortgages and conditional sales are forms of **asset‑based financing** that let firms spread the cost of an asset over its useful life, matching cash outflows with the income the asset generates.

## 3. Leasing: Renting Tomorrow’s Tools

Leasing is a contract where the **lessor** (owner) gives the **lessee** (user) the right to use an asset for a specified period in return for a series of rental payments. It is one of the most versatile mid‑term financing techniques, because it can be tailored to shift tax benefits, keep debt off the balance sheet (to an extent), and preserve cash.

### 3.1 Classifying Leases: Operating vs. Financial
How a lease is accounted for—and how it impacts financial statements—depends on whether it is an **operating lease** or a **financial (capital) lease**.

| Feature | Operating Lease | Financial (Capital) Lease |
|---------|-----------------|---------------------------|
| Duration | Short‑term, often cancelable | Long‑term, typically non‑cancelable |
| Ownership risks | Remain with lessor | Transferred substantially to lessee |
| Accounting (lessee) | Rent expense on income statement; no asset/liability on balance sheet (for short leases under current rules, but IFRS 16 / ASC 842 changed this – for simplicity, assume traditional distinction) | Capitalize asset and record lease liability; depreciation and interest expense recognized |
| Typical example | Rental of office space, copiers | Lease of a production line for most of its useful life |

**Capital lease criteria (U.S. GAAP, simplified):** A lease is a capital lease if it meets *any one* of these tests:
1. Ownership transfers to lessee by end of lease term.
2. Lease contains a **bargain purchase option** (allows lessee to buy asset at below fair value).
3. Lease term is **75% or more** of the asset’s estimated economic life.
4. The **present value of minimum lease payments** equals or exceeds **90%** of the asset’s fair market value.

The 90% test is a classic present‑value calculation. It answers: are the lease payments, discounted at the lessee’s incremental borrowing rate (or the implicit rate, if known), enough to cover almost the entire value of the asset?

**Step‑by‑Step Example 3.1 – The 90% Test**
A manufacturer wants to lease a CNC machine with a fair market value of $100,000. The lease term is 5 years, with annual payments of $22,500 made at the end of each year. The machine has an economic life of 7 years, and title does not transfer. The lessee’s incremental borrowing rate is 8%. Is this an operating or a capital lease?

First, note that 5 years / 7 years = 71.4%, so the 75%‑of‑life test is *not* met. No ownership transfer or bargain purchase option is mentioned. So everything hangs on the 90% test.

Calculate the PV of the lease payments:  
$PMT = 22,500$, $n = 5$, $r = 0.08$.  
$PVIFA = \frac{1 - (1.08)^{-5}}{0.08} = 3.99271$.  
$PV = 22,500 \times 3.99271 = \$89,836.0$.

Compare to fair value: $89,836 / 100,000 = 89.8\%$. This is just below the 90% threshold. Therefore, none of the four criteria are met, and the lease is classified as an **operating lease**. The company will simply report $22,500 as a rental expense each year.

Had the annual payment been $23,000, the PV would have been $23,000 × 3.99271 = $91,832, or 91.8%, crossing the 90% line—making it a capital lease and requiring asset/liability recognition.

### 3.2 Traditional Lease (Direct Lease)
A **traditional lease**, often called a direct lease, is the simplest form: the lessor (a leasing company or manufacturer’s captive finance arm) buys an asset from a supplier and leases it directly to the end user. The lessee chooses the equipment, the lessor writes the check and delivers the asset, and the lessee makes periodic rental payments. There is no prior relationship between lessee and the asset—it’s a straightforward three‑party chain: supplier → lessor → lessee.

Traditional leases can be structured as operating or capital, depending on the terms. They are the “plain vanilla” of equipment finance and are ideal when a firm wants off‑balance‑sheet treatment (operating lease) or simply does not want to tie up cash in a purchase.

### 3.3 Sale and Leaseback
Imagine selling your own house to an investor and then renting it back. You get a lump sum of cash in your pocket, you continue living in the same home, but now you write a monthly rent check instead of a mortgage check. That is exactly what a **sale and leaseback** does for a business.

A firm that owns a valuable asset—a building, a fleet of trucks, a power plant—sells it to a leasing company (or financial institution) and simultaneously signs a long‑term lease to continue using the asset. The seller/lessee:
- Receives an immediate cash injection equal to the market value of the asset.
- Retains full operational use of the asset.
- Converts a fixed asset on its balance sheet into cash, often improving liquidity ratios.
- May benefit from tax deductions on lease payments (which are fully deductible) versus depreciation and interest on a loan.

Sale‑leasebacks are especially popular in real estate and transportation. They can unlock billions of dollars of capital without disrupting operations.

**Example 3.2 – Sale and Leaseback in Action**
MegaRetail owns its headquarters building, which is carried on the books at $8 million (net of depreciation) but has a current market value of $25 million. It arranges a sale‑leaseback: it sells the building to a pension fund for $25 million and leases it back for 20 years at an annual rent of $2.2 million.

Immediate effect: MegaRetail’s cash balance jumps by $25 million, and it can use that cash to expand stores or pay down expensive debt. The building remains its workplace. Over the lease term, rental payments reduce reported operating income, but the company avoids the ongoing depreciation and property tax expenses that the new owner now bears. The arrangement is a textbook example of using assets to generate liquidity without losing functionality.

### 3.4 Leveraged Lease
When the asset is enormously expensive—think a $150 million jetliner—a single lessor may not want to put up all the cash, and a simple direct lease becomes impractical. A **leveraged lease** solves this by introducing a third player: a lender.

In a leveraged lease:
- **Lessee** – Uses the asset and makes periodic lease payments.
- **Lessor (equity participant)** – Puts up a portion of the asset’s cost, typically 20% to 40%, and buys the asset. The lessor becomes the legal owner and claims the tax benefits (depreciation, interest deductions) on the *entire* asset.
- **Lender(s)** – Provide the remaining 60% to 80% of the purchase price in the form of non‑recourse debt, secured solely by the asset and the assignment of lease payments. If the lessee fails to pay, the lender can repossess the asset but cannot pursue the lessor for the shortfall.

The lease payments flow first to service the debt (interest and principal), and any surplus goes to the lessor as a return on its equity. Because the lessor uses a large amount of borrowed money, the return on its invested equity can be highly magnified—hence “leveraged.” The lessee, meanwhile, enjoys the use of a crucial asset and often a slightly lower lease rate than it would in a direct lease, because the lessor’s tax advantages are shared.

*Think of a leveraged lease like buying a house with 80% mortgage and 20% down payment, then renting it out. The bank gets its mortgage payments from the rent; you, as the landlord, keep what’s left and claim depreciation on the whole house even though you only put down 20%. Now replace the house with an Airbus A350.*

Leveraged leases are complex and expensive to structure, so they are reserved for major capital assets. They became a staple of airline and shipping finance, allowing carriers to acquire fleets while keeping debt off their corporate balance sheets (in the past) and preserving capital for operations.

## 4. Choosing the Right Mid‑Term Instrument

The decision among a term loan, a conditional sale, or a lease is not just about the lowest interest rate. Financial managers weigh:

- **Cash flow timing** – Loans require a down payment and principal repayment; leases spread costs evenly, often with no down payment.
- **Tax impact** – Interest and depreciation are deductible for owners; lease payments are fully deductible for lessees of operating leases. The party that can use tax shields most efficiently (the lessor or lessee) often determines the best structure.
- **Balance sheet effects** – Capital leases increase reported liabilities, which may violate debt covenants. Operating leases traditionally kept liabilities off the balance sheet (though new accounting standards now require most leases to be recognized, the distinction still matters for some ratios).
- **Residual value risk** – In a purchase or capital lease, the firm bears the risk that the asset will be worth little at the end of its life. In an operating lease, the lessor bears that risk.

The mid‑term financing toolbox is rich. A well‑managed firm uses a mix: term loans for straightforward equipment purchases, conditional sales when the seller’s financing is attractive, operating leases for assets that become obsolete quickly, and sale‑leasebacks to unlock trapped value.

## Chapter Summary
- **Mid‑term financing** (1–10 years) bridges the gap between short‑term credit and permanent capital. The most common instrument is the **term loan**, provided by banks, insurance companies, and development institutions, and repaid through amortizing installments.
- **Chattel mortgages** allow the borrower to own the asset while the lender holds a lien; **conditional sales** keep the title with the seller until full payment, offering strong security to the financier.
- **Leasing** is an alternative to borrowing that comes in multiple forms:
  - **Operating lease**: short‑term, cancelable, off‑balance‑sheet rental.
  - **Financial (capital) lease**: long‑term, transfers the risks and rewards of ownership; must be capitalized if it meets any one of four tests (including the 90% PV test).
  - **Traditional (direct) lease**: the lessor purchases the asset and leases it to the end user.
  - **Sale and leaseback**: a firm sells an owned asset and leases it back, converting fixed capital into cash.
  - **Leveraged lease**: a three‑party structure using non‑recourse debt to finance a large asset, with the lessor reaping tax benefits on a minimal equity investment.
- The choice among these tools depends on cash flow, taxes, balance sheet impact, and who bears the residual risk.
