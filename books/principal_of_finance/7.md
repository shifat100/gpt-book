# Chapter 7  
## Valuation of Long‑Term Securities

### What Is a Financial Asset Really Worth?
You walk into a used‑car showroom. The dealer has a price tag on the windshield, but you know the car’s true worth depends on how many miles it will run, how much gas it drinks, and what similar cars have sold for down the street. Financial securities—bonds, preferred stock, common stock—are no different. They have a price and an intrinsic value. The price is what you pay; the intrinsic value is what the future cash flows are worth in today’s dollars. **Valuation** is the art and science of bridging that gap. In this chapter, you will learn how to value the three main types of long‑term securities. By the end, you’ll be able to price a bond, calculate its yield, and determine what a share of stock is truly worth to a patient, long‑term investor. Let’s open the toolbox.

---

## 1. Book Value vs. Market Value: Two Very Different Stories

Before we value anything, we must distinguish two numbers that appear constantly in finance.

- **Book Value** is the accounting value of an asset or the entire firm, pulled straight from the balance sheet. It equals historical cost minus accumulated depreciation and any impairments. Book value is rooted in the *past*—what the firm paid for an asset years ago.
- **Market Value** is the price at which an asset can be bought or sold in a competitive marketplace today. It is forward‑looking, reflecting what investors believe the asset will generate in the future.

Think of your grandmother’s ring: the book value might be the $200 she paid in 1980, but its market value today could be $1,200 because gold prices soared and it’s now an antique. The same gulf exists for a company’s bonds and shares. Financial managers care far more about **market value** because that is what investors will pay or receive. When we “value” a security, we are estimating its *market value*, not its accounting book value.

---

## 2. Bond Basics: Promises Carved in Stone

A **bond** is a long‑term debt instrument under which a borrower (the issuer) promises to make specified payments to the lender (the bondholder) on specific dates. It is essentially an IOU with a very detailed instruction manual. Three core ingredients define any bond:

- **Face Value (Par Value, F)** – The principal amount that will be repaid at maturity. Typically $1,000 per bond in the U.S. corporate market.
- **Coupon Rate** – The annual interest rate the issuer promises to pay, stated as a percentage of face value. For example, a 6% coupon on a $1,000 face bond means $60 of interest every year.
- **Maturity (n)** – The number of years (or periods) until the face value is repaid. Bonds can have maturities from 2 years to 30 years or more; a “finite maturity” bond has a definite end date.

The **coupon payment (C)** is the cash the bondholder receives each period:
$$C = \text{Face Value} \times \text{Coupon Rate}$$

If a bond pays interest annually, the cash flow stream is a series of equal coupon payments for $n$ years, plus a lump‑sum repayment of the face value at the end. This structure makes bond valuation a straightforward application of present value.

---

## 3. Valuing a Bond with Finite Maturity

The price (value) of a bond is the present value of all the future cash flows it will generate—the coupons and the face value—discounted at the investor’s required rate of return ($k_d$), also called the market yield or yield to maturity for similar‑risk bonds.

For an **annual coupon bond**:

$$V_b = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1 + k_d)^t} + \frac{F}{(1 + k_d)^n}$$

Or, using the annuity formula:
$$V_b = C \times \frac{1 - (1 + k_d)^{-n}}{k_d} + \frac{F}{(1 + k_d)^n}$$

Where:
- $V_b$ = value (price) of the bond
- $C$ = annual coupon payment = Face Value × Coupon rate
- $k_d$ = required rate of return on debt (market discount rate)
- $n$ = number of years to maturity
- $F$ = face value (par value)

**Step‑by‑Step Example 3.1 – Annual Bond Valuation**
Maple Inc. issued a 5‑year bond with a face value of $1,000 and a coupon rate of 7%. Interest is paid annually. Today, the market’s required return on bonds of this risk is 9%. What is the bond’s current price?

Here, $F = 1000$, coupon rate = 7% → $C = 1000 \times 0.07 = \$70$, $n = 5$, $k_d = 0.09$.

Step 1: Present value of the coupons (annuity).  
$PV_{\text{coupons}} = 70 \times \frac{1 - (1.09)^{-5}}{0.09}$.  
$(1.09)^{-5} \approx 0.649931$, so $1 - 0.649931 = 0.350069$. Divide by 0.09 = $3.88965$.  
$PV_{\text{coupons}} = 70 \times 3.88965 = \$272.28$.

Step 2: Present value of face value.  
$PV_{\text{face}} = \frac{1000}{(1.09)^5} = 1000 \times 0.649931 = \$649.93$.

Step 3: $V_b = 272.28 + 649.93 = \$922.21$.

The bond trades at a **discount** (below face value) because its coupon rate (7%) is less than the market’s required return (9%). Investors are only willing to pay $922.21 for a bond that promises $1,000 in five years plus $70 per year. If the required return had been below the coupon rate, the bond would trade at a **premium**.

---

## 4. Bonds with Semiannual Compounding

Most bonds in the real world pay interest **semiannually**—half of the annual coupon every six months. This changes the valuation slightly because we must adjust the rate and number of periods to match the payment frequency.

When compounding is semiannual:
- Divide the annual coupon payment by 2: $\text{Semiannual coupon} = C/2$.
- Divide the annual required rate by 2: $k_d/2$.
- Double the number of years: $\text{Total periods} = 2n$.
- The face value is still $F$, received at the end.

The bond value formula becomes:

$$V_b = \sum_{t=1}^{2n} \frac{C/2}{\bigl(1 + \frac{k_d}{2}\bigr)^t} + \frac{F}{\bigl(1 + \frac{k_d}{2}\bigr)^{2n}}$$

**Step‑by‑Step Example 4.1 – Semiannual Bond Valuation**
Take the same Maple Inc. bond, but now assume it pays interest semiannually. Face value = $1,000, coupon rate = 7% (so annual C = $70), $n = 5$ years, $k_d = 9\%$ (nominal annual required return, compounded semiannually).

- Semiannual coupon = $70 / 2 = \$35$.
- Semiannual discount rate = $0.09 / 2 = 0.045$ (4.5% per half‑year).
- Number of half‑year periods = $5 \times 2 = 10$.

Step 1: PV of coupons (10‑period annuity).  
$PVIFA = \frac{1 - (1.045)^{-10}}{0.045}$.  
$(1.045)^{-10} \approx 0.643928$, so $1 - 0.643928 = 0.356072$. / 0.045 = $7.9127$.  
$PV_{\text{coupons}} = 35 \times 7.9127 = \$276.94$.

Step 2: PV of face value.  
$PV_{\text{face}} = \frac{1000}{(1.045)^{10}} = 1000 \times 0.643928 = \$643.93$.

Step 3: $V_b = 276.94 + 643.93 = \$920.87$.

The price is slightly lower than the annual‑pay bond ($922.21) because half of the coupon is received later (the second semiannual payment each year), and the face value discounting uses the same effective semiannual compounding. The small difference shows that frequency matters in pricing. Always adjust period length correctly.

## 5. Yield to Maturity (YTM): The Investor’s True Return

While the coupon rate tells you the promised interest, **yield to maturity (YTM)** tells you the actual annualized return an investor will earn if the bond is bought at its current market price and held until maturity, assuming all coupons are reinvested at that same yield. YTM is the discount rate that makes the present value of all future cash flows equal to the bond’s market price.

Mathematically, YTM is the value of $k_d$ that solves:

$$\text{Bond Price} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1 + YTM)^t} + \frac{F}{(1 + YTM)^n}$$

Because this equation cannot be solved algebraically for YTM with more than one cash flow, we use **trial and error** and **linear interpolation**, much like we did for IRR in Chapter 3.

**Approximation formula (useful for a quick estimate):**
$$YTM \approx \frac{C + \frac{F - P}{n}}{\frac{F + P}{2}}$$
Where $P$ = current market price of the bond. This gives a rough YTM; interpolation yields a more precise answer.

**Step‑by‑Step Example 5.1 – Finding YTM by Interpolation**
A bond with face value $1,000, coupon rate 8% (annual C = $80), and 6 years to maturity is currently selling for $920. What is its YTM?

We need to find the rate $r$ such that:  
$920 = 80 \times \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-6}}{r} + \frac{1000}{(1+r)^6}$.

**Step 1: Try a lower guess, say 10%.**  
PV of coupons: $80 \times PVIFA(10\%, 6) = 80 \times 4.35526 = 348.42$.  
PV of face: $1000 \times (1.10)^{-6} = 1000 \times 0.564474 = 564.47$.  
Total PV = $348.42 + 564.47 = 912.89$.  
NPV = $912.89 - 920 = -\$7.11$ (price too low, so YTM is a bit lower than 10%? Actually, if computed PV is lower than price, the YTM is lower than the guess? Wait: price = 920. If discount rate = 10%, PV = 912.89 < 920, meaning we need a lower discount rate to raise PV to 920. So YTM < 10%).

**Step 2: Try a lower rate, say 9%.**  
$PVIFA(9\%, 6) = \frac{1 - (1.09)^{-6}}{0.09} = 4.48592$ (approx).  
PV coupons = $80 \times 4.48592 = 358.87$.  
PV face = $1000 \times (1.09)^{-6} = 1000 \times 0.596267 = 596.27$.  
Total PV = $358.87 + 596.27 = 955.14$. This is well above 920, so YTM is between 9% and 10%.

**Step 3: Interpolate.**  
We have:  
At 9%, PV = 955.14 (NPV = +35.14 relative to price? Actually we want PV equal to 920, so we have:  
9% → 955.14  
10% → 912.89  
The target is 920.

We can set up interpolation based on PV directly:
$$YTM \approx r_L + (r_U - r_L) \times \frac{PV_L - \text{Price}}{PV_L - PV_U}$$
Where $r_L = 0.09$, $PV_L = 955.14$, $r_U = 0.10$, $PV_U = 912.89$.

$YTM = 0.09 + (0.10 - 0.09) \times \frac{955.14 - 920}{955.14 - 912.89}$  
$= 0.09 + 0.01 \times \frac{35.14}{42.25}$  
$= 0.09 + 0.01 \times 0.8317$  
$= 0.09 + 0.008317 = 0.098317 \approx 9.83\%$.

The bond offers a YTM of about 9.83%. Because the price is below face value ($920 < $1,000), the YTM is higher than the coupon rate (8%)—the discount provides extra return.

**Important:** Interpolation gives an approximation; the true YTM is 9.82% if solved precisely. For decision‑making, this is close enough.

## 6. Preferred Stock Valuation: The Perpetuity Stream

**Preferred stock** is a hybrid security that pays a fixed dividend forever (or as long as the company exists). Because there is no maturity date, preferred stock is valued as a **perpetuity**—an infinite stream of equal dividend payments.

The formula is beautifully simple:

$$V_p = \frac{D_p}{k_p}$$

Where:
- $V_p$ = value (price) of preferred stock
- $D_p$ = fixed annual dividend per share
- $k_p$ = required rate of return on preferred stock (market capitalization rate)

No tax adjustment is needed because preferred dividends are not tax‑deductible for the issuing firm.

**Step‑by‑Step Example 6.1 – Preferred Stock Valuation**
Atlas Power’s preferred shares pay an annual dividend of $4.50. Similar‑risk preferred stocks are currently yielding 6% in the market. What is the intrinsic value of one share?

$V_p = \frac{4.50}{0.06} = \$75.00$.

If the preferred shares are trading at $70, they are undervalued relative to the required return (they offer a higher yield). If trading at $80, they are overvalued. Because the dividend is fixed, the price moves inversely with changes in the required return $k_p$.

---

## 7. Common Stock Valuation: The Constant Growth Model

Common stock represents ownership. Its cash flows are dividends, which unlike preferred dividends, are not fixed—they can grow over time as the company retains earnings and reinvests them profitably. The most widely used valuation model for a mature, steadily growing company is the **Gordon Growth Model** (Constant Growth Model).

If dividends are expected to grow at a constant rate $g$ forever, the price today is:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{k_e - g}$$

Where:
- $P_0$ = current intrinsic value of the stock
- $D_1$ = expected dividend one year from now
- $k_e$ = required rate of return on equity (from CAPM or other method)
- $g$ = constant growth rate of dividends (must be less than $k_e$)

This equation is derived from the present value of a growing perpetuity. The next dividend $D_1$ is related to the most recent dividend $D_0$ by: $D_1 = D_0 \times (1 + g)$.

**Key assumptions:**
- Dividends grow at a constant rate forever.
- The required return is greater than the growth rate ($k_e > g$).
- The firm pays dividends, and they are the sole source of value for investors.

**Step‑by‑Step Example 7.1 – Constant Growth Valuation**
Bayside Corporation just paid a dividend of $2.00 per share ($D_0 = 2.00$). Dividends are expected to grow at 5% per year indefinitely. The required return on Bayside’s stock is 12%. What is the intrinsic value of the stock?

Step 1: Calculate next year’s dividend.  
$D_1 = 2.00 \times (1 + 0.05) = \$2.10$.

Step 2: Apply the constant growth model.  
$P_0 = \frac{2.10}{0.12 - 0.05} = \frac{2.10}{0.07} = \$30.00$.

If the stock is trading in the market at $28, it is undervalued (buy signal). If trading at $35, it is overvalued (sell/hold). The model is sensitive to $g$ and $k_e$, so managers and analysts spend considerable effort forecasting the sustainable growth rate.

### What If There’s No Dividend?
Many growth companies pay no dividends, reinvesting all earnings. The constant growth model would give $D_1 = 0$ and $P_0 = 0$, which is clearly wrong. In such cases, analysts use valuation based on free cash flows or earnings multiples, which are advanced topics. For the purpose of this course, we focus on dividend‑paying firms where the model shines.

## Chapter Summary
- **Book value** is historical cost from the balance sheet; **market value** is the current price determined by investors. Valuation aims to estimate market value.
- A **bond** is a long‑term debt instrument with a face value, coupon rate, and finite maturity. Its price is the present value of future coupon payments and the face value, discounted at the required return ($k_d$).
- For **semiannual coupons**, divide the annual coupon by 2, the annual rate by 2, and double the number of periods.
- **Yield to maturity (YTM)** is the discount rate that equates the bond’s price to the PV of its cash flows. It is found by trial and error and interpolation. YTM > coupon rate when the bond sells at a discount; YTM < coupon rate when at a premium.
- **Preferred stock** is valued as a perpetuity: $V_p = D_p / k_p$.
- **Common stock** using the constant growth model: $P_0 = D_1 / (k_e - g)$, where $D_1 = D_0 (1+g)$. The model requires $k_e > g$ and applies to dividend‑paying firms with steady growth.
- Valuation links the firm’s risk (through the discount rate) and growth prospects to a current price, guiding both investment and financing decisions.
