## Capital Structure and Theories

### The Recipe for Financing a Firm
Imagine you are baking a cake. You can use different proportions of flour and sugar—each proportion changes the taste and texture. Finance is no different. A firm can fund its assets with a mix of debt (borrowed money) and equity (owner’s money). That blend is its **capital structure**. The question that has challenged financial economists for decades is: does the recipe matter? Does mixing in more cheap debt make the firm more valuable, or does the added risk of bankruptcy spoil the batter? This chapter explores the great debate on capital structure. You will learn how to calculate earnings per share under different financing plans, find the break‑even point where choices are indifferent, and journey through three classic theories—Net Income, Net Operating Income, and the revolutionary Modigliani‑Miller propositions. Let’s enter the kitchen.

---

## 1. What Is Capital Structure?

**Capital structure** is the mix of long‑term sources of funds—primarily debt, preferred stock, and common equity—that a firm uses to finance its operations and growth. It answers the question: *How much of the firm’s assets are financed by borrowing, and how much by owners?*

- **Debt** comes with fixed interest payments and a promise to repay principal. It is cheaper because interest is tax‑deductible, but it increases the firm’s risk of default.
- **Equity** is more expensive but never has to be repaid. It cushions the firm in bad times.

A firm’s capital structure decision is essentially choosing its **financial leverage**—the degree to which fixed‑income securities (debt and preferred) are used in the financing mix. High leverage means more debt; low leverage means more equity.

**Analogy:** Think of a bicycle. Debt is like pedaling in a high gear: you go faster on flat roads but struggle uphill. Equity is the low gear: steady, less risky, but not as fast. The capital structure is your gear ratio, and the road is the business environment.

---

## 2. Optimum Capital Structure

If capital structure matters, then there must be an **optimum capital structure**—the one mix of debt and equity that **maximizes the market value of the firm** by minimizing its overall cost of capital (WACC). This is the “Goldilocks” point: not too much debt (high bankruptcy risk), not too little (forgoing the tax‑shield benefit), but just right.

At the optimum, the marginal benefit of an additional dollar of debt (tax savings) exactly equals the marginal cost (increased financial distress and higher equity costs). Graphically, as debt increases from zero, the WACC falls at first because cheap debt replaces expensive equity. But beyond a certain point, the cost of both debt and equity rise sharply due to financial risk, and WACC turns upward. The firm’s value follows an inverted bowl shape, peaking at the optimum.

Whether such a point exists—and if it can be found in practice—is the central question that the competing theories of capital structure try to answer.

## 3. Earnings Per Share (EPS) and Financing Choices

Before diving into theories, we need a tool to compare financing alternatives: the **EBIT–EPS analysis**. This practical method shows how different capital structures affect the earnings available to common shareholders.

### 3.1 The EPS Formula
Earnings per share (EPS) for a given capital structure is calculated as:

$$EPS = \frac{(EBIT - I) \times (1 - T) - D_p}{N}$$

Where:
- $EBIT$ = earnings before interest and taxes (operating income)
- $I$ = annual interest expense on debt
- $T$ = corporate tax rate
- $D_p$ = preferred stock dividends (if any)
- $N$ = number of outstanding common shares

If the firm uses only equity, $I = 0$ and $N$ is larger. If it uses debt, $I$ is positive but $N$ is smaller. The trade‑off: interest reduces net income but fewer shares divide it.

## 4. The Indifference Point (EBIT Break‑Even)

The **indifference point** is the level of EBIT at which EPS is exactly the same under two different financing plans. It helps managers decide when debt becomes beneficial. Below the indifference point, the plan with less fixed financial charges (equity) yields higher EPS. Above it, leverage pays off and the debt plan gives higher EPS.

To find the indifference EBIT, set the EPS equations equal and solve for $EBIT$:

$$\frac{(EBIT - I_1)(1 - T) - D_{p1}}{N_1} = \frac{(EBIT - I_2)(1 - T) - D_{p2}}{N_2}$$

Where subscripts 1 and 2 refer to the two plans. If no preferred dividends, the equation simplifies to:

$$\frac{(EBIT - I_1)(1 - T)}{N_1} = \frac{(EBIT - I_2)(1 - T)}{N_2}$$

Cancel $(1-T)$, cross‑multiply, and solve.

**Step‑by‑Step Example 4.1 – Indifference Point**
Using the TechVentures plans from Example 3.1: Plan E: $I_E = 0$, $N_E = 300,000$. Plan D: $I_D = 180,000$, $N_D = 200,000$. Tax rate = 40% (cancels out). Find the EBIT where EPS is equal.

$$\frac{EBIT - 0}{300,000} = \frac{EBIT - 180,000}{200,000}$$

Cross‑multiply:
$200,000 \times EBIT = 300,000 \times (EBIT - 180,000)$
$200,000\ EBIT = 300,000\ EBIT - 54,000,000,000$
Bring EBIT terms together:
$300,000\ EBIT - 200,000\ EBIT = 54,000,000,000$
$100,000\ EBIT = 54,000,000,000$
$EBIT = 540,000$

The indifference EBIT is $540,000. Let’s verify:
- Plan E EPS = $540,000 \times 0.6 / 300,000 = 324,000 / 300,000 = $1.08.
- Plan D EPS = $(540,000 - 180,000) \times 0.6 / 200,000 = 360,000 \times 0.6 / 200,000 = 216,000 / 200,000 = $1.08.

Interpretation: If TechVentures expects EBIT above $540,000, the debt plan produces higher EPS and is favored. If EBIT is expected below $540,000, the equity plan is safer and gives better EPS. This is the essence of financial leverage: it amplifies returns when times are good but hurts when times are bad.

## 5. The Net Income (NI) Approach

Now we move from practical tools to the grand theories. The **Net Income (NI) Approach**, championed by David Durand, argues that **capital structure matters**. A firm can lower its overall cost of capital and increase its market value by using more debt.

### Core Assumptions of the NI Approach
1. The cost of debt ($K_d$) and the cost of equity ($K_e$) remain constant regardless of the amount of leverage. There is no perceived increase in risk by investors as debt rises.
2. The overall cost of capital ($K_o$) is a weighted average of $K_d$ and $K_e$, and because $K_d < K_e$, increasing the proportion of debt (which is cheaper) reduces $K_o$.
3. The total market value of the firm ($V$) equals the sum of the market value of debt ($B$) and equity ($S$): $V = B + S$.
4. Firm value is computed by capitalizing the Net Income (earnings available to equity holders? Actually NI Approach defines value as: Value of equity = Net Income / Ke, then add debt. Net Income is earnings after interest and taxes, i.e., NI = (EBIT - I)(1 - T). So V = B + S, where S = NI / Ke, and B = I / Kd (assuming perpetual debt). Overall cost of capital Ko = EBIT(1 - T) / V? Wait, the NI approach often uses a simpler no‑tax scenario. I'll present a version without taxes for clarity, then can mention tax extension.

For simplicity, many textbooks present the NI approach with no taxes: Net operating income (EBIT) is divided between interest and net income. Value of equity = Net Income / Ke. Overall Ko = EBIT / V.

I'll adopt a no-tax model for NI and NOI, and later introduce taxes for MM. But I can note that the NI approach can incorporate taxes. I'll use a no-tax scenario to compare directly with NOI, then show MM with tax.

I'll state assumptions clearly: no corporate taxes, constant Ke and Kd, firm can increase value by using more debt because Ko falls.

**Example 5.1 – NI Approach**
Alpha Corp. has EBIT of $200,000. It currently has $0 debt, all equity. Ke = 10%. It is considering substituting debt with Kd = 6% for some equity. Let's compute overall cost and value under two scenarios: (a) no debt, (b) $400,000 debt. No taxes.

(a) No debt: Interest = 0, Net Income = EBIT = $200,000. Value of equity S = 200,000 / 0.10 = $2,000,000. Total value V = $2,000,000. Overall cost Ko = EBIT / V = 200,000 / 2,000,000 = 10% (equals Ke).

(b) Debt of $400,000 at 6%: Interest = $24,000. Net Income = 200,000 - 24,000 = $176,000. Ke is still 10% (per NI). Value of equity S = 176,000 / 0.10 = $1,760,000. Value of debt B = 400,000. Total V = 1,760,000 + 400,000 = $2,160,000. Ko = EBIT / V = 200,000 / 2,160,000 ≈ 9.26%.

Firm value increased from $2,000,000 to $2,160,000, and Ko fell. So the NI approach says: use 100% debt if possible, because value keeps rising. This is an extreme view, but it formed the starting point of the debate.

## 6. The Net Operating Income (NOI) Approach (Also Found as “NY Approach”)

The **Net Operating Income (NOI) Approach**, also promoted by Durand, takes the opposite stance: **capital structure does not matter**. The overall capitalization rate ($K_o$) is constant regardless of leverage. Any advantage of cheap debt is exactly offset by a rise in the cost of equity, leaving the total firm value unchanged. (The term “NY Approach” seen in some syllabi is a typographical error for NOI; we’ll refer to it as the NOI Approach.)

### Core Assumptions of the NOI Approach
1. The market capitalizes the firm as a whole at a constant rate $K_o$, based on business risk. So $V = EBIT / K_o$ is fixed.
2. The cost of debt $K_d$ is constant (at least initially).
3. The cost of equity $K_e$ increases linearly with leverage because shareholders perceive higher financial risk and demand a higher return.
4. The increase in $K_e$ exactly offsets the use of cheaper debt, leaving $K_o$ unchanged.

The formula for equity cost under NOI:
$$K_e = K_o + (K_o - K_d) \times \frac{B}{S}$$
Where $B/S$ is the debt‑to‑equity ratio.

**Step‑by‑Step Example 6.1 – NOI Approach**
Take Alpha Corp. again: EBIT = $200,000, no taxes. Assume the market capitalizes the firm at $K_o = 10\%$. Initially, no debt, so V = 200,000 / 0.10 = $2,000,000, Ke = 10%.

Now the firm replaces $400,000 of equity with debt at $K_d = 6\%$. Under NOI, total value V remains $2,000,000 (since EBIT and Ko unchanged). So equity value S = V - B = 2,000,000 - 400,000 = $1,600,000$. The new cost of equity is:
$K_e = 0.10 + (0.10 - 0.06) \times \frac{400,000}{1,600,000} = 0.10 + 0.04 \times 0.25 = 0.10 + 0.01 = 0.11 = 11\%$.

Now compute overall cost as weighted average:
$K_o = \frac{B}{V} K_d + \frac{S}{V} K_e = \frac{400,000}{2,000,000} \times 6\% + \frac{1,600,000}{2,000,000} \times 11\% = 0.20 \times 6\% + 0.80 \times 11\% = 1.2\% + 8.8\% = 10\%$, unchanged.

Firm value remains $2,000,000. So under NOI, the capital structure is irrelevant—value is determined solely by the firm’s earning power and business risk, not by how that income stream is sliced between debtholders and shareholders. This view was the precursor to Modigliani and Miller’s famous proposition.

## 7. The Modigliani‑Miller (MM) Approach

In 1958, Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller published a paper that shook the finance world. They built a rigorous, arbitrage‑based theory of capital structure, starting from a world without taxes and then incorporating corporate taxes.

### 7.1 MM Proposition I – Without Taxes: The “Pie” Model
**Proposition I:** In a world with no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, and perfect capital markets, the value of a firm is independent of its capital structure. That is, the total market value of the firm is the same whether it is financed entirely by equity or with a mix of debt and equity.

$$V_L = V_U = \frac{EBIT}{K_o}$$

Where $V_L$ is the value of a levered firm, and $V_U$ is the value of an unlevered firm. The logic: investors can replicate the effects of corporate leverage on their own (homemade leverage) by borrowing or lending on personal account. If two identical firms had different values, arbitrageurs would sell the overvalued and buy the undervalued until prices equalized. The size of the pie (firm value) is determined by the real assets, not by how the pie is cut.

### 7.2 MM Proposition II – Without Taxes: Cost of Equity
**Proposition II:** The cost of equity for a levered firm increases linearly with the debt‑equity ratio.

$$K_e = K_o + (K_o - K_d) \times \frac{B}{S}$$

This is exactly the NOI formula. As debt increases, shareholders demand a higher return to compensate for the added financial risk. The rise in $K_e$ exactly offsets the benefit of the cheaper debt, leaving the WACC ($K_o$) constant. Thus, capital structure does not affect the overall cost of capital.

**Example 7.1 – MM without Taxes**
An unlevered firm has EBIT = $300,000, $K_o = 12\%$, so $V_U = 300,000 / 0.12 = \$2,500,000$. Now the firm levers up by issuing $1,000,000 in debt at 8% interest and repurchasing equity. Under MM, $V_L$ remains $2,500,000. Equity value $S = 2,500,000 - 1,000,000 = \$1,500,000$. The new $K_e = 0.12 + (0.12 - 0.08) \times (1,000,000/1,500,000) = 0.12 + 0.04 \times 0.6667 = 0.12 + 0.02667 = 14.67\%$. WACC remains 12%.

### 7.3 MM with Corporate Taxes: Debt Becomes Valuable
The 1958 model assumed no taxes. In 1963, MM revised their theory to include corporate taxes, and the result changed dramatically. Because interest payments are tax‑deductible, the government effectively subsidizes debt. The tax shield increases the total cash flow available to all investors.

**Proposition I – With Taxes:**
$$V_L = V_U + T \times D$$
Where $D$ (or $B$) is the market value of debt, and $T$ is the corporate tax rate. The levered firm’s value is the unlevered value plus the present value of the interest tax shield. The more debt, the greater the tax shield, and the higher the firm’s value—implying the optimal capital structure is **100% debt**.

**Proposition II – With Taxes:**
$$K_e = K_o + (K_o - K_d) \times (1 - T) \times \frac{B}{S}$$
The WACC falls continuously as debt increases:
$$WACC = K_o \times \left(1 - T \times \frac{B}{V}\right)$$
So, with corporate taxes, MM says: pile on the debt.

**Step‑by‑Step Example 7.2 – MM with Taxes**
An unlevered firm has EBIT = $400,000, $T = 30\%$, and its unlevered cost of equity (i.e., $K_o$ for the unlevered firm) is 10%. The firm now borrows $1,500,000 perpetual debt at 7% interest. Find the new firm value.

First, value of unlevered firm: After‑tax cash flow = EBIT × (1 - T) = 400,000 × 0.70 = \$280,000. Since it's a perpetuity, $V_U = 280,000 / 0.10 = \$2,800,000$.

Now, with debt: $V_L = V_U + T \times D = 2,800,000 + 0.30 \times 1,500,000 = 2,800,000 + 450,000 = \$3,250,000$.

Equity value $S = V_L - D = 3,250,000 - 1,500,000 = \$1,750,000$.
New cost of equity: $K_e = 0.10 + (0.10 - 0.07)(1 - 0.30) \times (1,500,000/1,750,000) = 0.10 + 0.03 \times 0.70 \times 0.8571 = 0.10 + 0.018 = 0.118 = 11.8\%$.

WACC = (D/V)×Kd(1‑T) + (S/V)×Ke = (1.5/3.25)×0.07×0.7 + (1.75/3.25)×0.118 ≈ 0.4615×0.049 + 0.5385×0.118 = 0.0226 + 0.0635 = 0.0861 = 8.61%, down from 10%. Value is maximized with 100% debt. Of course, in the real world, firms don't do that because of bankruptcy costs, agency costs, and personal taxes, leading to a trade‑off theory—but that's beyond our current scope.

## Chapter Summary
- **Capital structure** is the proportion of debt and equity used to finance a firm. **Optimal capital structure** minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value.
- **EBIT–EPS analysis** compares financing plans. **EPS** = (EBIT - I)(1 - T) / N. The **indifference point** is the EBIT where EPS is equal under two plans; above it, debt leverage yields higher EPS.
- **Net Income (NI) Approach:** Assumes $K_e$ and $K_d$ constant; using more debt lowers overall cost of capital and increases firm value. Capital structure matters greatly.
- **Net Operating Income (NOI) Approach (sometimes mislabeled NY):** Assumes overall $K_o$ constant; cost of equity rises with leverage, offsetting cheaper debt, so firm value is unchanged. Capital structure is irrelevant.
- **Modigliani‑Miller (MM) Approach:**
  - *Without taxes:* $V_L = V_U$, capital structure irrelevant; $K_e$ rises to keep WACC constant (homemade leverage).
  - *With corporate taxes:* $V_L = V_U + T \times D$, debt provides a tax shield, so optimal structure is 100% debt in this pure model.
- Theories evolve from NI (value depends on mix) to NOI/MM no‑tax (mix irrelevant) to MM with tax (debt increases value), highlighting the importance of real‑world frictions like bankruptcy costs.
