# Chapter 4: Market Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning (STP)

---

## 4.1 What is the STP Process?

Imagine you own a small bakery. You bake delicious cakes, cookies, and bread. You put a sign outside that says, "Fresh Baked Goods for Everyone!" 

But no one walks in.

Why? Because "everyone" is not a good target. A college student wants cheap, quick snacks. A working professional wants premium coffee and pastries. A diabetic senior wants sugar-free treats. Trying to be everything to everyone usually means you are nothing to anyone.

This is where the **STP Process** comes in. STP stands for:

1. **S**egmentation
2. **T**argeting
3. **P**ositioning

This is the strategic heart of modern marketing. Instead of throwing a wide net and hoping for the best, STP helps companies focus their efforts where they can win.

**Definition of STP:**
- **Market Segmentation:** Dividing a market into distinct groups of buyers who have different needs, characteristics, or behaviors.
- **Market Targeting:** Evaluating each segment's attractiveness and selecting which segments to enter.
- **Market Positioning:** Arranging for a product to occupy a clear, distinctive, and desirable place in the minds of target consumers relative to competing products.

**The Big Picture:** Think of STP like planning a party.
- **Segmentation:** You decide who to invite (e.g., classmates, family, or coworkers).
- **Targeting:** You choose to invite only your close friends (the best segment).
- **Positioning:** You tell your friends, "This will be the most fun party of the year," so they are excited to come.

## 4.2 Market Segmentation

### The Benefits of Segmentation
Why do companies spend so much money on segmentation research? Because it works. Here are the key benefits:

1. **Better Understanding of Customers:** Marketers can tailor products to specific needs.
2. **Efficient Use of Resources:** Instead of wasting money on mass advertising, you spend it only on the people most likely to buy.
3. **Competitive Advantage:** A brand that serves a niche better than anyone else builds fierce loyalty.
4. **New Market Opportunities:** Marketers can spot underserved "holes" in the market.

### The Four Bases of Segmentation

To divide the market, marketers use four main "bases" or categories. They often combine these to get a complete picture of the customer.

| **Segmentation Base** | **What it Divides By** | **Sub-Factors** | **Example** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Geographic** | Location and physical environment. | Region, city size, climate, urban/rural. | Surfboards sell well in coastal cities; snow shovels sell well in the North. |
| **2. Demographic** | Measurable population statistics. | Age, gender, income, education, occupation. | A luxury watch brand targets males, aged 40-60, with an income over $150,000. |
| **3. Psychographic** | Lifestyle, personality, and values. | Social class, lifestyle, personality traits. | Tesla targets "innovators" and "environmentalists." |
| **4. Behavioral** | Consumer actions and product usage. | Occasion, benefits sought, user status, loyalty. | Airline loyalty programs (frequent flyers vs. first-timers). |

---

#### 1. Geographic Segmentation
This is dividing the market based on location. Needs vary drastically depending on where people live.

- **Region:** Clothes in Mumbai (hot) are different from clothes in Siberia (cold).
- **City Size:** People in Manhattan have different shopping habits than people in a small town.
- **Climate:** Air conditioners sell in hot states; heaters sell in cold states.

**Real-World Example (Starbucks):**
Starbucks uses geographic segmentation masterfully. In Japan, they offer Matcha Frappuccinos (regional taste). In India, they source coffee from local estates and offer savory snacks like "Chicken Tikka Panini" to appeal to local palates. They adapt the menu to the *place*.

---

#### 2. Demographic Segmentation
This is the most popular base because consumer needs are closely tied to demographic variables. It is also the easiest to measure.

- **Age:** Different age groups want different products (e.g., Fortnite for teens, AARP for seniors).
- **Gender:** Cosmetics are traditionally female; shaving cream is male (though gender-neutral brands are emerging).
- **Income:** Luxury brands (Rolex) target high-income earners; Walmart targets value-seekers.
- **Occupation:** A lawyer needs formal suits; a construction worker needs durable boots.

**Real-World Example (Dove vs. Axe):**
Unilever owns both Dove and Axe. However, they use *demographic segmentation* to separate them. Dove targets women (and increasingly men) who value "Real Beauty" and natural skin care. Axe targets young teenage boys (18-24) who want to smell attractive to the opposite sex. They are the same company, but they speak to completely different demographics.

---

#### 3. Psychographic Segmentation
This looks at the "Why." Why do people buy what they buy? It deals with psychology, lifestyle, and values.

- **Lifestyle:** Are they health fanatics? Fashion-forward? Adventurers?
- **Social Class:** Upper-class vs. working-class values.
- **Personality:** Aggressive, outgoing, or shy personalities buy different products.

**Real-World Example (Patagonia):**
Patagonia targets people with a specific *psychographic*: outdoor enthusiasts who deeply care about sustainability. Their customers are not just buying a jacket; they are buying a belief in environmental activism. This psychographic bond creates incredibly loyal customers who proudly display the brand's logo.

---

#### 4. Behavioral Segmentation
This is based on how the consumer actually behaves with the product.

- **Occasion:** Weddings, birthdays, or "just because." Hallmark sells different cards for different occasions.
- **Benefits Sought:** Why did you buy it? For quality? Convenience? Price?
- **User Status:** Non-users, ex-users, potential users, first-time users, regular users.
- **Loyalty Status:** Hard-core loyals (only buy Apple) vs. switchers (buy whatever is on sale).

**Real-World Example (Coca-Cola):**
Coca-Cola uses behavioral segmentation brilliantly. They know that people drink Coke for different *benefits*. 
- **Coca-Cola Classic:** For "nostalgia" and "taste" (regular users).
- **Diet Coke:** For "low calorie" (health-conscious women).
- **Coke Zero:** For "masculine" calorie-conscious consumers who prefer the "real Coke taste."
They even sell smaller cans (7.5 oz) for those who want *portion control*. It is the same drink, but segmented by behavior and benefits.

## 4.3 Market Targeting

Once the market is divided into segments (like slices of a cake), the company must decide *which slices to eat*. This is **Market Targeting**.

**Definition:** The process of evaluating each market segment's attractiveness and selecting one or more segments to enter.

### Evaluating Segments
Before choosing, a marketer asks three key questions:
1. **Size and Growth:** Is this segment big enough to be profitable?
2. **Structural Attractiveness:** Is it easy to compete here? (Are there many powerful competitors? Are the buyers too powerful and demand low prices?)
3. **Company Objectives and Resources:** Does our company have the skills to serve this segment?

### The Four Target Market Strategies

Once evaluation is done, companies choose one of these four strategies:

| **Strategy** | **Approach** | **Marketing Mix** | **Example** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Undifferentiated (Mass)** | Ignores segments; targets the whole market with one offer. | One product, one price, one promotion for everyone. | Basic table salt, sugar, or agricultural commodities. |
| **2. Differentiated (Segmented)** | Targets several segments with different offers for each. | Multiple products and marketing mixes. | Marriott Hotels (Ritz-Carlton for luxury, Courtyard for business, Fairfield for budget). |
| **3. Concentrated (Niche)** | Targets one small, specific segment. | One highly specialized product. | Rolls-Royce (only the super-rich), or vegan-only restaurants. |
| **4. Micro-marketing** | Tailoring products to suit individual customers or local areas. | One-to-one customization. | Stitch Fix (personalized clothing boxes), local store ads on Google. |

---

### 1. Undifferentiated Marketing (Mass Marketing)
The company decides to ignore market segment differences and go after the whole market with one offer. It focuses on what is common in the needs of consumers rather than what is different.

- **Advantage:** Low costs (one production line, one advertising campaign).
- **Disadvantage:** Hard to sustain because competitors can easily target specific segments and steal your customers.

**Real-World Example (Henry Ford's Model T):**
Ford famously offered the Model T in only one color (black). They did not differentiate. It worked because they were the only game in town. Today, pure undifferentiated marketing is rare.

---

### 2. Differentiated Marketing (Segmented Marketing)
The company decides to target several market segments and designs separate offers for each. This creates more sales overall but is also more expensive.

**Real-World Example (Marriott International):**
Marriott does not just have one hotel. They have:
- **Ritz-Carlton:** For the ultra-luxury traveler.
- **Marriott Hotel:** For standard business travelers.
- **Courtyard by Marriott:** For budget-conscious travelers.
- **Sheraton:** For classic, traditional stays.
By differentiating, they capture a customer at every income level. If you get promoted, you move up within their ecosystem!

---

### 3. Concentrated Marketing (Niche Marketing)
Instead of going after a small share of a large market, the company goes after a large share of one or a few small segments (niches). This is especially attractive for companies with limited resources.

**Real-World Example (Rolls-Royce):**
Rolls-Royce does not try to sell cars to the middle class. They only target billionaires and royalty (a tiny, exclusive niche). They charge millions of dollars per car. They do not care about volume; they care about exclusivity.

**Real-World Example (Lush Cosmetics):**
Lush targets a niche of consumers who are against animal testing and love natural, handmade products. They are not for everyone, but their niche is fiercely loyal.

---

### 4. Micro-marketing
This is the ultimate in customization. It tailors products and marketing programs to suit the tastes of specific individuals or local customer groups.

- **Local Marketing:** McDonald's sells the McSpicy Paneer in India, but not in the US.
- **Individual Marketing (One-to-One):** Amazon's homepage recommendations are different for every single user based on their history.

**Real-World Example (Stitch Fix):**
Stitch Fix is an online personal styling service. Customers fill out a detailed profile, and stylists pick clothes tailored to their specific size, style, and budget. Each box is unique. That is micro-marketing at its finest.

## 4.4 Market Positioning

**Definition:** Market positioning is the act of designing the company's offering and image to occupy a distinctive place in the minds of the target market.

**Think of it this way:** The customer's mind is like a crowded shelf. If you just put your product on the shelf without context, it gets ignored. Positioning tells the customer: *"This is the box you should pick up because it is the cheapest, or the best quality, or the most fun."*

### Strategies for Market Positioning

Marketers can position their brand based on five key approaches:

| **Positioning Strategy** | **Focus** | **Real-World Example** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **By Attributes or Benefits** | Highlighting a specific feature. | **Volvo:** "Safety." They are known as the safest cars on earth. |
| **By Price or Quality** | Emphasizing high status or great value. | **Rolex:** High price = high prestige. **Walmart:** "Everyday Low Prices." |
| **By Use or Occasion** | Positioning for a specific moment. | **Gatorade:** Positioned for athletes and intense physical activity. |
| **By Competitor** | Comparing directly to another brand. | **Avis:** "We are number 2, so we try harder." |
| **By Cultural Symbol** | Using meaningful icons or lifestyles. | **Marlboro (historically):** The rugged American cowboy symbolizing freedom. |

---

### Choosing the Right Positioning Strategy

To choose a position, a marketer must follow a three-step process:

#### Step 1: Identify a Competitive Advantage
You must figure out what makes your brand better than the competition. This is your **Unique Selling Proposition (USP)**.
- Is it lower cost? (Honda)
- Is it better service? (Amazon Prime)
- Is it superior technology? (Apple)

#### Step 2: Select the Right Advantage
Not all advantages are worth pursuing. The advantage must be:
- **Distinctive:** Something competitors do not offer.
- **Communicable:** Easy to explain to customers.
- **Preemptive:** Hard for competitors to copy immediately.

#### Step 3: Communicate and Deliver the Position
You must tell the world about your position through the marketing mix (4 Ps). If you position as a "luxury" brand, your **price** must be high, your **place** must be exclusive, and your **promotion** must look elegant.

**Real-World Example (Tesla's Positioning):**
Tesla identified a competitive advantage: cutting-edge electric technology and high performance. They chose the "Luxury" and "Innovator" position. They communicated this by placing stores in high-end malls (not traditional auto dealerships), pricing their cars like luxury sedans, and promoting through tech-savvy channels rather than TV commercials. Tesla is positioned in the consumer's mind as the "Apple of cars."

---

### Developing a Positioning Statement

A powerful tool to summarize a brand's position is the **Positioning Statement**. This is an internal document (not usually shared with the public) that guides all marketing decisions.

**The Formula:**
> **"For [target segment], [brand name] is the [point of differentiation] because [reasons to believe]."**

Let's break it down:
- **Target Segment:** Who are we serving?
- **Brand Name:** What is our product?
- **Point of Differentiation:** What is the unique benefit we offer?
- **Reasons to Believe:** Why should the customer trust us?

**Real-World Example (Nike):**
Let's write a positioning statement for Nike's running shoes.
> **"For serious athletes, Nike is the performance brand that pushes your limits because we use cutting-edge technology and design innovations tested by the world's best athletes."**

**Real-World Example (Domino's Pizza):**
> **"For hungry pizza lovers who value speed, Domino's is the delivery brand that gets your pizza hot to your door within 30 minutes because we use a smart, optimized delivery system."**

**Why it matters:** This simple statement ensures that every single decision in the company—from R&D (should we make a toaster?) to sales (how do we greet customers?)—aligns with the "speedy delivery" position. If a new idea does not match the statement, the company should not do it.

## Chapter 4 Summary

- **The STP Process** is the strategic foundation of modern marketing: Segment, Target, Position.
- **Segmentation** divides the market into meaningful groups. There are four bases: **Geographic** (where), **Demographic** (who), **Psychographic** (why), and **Behavioral** (how they act).
- **Targeting** involves evaluating segments and choosing which to enter. Strategies include **Undifferentiated** (mass), **Differentiated** (multiple segments), **Concentrated** (niche), and **Micro-marketing** (individual/local).
- **Positioning** is how you place the product in the consumer's mind. Strategies include positioning by attributes, price, use, competitor, or cultural symbols.
- To choose a position, companies must find a competitive advantage, select the right one, and communicate it. The **Positioning Statement** (Target + Brand + Differentiation + Reasons to Believe) guides all internal marketing efforts.
