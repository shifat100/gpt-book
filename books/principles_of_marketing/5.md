# Chapter 5: Product and Service

## 5.1 What is a Product?

Imagine you are buying a brand-new iPhone. You walk out of the Apple Store with a sleek box containing glass, metal, and silicon chips. 

But are you really just buying glass and metal?

Of course not. You are buying *communication* with your family, *status* among your peers, *access* to an ecosystem of apps, and *peace of mind* with Apple's customer support. The physical phone is just the tip of the iceberg.

**Definition:** A product is anything that can be offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use, or consumption that might satisfy a want or need. 

Products are not just physical items. They include **services** (airline flights), **persons** (celebrity endorsements), **places** (tourist destinations like Paris), **organizations** (charities), and **ideas** (saving the environment). In short, a product is a bundle of benefits designed to solve a customer's problem.

---

## 5.2 Levels of Product

To understand what a product truly offers, marketers break it down into **three distinct levels**. Each level adds more value to the customer.

| **Level** | **What It Is** | **The Question It Answers** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Core Customer Value** | The fundamental benefit or problem-solving service the customer is actually buying. | *"What is the customer really buying?"* |
| **2. Actual Product** | The tangible, physical features, design, quality level, brand name, and packaging. | *"What does it look and feel like?"* |
| **3. Augmented Product** | The additional non-tangible benefits and services that come with the product. | *"What extras do I get?"* |

---

### Level 1: Core Customer Value
This is the *real* reason the customer buys the product. It is not about the features; it is about the *feeling* or the *solution*.

**Real-World Example (Hotel):**
When you book a room at the Ritz-Carlton, are you buying a bed, a bathroom, and a TV? No. You are buying a *restful night's sleep* and a *luxurious escape* from your daily stress. The core value is "rest and rejuvenation."

---

### Level 2: Actual Product
This is the physical good or service delivery that you can see, touch, or experience. It includes:
- **Features** (camera megapixels)
- **Design** (sleek, minimalist)
- **Quality Level** (premium materials)
- **Brand Name** (Apple, Nike)
- **Packaging** (the iconic Apple white box)

**Real-World Example (Starbucks):**
The actual product is a cup of brewed coffee. But it is served in a specific green-and-white branded cup, with a pleasant store design, and the barista writes your name on it. This transforms a simple drink into a branded experience.

---

### Level 3: Augmented Product
This includes all the extra services and benefits that enhance the product *beyond* the physical good. This is where companies build a competitive advantage.

**Real-World Example (Apple):**
The iPhone (Actual Product) is great. But the Augmented Product includes:
- Free software updates for years.
- The Apple Genius Bar for technical support.
- The iCloud backup system.
- A seamless ecosystem with MacBooks and Apple Watches.

**Professor's Tip:** Many companies compete fiercely at the Augmented Product level because it is hard to copy a strong ecosystem, even if competitors can copy the physical features.

---

## 5.3 Classification of Products

Products fall into two broad categories based on *who buys them*: Consumer Products and Industrial Products.

### 1. Consumer Products
These are products bought by final consumers for personal consumption. Marketers classify them further based on *consumer shopping habits*.

| **Type** | **Definition** | **Price & Effort** | **Example** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Convenience Goods** | Products bought frequently, with minimal comparison. | Low price, low effort. | Milk, bread, chewing gum, toothpaste. |
| **Shopping Goods** | Products compared on basis of quality, price, and style. | Moderate price, moderate effort. | Furniture, clothing, used cars, hotel rooms. |
| **Specialty Goods** | Products with unique characteristics or brand identification. | High price, high effort (specifically sought). | Luxury cars (Porsche), designer handbags, medical specialists. |
| **Unsought Goods** | Products the consumer does not know about or does not normally consider buying. | Varies; requires aggressive selling. | Life insurance, encyclopedias, blood donations. |

**Real-World Example (Rolex vs. Toothpaste):**
- Toothpaste is a **Convenience** good. You grab it off the shelf without much thought.
- A Rolex watch is a **Specialty** good. You don't just "happen" to buy one; you research authorized dealers, compare models, and actively seek out the brand.

---

### 2. Industrial Products
These are products bought by businesses and organizations for further processing or for use in conducting a business. They are classified into three groups:

| **Type** | **Definition** | **Example** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Materials & Parts** | Raw materials and manufactured parts that go directly into the final product. | Steel (for cars), flour (for bakeries), microchips (for computers). |
| **Capital Items** | Long-lasting goods that facilitate developing or managing the finished product. | Factory buildings, office buildings, heavy machinery, forklifts. |
| **Supplies & Business Services** | Short-lasting items and services that support business operations. | Office stationery, cleaning services, legal consulting, repair services. |

**Real-World Example (Nike's Supply Chain):**
Nike buys *materials* (leather, rubber, synthetic fabrics) from industrial suppliers. They purchase *capital items* (sewing machines, warehouse robotics) to manufacture and store shoes. They hire *business services* (marketing agencies, logistics firms like DHL) to run operations smoothly.

## 5.4 The New Product Development Process

You might wonder, "If a product is successful, why not just keep selling it forever?" Because products, like humans, have a life cycle. Eventually, they get old and sales drop. To survive, companies must constantly develop **new products**.

However, developing new products is risky. Studies show that about **90% of new products fail** in the market. To reduce this risk, successful companies follow a structured **8-Step New Product Development (NPD) Process**.

| **Stage** | **What Happens** | **Key Question** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Idea Generation** | Brainstorming new ideas from internal (R&D, employees) and external (customers, competitors) sources. | *"What if we tried this?"* |
| **2. Idea Screening** | Filtering out bad ideas early to save costs. | *"Is this worth pursuing?"* |
| **3. Concept Development & Testing** | Developing a detailed version of the idea and testing it on target consumers. | *"What do consumers think of this concept?"* |
| **4. Marketing Strategy** | Outlining the initial marketing plan: target market, positioning, price, and budget. | *"How will we sell this?"* |
| **5. Business Analysis** | Reviewing sales, costs, and profit projections to see if the idea is financially sound. | *"Will this make money?"* |
| **6. Product Development** | Creating the actual physical prototype. | *"Can we actually build this?"* |
| **7. Test Marketing** | Introducing the product into a limited, realistic market setting to gauge performance. | *"How does it perform in the real world?"* |
| **8. Commercialization** | Full-scale launch into the market. | *"Let's launch it worldwide."* |

---

### A Closer Look at the NPD Process:

**Real-World Example (Dyson):**
James Dyson is famous for his relentless innovation. 
- **Idea Generation:** He noticed his vacuum cleaner losing suction due to clogged bags.
- **Idea Screening:** He decided bagless cyclonic separation was worth exploring.
- **Development/Testing:** He created over 5,000 prototypes over 15 years!
- **Commercialization:** Dyson launched the bagless vacuum, which completely disrupted the market. Dyson did not rush to market; they perfected the engineering first.

**Real-World Example (Apple Vision Pro):**
Apple spent years in the *idea generation* and *development* phases for their mixed-reality headset. They heavily *tested the concept* with select developers before launching. Even now, they are in the early *commercialization* stage, testing the waters with a premium-priced product before rolling out a more mass-market version.

## 5.5 The Product Life Cycle (PLC)

Every product has a finite lifespan. The **Product Life Cycle (PLC)** is the course that a product's sales and profits take over its lifetime. It has **four distinct stages**.

| **Stage** | **Sales** | **Profits** | **Typical Consumers** | **Marketing Strategy** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Introduction** | Low, slow growth | Negative (losses) or zero | Innovators | Create product awareness and trial. Heavy promotion. |
| **2. Growth** | Rapid increase | Rising (peak at end) | Early Adopters | Build brand preference. Maximize market share. |
| **3. Maturity** | Peak, stable/slowing | Peak, then declining | Middle Majority | Defend market share. Innovate to differentiate. |
| **4. Decline** | Falling | Falling | Laggards | Reduce costs, phase out, or rejuvenate the product. |

---

### 1. Introduction Stage
The product is new to the market. Sales grow slowly. Profits are usually negative because of heavy R&D, advertising, and distribution costs.

- **Strategy:** Create "Awareness." Tell the customer *what* the product is and *why* they need it. Use skimming (high price) or penetration (low price) pricing.

**Real-World Example (Electric Vehicles 10 years ago):**
When Tesla first launched the Roadster, sales were slow, costs were astronomically high, and they barely made a profit. They focused on educating the public about what an electric car could do. They targeted "innovators" (tech enthusiasts and environmentalists).

---

### 2. Growth Stage
The product is accepted by the early adopters. Sales and profits start soaring. Competitors enter the market.

- **Strategy:** Build "Brand Preference." Improve product quality, add features, and expand distribution. Keep prices stable or slightly lower to attract the mass market.

**Real-World Example (Plant-Based Meat - Beyond Meat):**
After the initial buzz, Beyond Meat experienced huge growth. They expanded distribution to thousands of fast-food chains like KFC and McDonald's. They spent heavily on advertising to build brand preference over traditional meat.

---

### 3. Maturity Stage
This is typically the *longest* stage. Sales growth slows down because the market is saturated. There is intense price competition. Profits begin to decline.

- **Strategy:** "Defend" market share. Companies modify the product (new features), modify the market (find new users), or modify the marketing mix (sales promotions).

**Real-World Example (Coca-Cola):**
Coca-Cola is in the *mature* stage. Almost everyone on the planet who wants a cola already has one. To survive, they don't change the core drink; they innovate in *packaging* (mini cans), *flavors* (Coca-Cola with Coffee), and *promotions* (Share a Coke with your name). They defend their market share fiercely.

---

### 4. Decline Stage
Sales take a steep dive. Profits erode. The product may be overwhelmed by new technologies or shifting consumer tastes.

- **Strategy:** Either **Maintain** (keep it going to milk the last profits), **Harvest** (reduce costs drastically), or **Drop** (kill the product).

**Real-World Example (DVD Players):**
DVD players are in sharp decline because of streaming services like Netflix and Disney+. Most companies have dropped these products from their lineup or moved them to a niche market (collectors).

## 5.6 Understanding Services

Not all products are physical. When you take a flight, get a haircut, or visit a doctor, you are buying a **service**.

**Definition:** A service is a form of product that consists of activities, benefits, or satisfactions offered for sale that are essentially intangible and do not result in the ownership of anything.

### The 4 Characteristics of Services (IHIP)

Services are fundamentally different from physical goods in four distinct ways:

| **Characteristic** | **Definition** | **Meaning for Marketer** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Intangibility** | Cannot be seen, tasted, felt, heard, or smelled before purchase. | You cannot "test drive" a surgery. You rely on trust and reputation. |
| **2. Inseparability** | Produced and consumed simultaneously; cannot be separated from the provider. | The barber is physically present while cutting your hair. |
| **3. Variability** | Quality depends on who provides them, when, and where. | Your haircut by the junior stylist vs. the senior stylist will be different. |
| **4. Perishability** | Cannot be stored for later sale or use. | An empty airline seat on a flight at 8 AM is lost forever; you cannot sell it at 10 AM. |

---

#### 1. Intangibility
You cannot hold a flight ticket in your hand and feel the "flight." You buy the promise of getting from A to B safely.

**Marketing Strategy:** Make the intangible *tangible*. Use powerful imagery, testimonials, and physical evidence. 
**Real-World Example (Hotels):** Hotels cannot show you the "sleep" they sell. Instead, they show you crisp white sheets, a stunning pool, and smiling staff in photos to make the intangible experience feel real.

---

#### 2. Inseparability
The service provider and the customer are present together. The quality of the experience depends heavily on the interaction.

**Marketing Strategy:** Hire and train excellent staff. The employee *is* the product.
**Real-World Example (Ritz-Carlton):** They empower every employee (even housekeeping) to spend up to $2,000 to solve a guest's problem immediately. The service cannot be separated from the employee, so they invest heavily in the employee.

---

#### 3. Variability
Service quality depends on the service provider's skills and mood. A doctor can have a bad day; a waiter might be rude.

**Marketing Strategy:** Standardize the service process. Create checklists, use technology, and invest heavily in continuous training.
**Real-World Example (McDonald's):** Whether you are in Tokyo, London, or New York, a Big Mac tastes and looks the same. McDonald's uses strict operational standards (recipes, timers, temperature controls) to reduce *variability* globally.

---

#### 4. Perishability
Services cannot be inventoried. If you don't sell the seat, the room, or the hour of the consultant's time, it's gone forever.

**Marketing Strategy:** Manage demand and supply. Use dynamic pricing to shift demand to off-peak times.
**Real-World Example (Uber):** Uber uses *surge pricing* during rain or peak hours. When demand is high, prices go up to encourage more drivers to come online (supply) and to discourage some riders (demand). This balances the perishable "time" of the driver.

---

## 5.7 Marketing Strategies for Service Firms

Because services have these unique characteristics, service marketers must use a different toolkit than product marketers. While the 4 Ps are still used, services marketing adds three more Ps to create the **7 Ps of Services Marketing**.

| **Traditional 4 Ps** | **Extended 3 Ps (Services)** | **Meaning** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Product, Price, Place, Promotion | **People, Process, Physical Evidence** | - **People:** All human actors who play a part in service delivery (staff, customers).<br>- **Process:** The procedures, mechanisms, and flow of activities.<br>- **Physical Evidence:** The environment and tangible cues that support the service. |

**Real-World Example (Singapore Airlines):**
- **People:** Their flight attendants are world-renowned for their hospitality and grace. They are the *brand*.
- **Process:** The seamless online check-in, efficient boarding, and in-flight entertainment system create a smooth journey.
- **Physical Evidence:** The elegant airport lounges, the fine china, and the premium cutlery signal high quality even before you taste the food.

**Strategy Focus:** To win, service firms must:
1.  **Differentiate the service:** Add features or perks that competitors don't offer (e.g., free Wi-Fi on flights).
2.  **Manage service quality:** Consistently meet customer expectations. If you promise "fast delivery," you must deliver fast.
3.  **Manage productivity:** Find ways to serve more customers without sacrificing quality (e.g., self-checkout kiosks).

## 5.8 Branding Strategy

A product is made in a factory, but a **brand** is made in the mind of the consumer. 

**Definition:** A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol, or design, or a combination of these, intended to identify the goods or services of one seller or group of sellers and to differentiate them from those of competitors.

Building a strong brand is the most valuable asset a company can have. Apple's brand alone is worth over $100 billion. Here is how companies build strong brands through **Four Key Branding Decisions**.

| **Decision** | **What it Involves** | **Real-World Example** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Brand Positioning** | Deciding what attributes, benefits, and beliefs the brand should stand for. | **Volvo:** "Safety." <br> **Harley-Davidson:** "Freedom and rebellion." |
| **2. Brand Name Selection** | Choosing a name that is easy to remember, distinctive, and meaningful. | **Amazon:** Suggests vastness and endless selection. <br> **Google:** A play on "googol," implying massive data. |
| **3. Brand Sponsorship** | Deciding who owns the brand. | **Manufacturer's (Sony), Private (Kirkland), Licensed (Disney characters), Co-branding (Apple Pay + Mastercard).** |
| **4. Brand Development** | Deciding how to grow the brand. | **Line extension, Brand extension, Multi-brands, or New brands.** |

---

### 1. Brand Positioning
This aligns with the positioning concept from Chapter 4. Marketers must position their brand clearly in the minds of the target market. The brand promise must be authentic.

**Real-World Example (Volvo):**
Volvo chose to position itself on *safety* at a time when other carmakers were focusing on speed or luxury. They developed side-impact airbags, crumple zones, and seatbelt technology to back up that promise. Today, "Volvo" is synonymous with "safety."

---

### 2. Brand Name Selection
A good name creates a positive association. The name should:
- Suggest the product's benefits (e.g., "Snapchat" for quick photos).
- Be easy to pronounce and remember (e.g., "Nike").
- Be distinctive (e.g., "Xerox").

**Real-World Example (Tesla):**
The name "Tesla" honors Nikola Tesla, the brilliant (and slightly eccentric) electrical engineer. This positions the brand as innovative, slightly rebellious, and deeply connected to electricity. It is a highly distinctive name that stands out from "Ford" or "Chevrolet."

---

### 3. Brand Sponsorship
A manufacturer has four sponsorship options:

| **Sponsorship Type** | **Explanation** | **Example** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Manufacturer's Brand** | The brand is owned by the producer. | Sony, Samsung, Apple. |
| **Private (or Store) Brand** | The brand is owned by the retailer. | Costco's Kirkland Signature, Walmart's Great Value. |
| **Licensed Brand** | Using another company's brand name for a fee. | Disney licensing Mickey Mouse to t-shirt manufacturers. |
| **Co-branding** | Combining two established brands. | Google + Nest; Uber + Spotify (music integration). |

**Real-World Example (Kirkland Signature):**
Costco's private brand "Kirkland Signature" is famous for offering premium quality at a lower price than name brands. Costco owns the brand, so they can sell it cheaper, and it builds fierce loyalty to Costco itself.

---

### 4. Brand Development Strategies
A company has four growth options for its brands.

| **Strategy** | **Definition** | **Risk/Advantage** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Line Extension** | Introducing additional items in the same product category under the same brand name. | Low risk (Dove adds a new shampoo scent). |
| **Brand Extension** | Using an existing brand name for a new product category. | High risk but high reward (Apple moving from computers to phones). |
| **Multi-Brands** | Introducing multiple brands in the same category. | Avoids cannibalization (P&G owns Tide and Cheer, both detergents). |
| **New Brands** | Creating a completely new brand for a new product category. | High cost (Toyota creating Lexus for luxury). |

**Real-World Example (Virgin):**
Richard Branson is the king of *Brand Extension*. Virgin started as a music record label. Then it extended the "Virgin" brand to airlines, mobile phones, trains, and even space travel (Virgin Galactic). The brand stands for "fun, rebellious, and innovative," which allowed them to enter wildly different categories.

## Chapter 5 Summary

- A **Product** is anything offered to satisfy a need. It has three levels: **Core** (the real benefit), **Actual** (tangible features), and **Augmented** (extra services).
- Products are classified as **Consumer** (Convenience, Shopping, Specialty, Unsought) or **Industrial** (Materials, Capital, Supplies).
- The **New Product Development (NPD)** process involves 8 steps from Idea Generation to Commercialization. This structured process reduces the high risk of new product failure.
- The **Product Life Cycle (PLC)** has four stages: Introduction, Growth, Maturity, and Decline. Marketing strategies must change at each stage.
- **Services** are intangible, inseparable, variable, and perishable (IHIP). Marketers must manage these unique traits by adding **People, Process, and Physical Evidence** (the 7 Ps).
- **Branding** is the art of creating meaning in the consumer's mind. Strong brands are built through strategic decisions on **Positioning, Name Selection, Sponsorship, and Development** (Line extensions, Brand extensions, etc.).
