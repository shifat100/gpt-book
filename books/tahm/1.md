## 1.1 Introduction: Why Tourism Demands a System Perspective

Tourism is frequently described as the world's largest industry, yet it is unlike any conventional industry. There is no single "tourism product" that rolls off an assembly line; there is no homogeneous "tourism firm" that can be listed on a stock exchange under a single sector code. Instead, tourism is a **composite phenomenon**: a temporary movement of people that activates transport providers, accommodation establishments, food services, attractions, intermediaries, and public infrastructure simultaneously. A tourist purchasing an airline ticket, a hotel night, a museum entry, and a restaurant meal is, in effect, assembling a product from fragments supplied by firms that may not even perceive themselves as belonging to the same industry.

This composite character is precisely why the study of tourism requires a **systems approach**. Isolated definitions—of the tourist, of the industry, of the economic impact—capture only fragments. The framework that has proven most durable in tourism scholarship is Neil Leiper's **Whole Tourism System Model**, which treats tourism as an open system comprising five interacting elements. Before examining that model, however, we must establish the foundational vocabulary: who, precisely, qualifies as a tourist?

## 1.2 Tourism in Perspective: Definitions and Boundaries

### 1.2.1 Defining Tourism, Tourist, Excursionist, and Hospitality

The contemporary statistical architecture of tourism rests on the **International Recommendations for Tourism Statistics 2008 (IRTS 2008)** , adopted by the United Nations Statistical Commission and promulgated by UN Tourism (formerly the UN World Tourism Organization, UNWTO). These recommendations define tourism as:

> “The activities of travellers taking trips to a main destination **outside their usual environment** for less than one year for any main purpose (business, leisure and other personal) **other than to be employed by a resident entity in the place visited**”.

Three elements of this definition warrant close attention. First, the trip must occur **outside the usual environment**—the geographical area within which an individual conducts regular life routines. A commute to one's regular workplace is not tourism; a weekend stay in a neighbouring district may be. Second, the duration must be **less than one year**; longer stays are classified as migration or residence rather than visitation. Third, the purpose must exclude **employment by a resident entity** in the place visited; a Bangladeshi engineer posted to a project in Dubai for two years is a migrant worker, not a tourist, even if the posting is temporary.

All travellers meeting these criteria are called **visitors**. Visitors are then classified along two axes:

- **By length of stay**: A **tourist** (or *overnight visitor*) stays for **at least one night** in the place visited. An **excursionist** (or *same-day visitor*) completes the visit **without an overnight stay**.
- **By country of residence relative to the country visited**: An **international visitor** has a country of residence different from the country visited; a **domestic visitor** resides within the country visited.

Crucially, an **international excursionist**—for example, a day-tripper crossing from Bangladesh into India and returning the same evening—counts as a visitor and generates tourism expenditure, but is excluded from hotel occupancy statistics and from the count of "tourists" in the narrow sense. This distinction has profound consequences for measurement: destinations that rely heavily on day-trippers may record impressive visitor volumes while capturing comparatively modest accommodation revenue.

**Hospitality**, by contrast, is a broader and older concept. It refers to the **receptive, welcoming treatment of strangers and guests**, encompassing accommodation, food and beverage service, and the relational ethos of host-guest encounter. Tourism is the *movement-driven* phenomenon; hospitality is the *service and reception* phenomenon. A restaurant serving only local residents practices hospitality but does not participate in tourism; a campsite with no food service participates in tourism but offers minimal hospitality in the traditional sense. The two concepts overlap heavily in the modern accommodation and food-service sectors, which is why they are taught together, but they are analytically distinct.

**Table 1.1: Tourist versus Excursionist (UNWTO / IRTS 2008 Criteria)**

| Criterion | Tourist (Overnight Visitor) | Excursionist (Same-Day Visitor) |
|---|---|---|
| **Length of stay** | At least one night | Less than 24 hours; no overnight stay |
| **Core UNWTO definition** | A visitor whose trip includes an overnight stay | A visitor whose trip does not include an overnight stay |
| **Typical examples** | Hotel guest on a 5-night leisure package; business traveller staying 3 nights | Cruise passenger disembarking for a day; border day-tripper; domestic pilgrim returning home the same evening |
| **Accommodation expenditure** | Yes (hotel, guesthouse, serviced apartment) | Typically no accommodation expenditure (may purchase meals and attractions) |
| **Statistical visibility** | Captured in accommodation occupancy and tourism satellite accounts | Often under-recorded; significant in border and cruise economies |
| **Economic footprint** | Broader: accommodation, meals, multiple attractions | Narrower: meals, admissions, local transport, souvenirs |

The practical implication for destination managers is that **volume and value are not the same thing**. A resort that attracts 100,000 excursionists may generate less local income than one attracting 20,000 overnight tourists, because excursionists do not purchase beds, breakfasts, or evening meals. Conversely, excursionists can be valuable for filling daytime capacity at attractions and restaurants without imposing additional accommodation infrastructure demands.

### 1.2.2 The Whole Tourism System Model (Neil Leiper)

In a landmark 1979 paper in *Annals of Tourism Research*, Neil Leiper argued that the multi-faceted nature of tourism demands a **holistic definition** grounded in **systems methodology**. He isolated **five interacting elements**: tourists, three geographical elements (the **traveller-generating region**, the **transit route**, and the **destination region**), and a **tourism industry**. The model's enduring value lies in its insistence that tourism cannot be understood by studying destinations alone, nor by studying tourists alone, but only by tracing the **functional and spatial connections** across the entire system.

**Element 1: The Traveller-Generating Region.** This is the origin market—the place where tourists live, work, and decide to travel. It is the region that *pushes* travellers into the system. The generating region supplies not only the tourists themselves but also the **motivations, financial capacity, and travel-buying habits** that shape demand. A recession in a generating region, a currency depreciation, or a shift in holiday entitlement law will ripple through the entire system. For Bangladesh, Europe, the Gulf states, and the broader South Asian region function as important generating regions for inbound tourism, while Dhaka and Chittagong generate domestic tourists bound for Cox's Bazar and the Hill Tracts.

**Element 2: The Transit Route.** The transit route is the **spatial and functional link** between generating and destination regions—the corridors, modes, and intermediaries through which travellers move. It includes physical infrastructure (air routes, highways, railways, sea lanes) and service infrastructure (airlines, coach operators, travel agents, visa regimes). The transit route is not merely a neutral conduit; it shapes the **cost, duration, comfort, and even the possibility** of travel. A destination that is physically attractive but served only by a single unreliable air link has a constrained tourism system, regardless of its intrinsic appeal. The historical expansion of tourism—from turnpike roads to jet aircraft—can be read as a continuous revolution in the capacity and affordability of transit routes.

**Element 3: The Destination Region.** This is the region that *pulls* travellers—the location of the natural and cultural features that motivate the trip, together with the accommodation, attractions, and services that support the stay. The destination region is where the bulk of tourism expenditure occurs and where the most visible impacts—economic, environmental, and socio-cultural—are felt. It is also the region where the **host community** lives, a point to which we return in the four-components discussion.

**Element 4: The Tourist.** The tourist is the **human actor** whose decision and movement activate the entire system. Leiper treats the tourist not as a passive consumer but as a system element whose motivations, expectations, and behaviours feed back into the generating region (through word-of-mouth and repeat demand) and into the destination region (through expenditure and behavioural impacts).

**Element 5: The Tourism Industry.** The tourism industry comprises the **collection of firms and organisations** that provide services to tourists—transport carriers, accommodation providers, food-service operators, attractions, and intermediaries such as tour operators and travel agents. Leiper emphasised that the industry is **partially industrialised**: some functions are performed by formal firms, while others are performed by informal providers, public agencies, or the tourists themselves. This is why tourism industry statistics are notoriously difficult to bound; there is no natural "fence" around the industry.

The model's power lies in its depiction of **feedback loops**. Tourist dissatisfaction at a destination can degrade the generating region's demand; investment in a transit route can open a previously inaccessible destination; leakage of tourism revenue out of the destination can weaken the local economy and, eventually, the quality of the tourist experience itself. The whole tourism system is thus a **dynamic, open system**, not a static value chain.

### 1.2.3 The Four Core Components of Tourism Management

While Leiper's model describes the *system*, the *management* of tourism requires attention to four stakeholder groups whose interests must be balanced. These four components are frequently identified in destination management literature as government institutions, the local community, industry entrepreneurs, and tourists themselves.

**Component 1: The Tourist (Seeking Experiences).** The tourist is the demand-side protagonist. Contemporary tourism management recognises that tourists are not merely purchasing beds and meals; they are seeking **experiences**—memorable, personally transformative encounters that combine novelty, learning, relaxation, social connection, and often a degree of risk or adventure. Kotler et al. emphasise that hospitality and tourism marketing must therefore focus on **experience design** and **customer value**, not merely on service delivery. The tourist's expectations are shaped by the generating region's culture, by digital media, and by prior travel experience; managing satisfaction requires understanding the **gap** between expectation and perception.

**Component 2: The Business Providers (Commercial Services).** These are the firms that supply transport, accommodation, food and beverage, attractions, retail, and intermediation. Their interest is in **profitability and return on investment**, which depends on occupancy rates, yield management, seasonality smoothing, and cost control. Business providers range from multinational hotel chains and airlines to micro-enterprises such as family guesthouses and street-food vendors. The management challenge is that the industry is **highly fragmented** and its firms often have divergent time horizons: an airline may prioritise load factors over destination sustainability, while a family guesthouse depends directly on the local environment's health.

**Component 3: The Host Government (Regulatory and Economic Framework).** Government plays multiple roles: **regulator** (visa policy, safety standards, environmental protection, licensing), **facilitator** (infrastructure provision, destination marketing, aviation bilateral agreements), **stimulator** (tax incentives, investment promotion), and sometimes **operator** (state-owned hotels, national carriers, tourism corporations). The host government's interest is in **maximising net public benefit**—foreign exchange earnings, employment, tax revenue, and regional development—while mitigating negative externalities. In Bangladesh, the Ministry of Civil Aviation and Tourism and the Bangladesh Parjatan Corporation (BPC) exemplify the governmental component, with BPC established in 1972 as the national tourism organisation.

**Component 4: The Host Community (Cultural and Social Context).** The host community is the resident population of the destination region. Its interest lies in **livelihood, cultural continuity, environmental quality, and equitable access to the benefits of tourism**. The host community is not a passive backdrop: residents are the custodians of local culture, the informal ambassadors of hospitality, and the first to bear the costs of overcrowding, inflation, and cultural commodification. Sustainable tourism management requires **community participation** in planning and a fair distribution of tourism revenues. Where communities are excluded from decision-making, resentment and resistance can undermine the very attractiveness of the destination.

Effective tourism management, then, is the art of **aligning four partially conflicting logics**: the tourist's search for authentic experience, the business provider's search for profit, the government's search for public benefit, and the community's search for wellbeing and identity.

### 1.2.4 Basic Approaches to the Study of Tourism

Tourism is studied from multiple disciplinary vantage points. The eight approaches below are not mutually exclusive; sophisticated analysis typically combines several.

**The Institutional Approach** focuses on the **organisations and intermediaries** that facilitate travel: travel agencies, tour operators, airlines, hotel chains, and government tourism bodies. Its disciplinary root is **business and management studies**. The institutional approach asks: how are tourism institutions structured, how do they compete and collaborate, and how do their practices shape the traveller's experience? It is particularly useful for understanding distribution channels and industry structure, but it can neglect the broader social and environmental context.

**The Product Approach** examines tourism through the **bundles of goods and services** that are marketed to travellers—the "tourism product." It analyses product design, differentiation, life cycles, and quality. Its root is **marketing**. Kotler et al.'s treatment of hospitality and tourism marketing is essentially a sophisticated product approach, enriched by service-dominant logic. The limitation is that the tourism "product" is notoriously difficult to define and bound; a destination is a product, but so is a single museum ticket, and the two operate at different levels of aggregation.

**The Historical Approach** traces the **origins and evolution** of travel, hospitality, and tourism institutions. It explains how contemporary practices—the package tour, the hotel rating system, the passport—emerged from particular historical conjunctures. Its disciplinary home is **history**. The historical approach is indispensable for understanding path dependence: why, for example, certain destinations remain locked into mass-tourism models long after the model's drawbacks have become apparent.

**The Managerial Approach** is **firm-oriented and microeconomic** in orientation, focusing on the management activities necessary to operate a tourist enterprise: operations, human resources, finance, revenue management, and strategic planning. It is the dominant approach in hospitality business schools. Its strength is practical relevance; its limitation is a tendency to treat the destination as an externality rather than as a managed system.

**The Economic Approach** analyses tourism as a **generator of income, employment, foreign exchange, and tax revenue**, and examines its costs in terms of leakage, inflation, and opportunity cost. Its disciplinary root is **economics**. The economic approach has produced the Tourism Satellite Account and multiplier analysis, which are treated in detail later in this chapter. It can, however, be reductionist: reducing the tourist experience to expenditure flows misses the cultural and psychological dimensions of travel.

**The Sociological Approach** studies tourism as a **social phenomenon**: the meanings travellers attach to travel, the host–guest relationship, the commodification of culture, and the social stratification of travel access. Its root is **sociology and anthropology**. The sociological approach is essential for understanding overtourism, cultural authenticity, and the politics of representation, but it is less concerned with firm-level profitability or macroeconomic aggregates.

**The Geographical Approach** examines the **spatial dimensions** of tourism: the location of attractions and accommodation, the spatial patterns of tourist flows, regional development disparities, and the environmental carrying capacity of destinations. Its root is **geography**. The geographical approach underpins destination planning and land-use regulation, and it provides the spatial vocabulary for Leiper's model.

**The Interdisciplinary and Systems Approach** treats tourism as a **complex, adaptive system** that cannot be reduced to any single discipline. It draws on economics, sociology, geography, management, environmental science, and political science simultaneously. Leiper's Whole Tourism System Model is the canonical example. This approach is the most intellectually ambitious and the most faithful to the phenomenon's complexity, but it demands methodological pluralism and can be difficult to operationalise in a single research project.

**Table 1.2: Approaches to the Study of Tourism**

| Approach | Core Focus | Primary Disciplinary Root |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional | Organisations, intermediaries, industry structure | Business / Management |
| Product | Tourism products, marketing, service design | Marketing |
| Historical | Origins, evolution, path dependence | History |
| Managerial | Firm-level operations and strategy | Management (microeconomics) |
| Economic | Income, employment, foreign exchange, multipliers | Economics |
| Sociological | Host–guest relations, cultural meaning, social access | Sociology / Anthropology |
| Geographical | Spatial patterns, location, carrying capacity | Geography |
| Interdisciplinary / Systems | Whole-system interactions, feedback loops | Multiple disciplines (systems science) |

No single approach "owns" tourism. The mark of a well-trained tourism professional is the ability to **switch registers**: to move from a managerial discussion of hotel revenue per available room to an economic discussion of leakage, to a sociological discussion of cultural commodification, without losing sight of the whole system.

## 1.3 Economic Importance, Benefits, and Costs of Tourism

### 1.3.1 Direct, Indirect, and Induced Contributions

Tourism's economic footprint is conventionally decomposed into three layers. **Direct contributions** arise from the **first-round expenditure** of tourists: payments to hotels, restaurants, airlines, attractions, and retailers. These expenditures generate value added and employment in the industries that directly serve visitors. **Indirect contributions** arise when direct tourism businesses purchase **inputs from other firms** in the domestic supply chain: a hotel buys vegetables from a local farmer, laundry services from a local supplier, and furniture from a domestic manufacturer. These inter-industry purchases generate further output and employment. **Induced contributions** arise when the **households that earn income** from direct and indirect tourism activity spend that income on goods and services in the wider economy—housing, education, healthcare, and consumption goods.

The **tourism multiplier** is the number by which an initial injection of tourist expenditure is multiplied to yield the total (direct plus indirect plus induced) effect on output, income, or employment. A multiplier of 1.8, for example, implies that every $1 million of tourist expenditure generates $1.8 million in total economic activity. Multipliers are larger when the destination has **strong intersectoral linkages** (so that tourism businesses source locally) and smaller when the destination is heavily dependent on imports (because expenditure leaks out before it can circulate). The import content of tourism supply and the size of the multiplier are therefore **inversely related**.

### 1.3.2 Tangible Economic Benefits

**Foreign currency earnings.** Inbound tourism is an **invisible export**: foreign visitors pay for services consumed domestically, generating foreign exchange without shipping physical goods abroad. For countries with limited manufacturing export capacity, tourism can be a significant source of hard currency. Unlike commodity exports, tourism receipts are not subject to the same volatility of world commodity prices, though they are vulnerable to exchange-rate movements, security shocks, and pandemics.

**Job creation.** Tourism is **labour-intensive**, particularly in accommodation, food service, and attractions. It creates employment across a wide skill spectrum—from housekeeping and waiting staff to tour guides, revenue managers, and destination marketers. It also generates **indirect employment** in agriculture, construction, transport, and handicrafts. For economies with surplus labour and limited industrial absorption capacity, tourism's labour intensity is a major policy attraction.

**Tax revenues.** Tourism generates tax revenue through **direct taxes** on tourism businesses and their employees, **indirect taxes** such as value-added tax on accommodation and meals, **departure taxes** and visa fees, and **property taxes** on hotel real estate. These revenues can fund public infrastructure and destination management, though the fiscal yield depends heavily on the tax regime and on the informal economy's share of tourism activity.

**Stimulation of regional development.** Tourism can bring economic activity to **peripheral regions** that lack comparative advantage in manufacturing or commercial agriculture. Coastal areas, mountain regions, and heritage towns may possess tourism assets—landscapes, cultural traditions, monuments—that have limited value in other sectors. Tourism can thus counteract spatial inequality, provided that infrastructure and investment are directed beyond the capital city.

**The multiplier effect.** As discussed above, tourist expenditure circulates through the economy, generating successive rounds of income and employment. The multiplier is not a free lunch—its size depends on the structure of the local economy—but it captures the important insight that tourism's economic significance exceeds the direct receipts recorded at hotel front desks.

### 1.3.3 Hidden Economic Costs and Risks

**Economic leakage.** Not all tourist expenditure remains in the destination economy. **Import leakage** occurs when tourism businesses import goods and services—food, beverages, fuel, construction materials, furniture, even labour—because local supply is unavailable, inadequate, or uncompetitive. **Factor leakage** (or *export leakage*) occurs when the **profits, dividends, and repatriated salaries** of foreign-owned tourism firms flow out of the destination, or when external debt repayments absorb tourism earnings. In extreme cases, leakage rates of 50–75% have been observed, meaning that only a fraction of gross tourism receipts remains in the local economy. High leakage fundamentally undermines the claim that tourism is a development panacea.

**Inflationary pressures.** Tourism demand can drive up **local prices** for land, housing, food, and services. Hotel and resort development bids up land values, displacing agricultural use and making housing unaffordable for residents. Restaurants and shops orient their offerings toward tourist willingness-to-pay, raising the cost of living for host communities. This **cost-push inflation** is a regressive transfer from residents to tourism businesses and landlords, and it is a common source of local resentment.

**Heavy infrastructure maintenance costs.** Tourism requires **public infrastructure**—airports, roads, water supply, sanitation, electricity, waste management, security—that must be maintained even during off-peak seasons. The **fixed costs** of infrastructure are borne by taxpayers, while the **variable revenues** accrue disproportionately to private tourism firms. In destinations with pronounced seasonality, infrastructure may be underutilised for much of the year, imposing a permanent fiscal burden.

**Dangerous over-reliance on a seasonal, volatile single sector.** Tourism demand is **income-elastic** (it falls sharply in recessions), **security-sensitive** (terrorism, political instability, and health crises can collapse arrivals overnight), and **seasonal** (climatic and holiday-calendar patterns concentrate demand into narrow windows). An economy that becomes heavily dependent on tourism exposes itself to **exogenous shocks** for which it has limited domestic stabilisers. The COVID-19 pandemic provided a stark demonstration: international arrivals collapsed by over 70% in 2020, devastating destinations that had no diversified export base. Over-reliance is therefore a **structural vulnerability**, not merely a cyclical inconvenience.

**Table 1.3: Economic Benefits versus Economic Costs of Tourism**

| Economic Benefits | Economic Costs and Risks |
|---|---|
| Foreign currency earnings (invisible export) | Import leakage (goods, services, fuel, labour) |
| Job creation (labour-intensive, wide skill spectrum) | Factor leakage (repatriated profits, debt service) |
| Tax revenues (direct, indirect, departure taxes) | Inflationary pressure on land, housing, and local prices |
| Regional development in peripheral areas | Heavy fixed costs of infrastructure maintenance |
| Multiplier effects (indirect and induced activity) | Over-reliance on a seasonal, volatile sector |
| Stimulation of local supply chains | Opportunity cost of investment diverted from other sectors |
| Entrepreneurship and SME development | Displacement of traditional livelihoods |

The policy implication is not that tourism is "good" or "bad," but that its **net developmental contribution is conditional** on leakage containment, supply-chain deepening, infrastructure planning, and demand diversification. Tourism that is well governed can be a powerful engine of inclusive growth; tourism that is poorly governed can be a mechanism for extracting value from host communities while externalising costs onto them.

## 1.4 Tourism Through the Ages: A Historical Evolution

### 1.4.1 Early Beginnings: Sumerians, Phoenicians, and Egyptians

The historical roots of travel lie in **trade and administration**, not leisure. The **Sumerians** of Mesopotamia (c. 4000–2000 BCE) developed three innovations of enduring significance for travel: **monetary systems** (standardised weights and silver-based exchange), the **wheel** (which revolutionised overland transport), and **paved tracks** (early road infrastructure). These were not "tourism" innovations in the modern sense, but they reduced the transaction costs of movement and made long-distance journeys conceivable.

The **Phoenicians** (c. 1500–300 BCE), operating from ports along the eastern Mediterranean, became the ancient world's foremost maritime traders. Their voyages established **trade routes** that connected the Levant, North Africa, and the western Mediterranean, and their port cities functioned as early **transit hubs**. Travel in this era was overwhelmingly **commercial and diplomatic**; leisure travel was confined to a tiny elite and was rarely distinguished from pilgrimage or official mission.

**Ancient Egypt** contributed two important travel patterns. First, **shrines and temples** attracted pilgrims and the curious, creating early forms of **religious visitation**. Second, **trade festivals** and periodic markets drew merchants and buyers from considerable distances, establishing the principle that a **temporal event** can generate travel demand. Egyptian travellers left **graffiti and votive inscriptions** at monuments—ancient analogues of the tourist photograph—indicating that the experience of "being there" already carried social meaning.

### 1.4.2 Early and Later Tourist Attractions: The Seven Wonders, Greek Festivals, and Roman Travel

The **Seven Wonders of the Ancient World**—the Great Pyramid of Giza, the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, the Statue of Zeus at Olympia, the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, the Colossus of Rhodes, and the Lighthouse of Alexandria—were not merely architectural achievements. They were **proto-tourist attractions**: sites that travellers deliberately went to see, and that were catalogued and ranked in what can reasonably be called the earliest **guidebooks**. The very act of listing "wonders" implies a **comparative evaluative gaze**—the recognition that some places are worth travelling to see because they are extraordinary.

**Greek civilisation** added two institutions that shaped tourism for millennia. The **Olympic Games**, held every four years at Olympia from 776 BCE, created a **periodic mega-event** that attracted athletes, spectators, merchants, and diplomats from across the Greek world. The Games were accompanied by **theatre festivals**, religious processions, and markets—an integrated **event-tourism package** avant la lettre. Greek **theatre festivals** at Athens and elsewhere likewise drew audiences and performers, establishing the principle that **cultural performance** can be a travel motivator.

The **Roman Empire** represents the first great flourishing of **organised leisure travel** in the West. Roman engineers built an extensive network of **paved highways**—some 80,000 kilometres at the Empire's height—that linked the provinces to Rome and to one another. Wealthy Romans maintained **villas** in the countryside and at the coast, and developed **seaside resorts** such as Baiae on the Bay of Naples. They also patronised **medicinal mineral spas**, seeking therapeutic relief in thermal waters from Britain to Anatolia. The Romans travelled for **pleasure, health, and culture**, visiting Greek monuments and Olympic festivals, and they developed a rudimentary **hospitality infrastructure**—*mansiones* (official rest stops) and *cauponae* (inns)—along the roads. The Roman experience demonstrates that leisure tourism is not a modern invention; it is a **recurrent phenomenon** that flourishes when prosperity, security, and transport infrastructure coincide.

### 1.4.3 Religious Pilgrimages

Pilgrimage has been one of the most durable and widespread motives for travel. The **Islamic Hajj**—the annual pilgrimage to Mecca, obligatory for able-bodied Muslims who can afford it—has mobilised millions of travellers across fourteen centuries. The Hajj is remarkable for its **scale, regularity, and logistical complexity**: it requires the coordinated movement of pilgrims from every continent, the provision of accommodation and food for a temporary city, and the management of public health and safety on a massive scale. It is, in effect, a **global tourism system** with a sacred purpose.

In **medieval Europe**, pilgrimage to **Rome** (the seat of Latin Christendom), **Jerusalem** (the Holy Land), and **Santiago de Compostela** (the shrine of St James in north-western Spain) generated a continuous flow of travellers. The **Camino de Santiago** developed an infrastructure of **hospices, monasteries, bridges, and way-marked routes** that anticipates the modern long-distance trail. Pilgrimage was not purely devotional: it involved **commercial transactions** (inns, food, souvenirs, relics), **social mixing** across classes, and **personal transformation narratives** that resonate strongly with contemporary "spiritual tourism" and "wellness travel".

### 1.4.4 The Grand Tour Era

Between the late sixteenth and the early nineteenth centuries, the **Grand Tour** emerged as a defining institution of aristocratic education in northern Europe, particularly England. Young men of high social standing—accompanied by tutors, servants, and sometimes artists—undertook extended journeys through **France, Italy, and Switzerland**, with Paris, Venice, Florence, Rome, and Naples as canonical destinations. The purpose was **educational and cultural**: to acquire fluency in languages, familiarity with classical antiquities, connoisseurship in art and architecture, and the social polish expected of a gentleman. Richard Lassels, who is generally credited with coining the term in 1670, described the Grand Tour as a "necessary capstone" to a classical education.

The Grand Tour is the **direct precursor of modern cultural tourism**. It established several enduring patterns: the **itinerary** as a curated sequence of cultural sites; the **travel diary and correspondence** as instruments of social display; the **purchase of art and antiquities** as a marker of taste; and the association of travel with **self-formation**. It also established the **class and gender exclusivity** of early leisure travel: the Grand Tourist was wealthy, male, and European, and the tour was financed by the labour of servants and the rents of estates. The democratisation of the Grand Tour ideal—its extension to the middle classes through railways, and eventually to mass markets through aviation—is one of the central narratives of tourism history.

### 1.4.5 Early Economic References

Classical political economists were attentive to travel expenditure, although they did not develop a theory of tourism as such. **Adam Smith**, **David Ricardo**, and their contemporaries observed that **travel spending abroad** functioned as a debit in the balance of trade, and that the "expense of travelling" could affect national wealth. The **mercantilist** tradition, which dominated economic thought before Smith, viewed outbound travel expenditure as a drain on the nation's stock of bullion; later classical economists were more nuanced, recognising that travel could stimulate **knowledge transfer, commercial connections, and cultural capital** even as it transferred income abroad. The classical economists' scattered observations on travel anticipate the modern concern with **tourism balance of payments** and **foreign exchange leakage**, though they lacked the statistical apparatus to measure these flows systematically.

### 1.4.6 The First Travel Agents: Thomas Cook and His Rivals

The modern **travel agency** and the **package tour** emerged in the nineteenth century, and their emblematic figure is **Thomas Cook**. On **5 July 1841**, Cook organised a **charter train** from Leicester to Loughborough for a temperance meeting, carrying some 570 passengers at a fare of one shilling each, with a picnic lunch and a brass band. This excursion is conventionally treated as the birth of the **organised package tour**: Cook assembled transport, catering, and a social programme into a single purchasable product. The venture was successful enough that Cook expanded into regular excursions, circular tours of Scotland, and eventually **international tours**.

Cook's innovations extended beyond the charter train. He developed a **"Circular System" of tickets**, **hotel coupons**, and **circular notes**—paper instruments that allowed travellers to obtain local currency and pay for accommodation without carrying cash, and which are the direct ancestors of the **travellers' cheque**. He also pioneered the **foreign currency exchange** function of travel agencies and the practice of **escorting groups** with a professional courier. Cook's genius lay not in inventing any single component of travel but in **integrating** them into a system that made travel accessible to the **middle classes**—teachers, clerks, Dissenters, and later working-class excursionists—who had previously been excluded from the aristocratic Grand Tour.

Cook's early rivals included **American Express**, founded in 1850 by **Henry Wells**, **William Fargo**, and John Butterfield. American Express began as a **freight and valuables forwarding company** and evolved into a travel-finance and travel-services giant, issuing **travellers' cheques** and operating a global network of offices. Wells and Fargo also founded **Wells Fargo & Co.** , which served the American West and became an icon of stagecoach travel and frontier logistics. The rivalry between Cook and American Express established the template for **global travel intermediation**: a contest between a European tour-operator model and an American financial-services model.

### 1.4.7 Historic Transportation

The history of tourism is inseparable from the history of **transport technology**. Each transport revolution lowered the **cost, time, and discomfort** of movement, and each expansion of mobility enlarged the pool of potential tourists.

**Stagecoaches and turnpikes.** In seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Europe, **stagecoaches** provided scheduled passenger service along **turnpike roads** (roads financed by tolls). The stagecoach was slow, expensive, and uncomfortable, but it regularised long-distance travel and created a network of **coaching inns** that served as nodes of hospitality and information.

**Passenger steam railways.** The opening of the **Liverpool and Manchester Railway** in 1830 inaugurated the railway age, and within two decades railways had transformed travel from an elite privilege into a **mass market**. Railways offered **speed, reliability, and scale**; they ran to schedules, carried large numbers of passengers, and stimulated the growth of **resort towns** at the ends of the line. Thomas Cook's charter train was a direct application of railway capacity to the tourism market.

**Transatlantic steam ocean liners.** From the mid-nineteenth century, **steam-powered ocean liners** connected Europe and North America with increasing speed and regularity. The liner trade created a **transatlantic tourism corridor** and a distinctive **onboard hospitality culture**—classes, dining, entertainment—that prefigured the cruise industry. The great liners of the early twentieth century (the *Titanic*, the *Queen Mary*, the *Normandie*) were floating hotels as much as transport vessels.

**Commercial aviation and the Jet Age.** The decisive break came with **commercial aviation**. After the Second World War, surplus **propeller aircraft** (DC-3s, Constellations) were converted to civilian use, and airlines began to offer **affordable international travel** on a significant scale. The introduction of the **Boeing 707** in **1958** inaugurated the **Jet Age**: jet aircraft were faster, larger, and more economical per passenger-kilometre than piston-engine planes, and they made **mass long-haul tourism** economically viable. The subsequent development of **wide-body jets** (Boeing 747, 1970), **deregulation** (US Airline Deregulation Act, 1978), and **low-cost carriers** (from the 1990s) progressively drove down fares and democratised air travel. The modern tourism system—with its global generating regions, long-haul destination regions, and aviation-dependent transit routes—is inconceivable without the Jet Age.

**Personal automobiles and highway network expansion.** The **mass-produced automobile** (Ford Model T, 1908) and the subsequent construction of **highway networks**—the German Autobahn, the US Interstate Highway System (1956), and national road programmes elsewhere—created a parallel **car-based tourism system**. The automobile offered **flexibility, privacy, and door-to-door mobility**, and it spawned a distinctive accommodation form: the **motel** (motor hotel), located on the roadside and oriented to the driving traveller. Car-based tourism remains dominant in domestic markets, particularly in large countries such as the United States, China, and India.

### 1.4.8 Evolution of Accommodations

**Caravanserais.** Along the **Silk Road** and the trade routes of the Middle East and Central Asia, **caravanserais**—large walled inns designed to shelter merchants, their servants, and their animals—provided secure overnight accommodation. They were typically spaced a day's journey apart, fortified against bandits, and equipped with stabling, water, and a courtyard for commerce. The caravanserai is the **ancestor of the hotel** in the Islamic world and a powerful symbol of hospitality as a **sacred duty** as well as a commercial service.

**Coaching inns.** In Europe, the **coaching inn** served the stagecoach network. It offered food, drink, lodging, and fresh horses, and it functioned as a **social centre**—a place where travellers exchanged news and locals gathered. The coaching inn was the principal accommodation institution of the pre-railway era.

**Nineteenth-century luxury grand hotels.** The **Tremont House**, opened in Boston in **1829**, is widely regarded as the first modern hotel: it offered **170 private rooms**, indoor plumbing, locks on doors, and a **lobby** as a social space, establishing the template for the **grand hotel**. The **Ritz Hotel** in Paris, opened in **1898** by César Ritz, became the emblem of **luxury hospitality**, with its emphasis on personalised service, culinary excellence, and opulent interiors. The grand hotel era was an era of **railway hotels, resort hotels, and urban palaces**, catering to the wealth created by industrial capitalism.

**Twentieth-century motel chains.** The **motel** emerged with the automobile, but it was **Kemmons Wilson** who transformed it into a **chain**. After a disappointing family road trip in 1951, Wilson opened the first **Holiday Inn** in Memphis, Tennessee, in **1952**, offering standardised, family-friendly rooms with predictable quality at roadside locations. Holiday Inn pioneered **chain standardisation, franchising, and centralised reservation systems** (Holidex, 1965), and by 1968 it operated 1,000 properties. The motel chain demonstrated that **brand consistency** could substitute for the personalised knowledge of the grand hotel, and it made reliable accommodation accessible to the emerging **middle-class automobile traveller**.

**Integrated casino resorts.** In the late twentieth century, the **integrated resort** emerged, most spectacularly in **Las Vegas** and **Macau**. These are large-scale complexes that combine **gaming, hotels, restaurants, entertainment, retail, and convention facilities** into a single destination. The integrated resort represents the **convergence of hospitality, entertainment, and real-estate development**, and it has become a dominant model for tourism-led urban development in Asia and beyond. It also raises distinctive **regulatory and social-policy questions** that are beyond the scope of this chapter but essential to professional tourism management.

## 1.5 Bangladesh Contextual Vignette: The Evolution of Travel in Bengal

Bengal's travel history is **older and richer** than the modern boundaries of Bangladesh might suggest. The fertile delta, with its **riverine arteries** and access to the Bay of Bengal, was a crossroads of trade, pilgrimage, and scholarship long before the emergence of the modern state. **Banglaphilia** — the love of Bengal — is not a modern tourism slogan; it describes a pattern of attraction that drew travellers from across Asia and Europe for centuries.

**Ancient and medieval travellers.** **Pliny the Elder** (first century CE) referred to **Tamralipti**, a port in the Gangetic delta, as a significant trading emporium; **Ptolemy** (second century CE) described the **Gangaridai** region. The Chinese Buddhist pilgrims **Fa-Hien** (fifth century) and **Hiuen Tsang** (seventh century) visited Bengal's **Buddhist monasteries and seats of learning**, including **Mahasthan, Samatata, Karnasuvarna, and Tamralipti**. The Moroccan traveller **Ibn Battuta** reached **Chittagong and Sonargaon** in the fourteenth century, and his travelogue records the region's prosperity, its textile trade, and its hospitality. Later travellers included **Ma Huan** and **Fei Xin** of China, **Duarte Barbosa** of Portugal, and **Niccolao Manucci** of Italy—a cosmopolitan parade of merchants, pilgrims, diplomats, and chroniclers that demonstrates Bengal's integration into **transregional travel networks**.

**British-era transformations.** Colonial rule reorganised Bengal's travel geography around **railways, hill stations, and port cities**. The **Assam Bengal Railway** and the **Sylhet–Akhaura line** (laid in 1898) connected the tea districts of Sylhet and Assam to the port of Chittagong, facilitating the movement of **tea planters, officials, and goods**. **Hill stations** such as **Sylhet** and the **Chittagong Hill Tracts** attracted colonial officials and their families seeking respite from the heat of the plains, establishing a pattern of **seasonal leisure migration** that persists in Bangladeshi domestic tourism today. **Railway hotels** and **inspection bungalows** provided accommodation for travelling officials and businessmen. The British also developed **Cox's Bazar** as a seaside retreat, though its transformation into a mass-market destination would await the post-independence era.

**Post-independence institutionalisation.** After the independence of Bangladesh in 1971, tourism was formally recognised as a sector of national importance. On **27 November 1972**, **Presidential Order No. 143** established the **Bangladesh Parjatan Corporation (BPC)** as the national tourism organisation, tasked with developing, promoting, and regulating tourism. The BPC began operations in 1973 and remained the sole national tourism body until the establishment of the **Bangladesh Tourism Board** in **2010**. The government recognised tourism as an **industry** in **1999**, and subsequent policy instruments—the **Tourism Development Policy (2010)** , the **Bangladesh Tourism Reserved Area and Special Tourism Zone Act (2010)** , and the **Bangladesh Tourism Board Act (2010)** —provided a modern legal architecture.

**Contemporary patterns.** Bangladesh's tourism sector today is **domestically dominated**: Cox's Bazar, the Sundarbans, Sylhet's tea gardens, and the Hill Tracts attract large volumes of Bangladeshi visitors, while inbound international tourism remains modest relative to the country's population and potential. The sector faces familiar challenges: **infrastructure deficits, seasonal concentration, leakage of tourism expenditure through imports, and the absence of a fully operational Tourism Satellite Account** to measure the sector's contribution rigorously. Yet the historical record—from the caravans of the Silk Road to the pilgrims at Mahasthan, from the railway age to the digital age—suggests that Bengal's position at the intersection of **riverine, maritime, and overland routes** is a durable asset. The task of contemporary tourism management is to convert that asset into **sustainable, inclusive, and well-measured development**.

## 1.6 Methodological/Analytical Framework: Measuring Tourism's Economic Contribution through the Tourism Satellite Account (TSA)

Governments cannot manage what they cannot measure. The **Tourism Satellite Account (TSA)** is the international standard for measuring tourism's **direct economic contribution** in a way that is consistent with the **System of National Accounts (SNA)** . It is "satellite" because it orbits the central national accounts, extracting and reorganising tourism-related data without disturbing the core GDP aggregates. The TSA is the authoritative source for the **direct** contribution of tourism to value added and GDP; indirect and induced effects must be estimated through **input–output or computable general equilibrium models** that build on the TSA's supply-and-use tables.

The TSA methodology proceeds through the following logical steps.

**Step 1: Define tourism characteristic products and industries.** The TSA begins by identifying the **goods and services** that are typically consumed by visitors—accommodation, food and beverage serving, passenger transport, travel agency services, cultural services, sports and recreation, and so on—and the **industries** that produce them. Not every restaurant meal is a tourism purchase; the TSA must isolate the **tourism share** of each product's total consumption.

**Step 2: Measure tourism consumption.** The TSA compiles **internal tourism consumption**, which comprises **inbound tourism expenditure** (spending by non-resident visitors within the territory) and **domestic tourism expenditure** (spending by resident visitors within the territory). This requires **visitor surveys, border surveys, and administrative data** on accommodation occupancy, transport receipts, and attraction admissions.

**Step 3: Construct a supply-and-use table for tourism.** The TSA aligns tourism consumption with **domestic supply** (output by resident industries), **imports**, **taxes less subsidies**, and **trade and transport margins**. The **tourism ratio**—the share of each product's total supply that is absorbed by tourism consumption—is calculated for each product and industry. This ratio is the key analytical device: it allows the statistician to attribute a proportion of a hotel's output, a restaurant's output, or an airline's output to tourism rather than to local consumption.

**Step 4: Calculate tourism direct gross value added (TDGVA) and tourism direct GDP (TDGDP).** By applying the tourism ratios to each industry's **gross value added** (output minus intermediate consumption), the TSA derives **TDGVA**—the value added generated by tourism-characteristic industries in serving visitors. **TDGDP** adds net taxes on tourism products to TDGVA. The critical point is that the TSA measures **direct** effects only: it captures the value added by hotels, restaurants, airlines, and attractions in their transactions with visitors, but **not** the value added by the farmer who supplies vegetables to a hotel or the laundry that supplies sheets. Those are **indirect** effects and lie outside the TSA's direct measurement boundary.

**Step 5: Estimate indirect and induced effects (outside the core TSA).** To estimate the **indirect** contribution, analysts use **input–output tables** to trace the supply-chain purchases of tourism industries: the hotel buys food, the farmer buys fertiliser, the fertiliser producer buys energy, and so on. The **induced** contribution is estimated by modelling how the **households** that earn income from direct and indirect tourism activity spend that income on consumption. The sum of direct, indirect, and induced effects yields the **total economic contribution** of tourism, which is always larger than the TSA's direct figure.

**Step 6: Measure tourism employment.** The TSA framework includes a **tourism employment module** that estimates the number of persons employed in tourism-characteristic industries, adjusted by tourism ratios, plus a **tourism employment ratio** for the economy as a whole. This is essential for policy because tourism's **labour intensity** is one of its principal developmental arguments.

**Step 7: Reconcile and validate.** The TSA must be reconciled with the **balance of payments** (for inbound and outbound tourism expenditure), the **government accounts** (for tourism-related taxes and expenditure), and the **SNA** aggregates. Discrepancies are investigated and documented. The credibility of the TSA depends on **transparent methodology and consistent time series**, not on the precision of any single estimate.

The policy value of the TSA is considerable: it allows governments to compare tourism's direct contribution with that of agriculture, manufacturing, or financial services on a **like-for-like basis**; it identifies **leakage-prone products** where import dependence is high; and it provides the evidentiary foundation for **tourism taxation, investment incentives, and infrastructure planning**. For a country such as Bangladesh, where tourism's contribution is often asserted but poorly measured, the TSA framework offers a disciplined path from anecdote to evidence.

### 1.7.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions (with Model Answers)

**Question 1. Distinguish between a tourist and an excursionist according to UNWTO criteria, and explain why the distinction matters for destination management.**

**Model Answer.** Under the IRTS 2008, both tourists and excursionists are **visitors**—travellers who go outside their usual environment for less than one year for a purpose other than employment by a resident entity. The distinction turns on **overnight stay**: a **tourist** stays for at least one night, while an **excursionist** (same-day visitor) completes the visit without an overnight stay. The distinction matters because the two categories generate **different expenditure profiles and different infrastructure demands**. Overnight tourists purchase accommodation, breakfast, dinner, and multiple days of attractions, whereas excursionists typically purchase meals, admissions, and local transport but not beds. A destination that measures only "visitor arrivals" may overstate its economic yield if arrivals are dominated by excursionists. Conversely, excursionists can fill daytime capacity without requiring additional hotel stock. Destination managers must therefore track **both volume and value**, and design products that convert excursionists into overnight visitors where accommodation capacity exists.

---

**Question 2. Explain the five elements of Leiper's Whole Tourism System Model and why the model is described as "holistic."**

**Model Answer.** Leiper's model comprises **five interacting elements**: (1) the **tourist**—the human actor whose decision activates the system; (2) the **traveller-generating region**—the origin market that supplies tourists and shapes demand; (3) the **transit route**—the spatial and functional link between generating and destination regions; (4) the **destination region**—the location of attractions, accommodation, and the host community; and (5) the **tourism industry**—the collection of firms and organisations that provide services to tourists. The model is **holistic** because it insists that tourism cannot be understood by studying any single element in isolation. Changes in one element propagate through the others: a recession in the generating region reduces demand at the destination; a new transit route opens a destination to new markets; leakage from the destination weakens the local economy and degrades the tourist experience, which in turn affects future demand. The model also recognises that the tourism industry is **partially industrialised**, with formal firms, informal providers, and public agencies all playing roles. This systemic perspective is the foundation of contemporary destination management.

---

**Question 3. What is economic leakage in tourism? Distinguish between import leakage and factor leakage, and explain one policy measure a government could use to reduce each.**

**Model Answer.** **Economic leakage** is the proportion of tourism expenditure that does not remain in the destination economy but instead flows out through imports, repatriated profits, debt service, or other external channels. **Import leakage** occurs when tourism businesses purchase goods and services from abroad—food, beverages, fuel, construction materials, furniture, or even labour—because local supply is inadequate or uncompetitive. A policy measure to reduce import leakage is **supply-chain development**: providing incentives and technical assistance for local farmers, food processors, and manufacturers to meet the quality, volume, and reliability standards demanded by hotels and restaurants, thereby substituting domestic inputs for imports. **Factor leakage** occurs when the **profits, dividends, management fees, and repatriated salaries** of foreign-owned tourism firms leave the destination, or when external debt repayments absorb tourism earnings. A policy measure to reduce factor leakage is **local ownership and joint-venture requirements**: structuring investment incentives to favour domestic equity participation, management training, and profit retention, while ensuring that foreign investment brings genuine technology and market access rather than merely extracting rents.

**Question 4. Outline the principal innovations of Thomas Cook and explain why he is regarded as the founder of modern tourism.**

**Model Answer.** Thomas Cook organised a **charter train** from Leicester to Loughborough on 5 July 1841, carrying some 570 passengers at a fare of one shilling each, with a picnic lunch and a brass band. This is conventionally treated as the first **package tour**: Cook assembled transport, catering, and a social programme into a single purchasable product. He subsequently developed a **"Circular System" of tickets**, **hotel coupons**, and **circular notes**—paper instruments that allowed travellers to pay for accommodation and obtain local currency without carrying cash, and which are the ancestors of the **travellers' cheque**. He also pioneered **escorted group travel**, **foreign currency exchange** within travel agencies, and **printed itineraries and guidebooks**. Cook is regarded as the founder of modern tourism because he **integrated** these components into a **repeatable system** that made travel accessible to the **middle classes**, not merely to aristocrats. His model of the **tour operator as intermediary**—assembling and wholesaling travel components—remains the structural foundation of the contemporary tourism industry.

---

**Question 5. Explain the difference between the direct and indirect economic contributions of tourism, and why the Tourism Satellite Account measures only the direct contribution.**

**Model Answer.** The **direct contribution** of tourism consists of the **value added and employment generated by tourism-characteristic industries in their transactions with visitors**—hotels, restaurants, airlines, attractions, and travel agencies. The **indirect contribution** arises when those direct tourism businesses purchase **inputs from other domestic firms**—food, laundry, furniture, utilities—thereby generating output and employment in the supply chain. The **induced contribution** arises when the households earning income from direct and indirect activity spend that income in the wider economy. The **TSA** measures only the **direct** contribution because its purpose is to provide a **rigorous, SNA-consistent** account of tourism's value added that can be compared on a like-for-like basis with other industries. Measuring indirect and induced effects requires **behavioural assumptions and modelling** (input–output multipliers, household expenditure propensities) that go beyond the TSA's statistical boundary. The TSA is therefore the **authoritative foundation** for direct measurement, while indirect and induced effects are estimated through **satellite modelling** that builds on TSA data.

**Question 6. Why is over-reliance on tourism described as a "structural vulnerability"? Illustrate with reference to seasonality and external shocks.**

**Model Answer.** Tourism demand is **income-elastic**, **security-sensitive**, and **seasonal**. When a national or regional economy becomes heavily dependent on tourism, these characteristics transmit **exogenous shocks** directly into the domestic economy. **Seasonality** concentrates demand into narrow windows, creating **underemployment** in the off-season and **capacity strain** in the peak season; infrastructure must be sized for the peak but is underutilised for much of the year. **External shocks**—recessions, terrorist attacks, political instability, epidemics, and pandemics—can cause **abrupt and severe collapses** in arrivals and receipts. The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated this vividly: international arrivals fell by more than 70% in 2020, devastating destinations that lacked a diversified export base. Because tourism cannot be "stored" or "inventoried," a demand shock translates almost immediately into lost revenue and unemployment. Over-reliance is therefore a **structural** vulnerability, not merely a cyclical downturn: it reflects an **economic specialisation** that exposes the destination to risks beyond its control. Diversification into other tradable sectors, **domestic demand stabilisers**, and **seasonality-smoothing policies** are the appropriate responses.

(c) A tourist paying departure tax at the airport
(d) A local farmer selling vegetables to a hotel

**Answer: (b).** Import leakage occurs when tourism businesses purchase goods and services from abroad. Repatriated profits are an example of **factor leakage** (option a). Departure tax is a government revenue, not a leakage. A local farmer selling to a hotel is a **domestic supply linkage**, which reduces leakage.

### 1.7.4 Applied Analytical Case Scenarios (with Model Solutions)

**Case Scenario 1: The Island Resort and the Leakage Trap**

*Coral Bay* is a small island destination in the Indian Ocean. It receives 120,000 international tourists per year, generating $300 million in gross tourism receipts. However, 65% of the resort's food and beverage inputs are imported, 80% of managerial staff are foreign nationals whose salaries are repatriated, and the resort is owned by a foreign chain that remits 70% of profits to its headquarters. The government has identified tourism as its primary development strategy and plans to double arrivals within five years.

**Questions:**
(a) Calculate the approximate leakage rate implied by the scenario, and explain its implications for the government's development strategy.
(b) Identify two policy measures the government could adopt to reduce import leakage and two to reduce factor leakage.
(c) What risks does the planned doubling of arrivals pose if leakage is not addressed?

**Model Solution.**

(a) The scenario describes multiple leakage channels. Import leakage: 65% of food and beverage inputs are imported. Factor leakage: 80% of managerial salaries are repatriated, and 70% of profits are remitted abroad. While a precise aggregate leakage rate would require expenditure weights, the pattern clearly implies that **well over half** of gross receipts may leave the economy. The implication is that **gross receipts substantially overstate net developmental benefit**. Doubling arrivals without reducing leakage would double the **gross** figure while leaving the **net** benefit to the local economy proportionally unchanged or even diminished (if infrastructure costs rise faster than retained value).

(b) **Import leakage measures:** (i) **Supply-chain development**—provide extension services, cold-chain infrastructure, and quality certification to local farmers and food processors so that they can meet hotel procurement standards; (ii) **Local content requirements**—require resorts above a certain size to source a minimum percentage of food, beverages, and furnishings domestically, with phased targets and technical support. **Factor leakage measures:** (i) **Local ownership and joint-venture requirements**—structure investment approvals to favour domestic equity participation and profit retention; (ii) **Management training and localisation mandates**—require foreign operators to fund and implement a timetable for replacing expatriate managers with trained local staff.

(c) Without addressing leakage, doubling arrivals would **exacerbate** the structural problems: infrastructure costs (airport expansion, water, waste) would be borne by the public purse while the incremental revenue would largely flow abroad; land and housing prices would rise, displacing residents; and the destination would become more **dependent** on a volatile external market without building a **domestic economic base**. The government's strategy would generate **growth without development**—a larger tourism sector with a proportionally smaller local developmental dividend.

---

**Case Scenario 2: The Heritage City and the Four Components**

*Historic City* is a UNESCO World Heritage Site in South Asia. Its old town attracts 500,000 visitors per year, mostly day-trippers from the capital. Residents complain that the narrow streets are congested, that rents have risen sharply, and that the new cafés and souvenir shops cater exclusively to tourists. The municipal government wants to increase tourism revenue; hotel developers want to build a 200-room luxury hotel inside the heritage zone; heritage conservationists oppose the development; and a residents' association is demanding a cap on visitor numbers.

**Questions:**
(a) Analyse the positions of the four core components of tourism management in this scenario.
(b) Propose a management strategy that could align their interests, identifying specific mechanisms.
(c) How would a Tourism Satellite Account help the municipal government make a more informed decision?

**Model Solution.**

(a) **Tourists:** Seek access to the heritage site, but the day-tripper profile means low per-visitor spending and high congestion. **Business providers:** Hotel developers want to capture higher-spending overnight visitors; existing café and shop owners benefit from visitor volume but may be displaced by rising rents. **Host government:** Wants increased revenue and prestige but faces political pressure from residents and conservationists. **Host community:** Bears congestion, inflation, and cultural disruption while capturing a limited share of tourism spending; demands a cap and a say in decision-making.

(b) A **participatory destination management plan** could align interests through: (i) **A visitor management framework** that differentiates day-trippers from overnight visitors, using timed entry, pricing, and dispersal to secondary attractions to reduce congestion; (ii) **A community benefit agreement** that channels a proportion of tourism revenue into local housing, infrastructure, and heritage conservation, and gives residents' representatives a formal role in planning; (iii) **Inclusive heritage zoning** that permits compatible small-scale accommodation and local enterprise while restricting large-format development inside the heritage core; (iv) **Local content and employment requirements** to ensure that tourism spending supports local livelihoods; (v) **Transparent monitoring** of visitor numbers, spending, and resident satisfaction, with **adaptive caps** if thresholds are breached.

(c) A **TSA** would allow the municipality to move beyond **gross visitor counts** to measure **tourism's direct value added, employment, and tax contribution**, disaggregated by product and industry. It would reveal whether the day-tripper market is **economically significant** or merely **congestive**, and it would quantify the **potential incremental value** of overnight visitors relative to the **infrastructure and conservation costs** they impose. By making these trade-offs visible, the TSA would provide a **common evidentiary language** for negotiations among the four components, reducing the scope for purely rhetorical claims.

## 1.8 Chapter Summary

This chapter has established the conceptual and historical foundations of tourism and hospitality management.

**Conceptual foundations.** Tourism is defined by UN Tourism as the activities of travellers outside their usual environment for less than one year, excluding employment by a resident entity. Visitors are classified as **tourists** (overnight) or **excursionists** (same-day), and as **international** or **domestic**. **Hospitality** is the receptive treatment of guests, overlapping with but distinct from tourism. **Leiper's Whole Tourism System Model** integrates the tourist, generating region, transit route, destination region, and tourism industry into a dynamic, open system. **Four core components**—tourist, business providers, host government, and host community—must be aligned for sustainable management. **Eight approaches** to the study of tourism—institutional, product, historical, managerial, economic, sociological, geographical, and interdisciplinary/systems—offer complementary lenses on a complex phenomenon.

**Economic dimensions.** Tourism generates **direct, indirect, and induced** contributions. Its **benefits** include foreign exchange, employment, tax revenue, regional development, and multiplier effects. Its **costs and risks** include import and factor leakage, inflation, infrastructure burden, and dangerous over-reliance on a seasonal, volatile sector. The **net developmental contribution is conditional** on governance.

**Historical evolution.** Travel began with **trade and administration** in Sumer, Phoenicia, and Egypt; **attractions and festivals** in Greece and Rome; **pilgrimage** in Islam and medieval Christianity; the **Grand Tour** of aristocratic Europe; **Thomas Cook's package tours** and the **travellers' cheque**; **transport revolutions** from stagecoach to railway, steamship, automobile, and jet aircraft; and **accommodation innovations** from caravanserai to coaching inn, grand hotel, motel chain, and integrated resort. **Bengal's** travel history—from the ports of Tamralipti and the visits of Fa-Hien, Hiuen Tsang, and Ibn Battuta, through British-era railways and hill stations, to the establishment of the **Bangladesh Parjatan Corporation in 1972**—illustrates the global patterns in a South Asian register.

**Measurement.** The **Tourism Satellite Account** provides the international standard for measuring tourism's **direct** contribution in an SNA-consistent framework, while indirect and induced effects are estimated through input–output modelling.

## 1.9 Key Terms Glossary

1. **Tourism:** The activities of travellers taking trips outside their usual environment for less than one year for purposes other than employment by a resident entity.
2. **Tourist (overnight visitor):** A visitor whose trip includes at least one overnight stay.
3. **Excursionist (same-day visitor):** A visitor whose trip does not include an overnight stay.
4. **Usual environment:** The geographical area within which an individual conducts regular life routines; travel outside this area is a precondition for being a visitor.
5. **Whole Tourism System Model:** Leiper's framework comprising the tourist, generating region, transit route, destination region, and tourism industry.
6. **Traveller-generating region:** The origin market that supplies tourists and shapes demand.
7. **Transit route:** The spatial and functional link between generating and destination regions.
8. **Destination region:** The location of attractions, accommodation, and the host community.
9. **Direct economic contribution:** Value added and employment generated by tourism-characteristic industries in transactions with visitors.
10. **Indirect economic contribution:** Value added and employment generated in the supply chain of direct tourism businesses.
11. **Induced economic contribution:** Value added and employment generated when households earning income from tourism spend it in the wider economy.
12. **Economic leakage:** The proportion of tourism expenditure that leaves the destination economy through imports, repatriated profits, or debt service.
13. **Tourism multiplier:** The number by which initial tourist expenditure is multiplied to yield total economic effects.
14. **Tourism Satellite Account (TSA):** The international statistical standard for measuring tourism's direct contribution to GDP, consistent with the System of National Accounts.
15. **Grand Tour:** The extended educational journey of young European aristocrats through France, Italy, and Switzerland, from the late sixteenth to the early nineteenth century.
