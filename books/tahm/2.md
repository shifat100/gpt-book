## 2.1 World, National, Regional, and Other Organizations

### 2.1.1 The Need for Inter-Organizational Governance

Tourism is produced by thousands of independent firms and public agencies whose actions must be **coordinated** if the system is to function coherently. No single firm can set aviation safety standards, certify hotel quality, negotiate landing rights, or compile comparable international tourism statistics. These are **collective goods** that require **inter-organizational governance**. Governance in tourism operates at four levels: **global** (UN Tourism, WTTC, ICAO, IATA), **regional** (PATA, ETC), **national** (NTOs and NTAs), and **sub-national** (state tourism departments, RTOs, DMOs, CVBs). Each level performs functions that markets alone cannot supply: **standard-setting, statistical harmonisation, ethical frameworks, advocacy, destination promotion, and infrastructure coordination**.

### 2.1.2 International Organizations

**UN Tourism (formerly UNWTO).** UN Tourism is the **United Nations specialized agency** responsible for promoting responsible, sustainable, and universally accessible tourism. Its functions include **intergovernmental leadership** (convening member states to adopt tourism policies), **statistical standardisation** (the UN Statistical Commission recognises UN Tourism as the appropriate body to collect, analyse, publish, and standardise tourism statistics), and **ethical governance** through the **Global Code of Ethics for Tourism** and the **World Committee on Tourism Ethics**, which promotes, interprets, and monitors implementation. UN Tourism also produces the **World Tourism Barometer** and supports countries with technical assistance in building statistical capacity. For Bangladesh, UN Tourism provides the methodological framework (IRTS 2008, TSA:RMF 2008) within which national tourism statistics must be compiled.

**World Travel & Tourism Council (WTTC).** The WTTC is the **forum for business leaders** in travel and tourism, representing the **private sector** globally. It has conducted **economic impact research** for over 30 years across 185 countries and 26 geographic or economic regions, producing the authoritative private-sector estimates of tourism's contribution to GDP and employment. WTTC research is widely cited to demonstrate that travel and tourism supports approximately **260 million jobs** and generates around **9–10% of global GDP**. Its advocacy focuses on **public–private collaboration**, taxation policy, and removing barriers to travel (visa facilitation, border policy). The distinction between **UN Tourism** (intergovernmental, statistical, ethical) and **WTTC** (private-sector, advocacy, economic impact) is fundamental: the former speaks for governments; the latter speaks for industry.

### 2.1.3 Developmental Organizations (International and National)

**The World Bank Group** finances tourism-related infrastructure—airports, roads, water supply, sanitation, and destination development—through loans, credits, and guarantees. It also supports **policy reform** and **institutional capacity building** in tourism-dependent economies. **The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP)** integrates tourism into broader **human development** and **SDG** frameworks, supporting community-based tourism, livelihood diversification, and environmental conservation. **The Asian Development Bank (ADB)** finances transport connectivity, urban infrastructure, and tourism corridor development across Asia and the Pacific, often linking tourism investment to **regional integration** objectives. For Bangladesh, these institutions have supported transport corridors, port development, and urban infrastructure that indirectly enable tourism, though dedicated tourism lending has been more limited. The key analytical point is that **developmental finance shapes the transit route and destination infrastructure** on which tourism depends, even when the loan is not labelled "tourism."

### 2.1.4 Regional International Organizations

**The Pacific Asia Travel Association (PATA)** is a **not-for-profit membership association** founded in 1951, dedicated to advancing a meaningful Pacific Asia tourism economy. It brings together **public and private sectors** to deliver partnerships, research, and events, and acts as a **catalyst for responsible development** of travel to, from, and within the Asia Pacific region. PATA provides **aligned advocacy, insightful research, and innovative events** to its members, and works to protect the interests of all members through coordinated strategies. **The European Travel Commission (ETC)** is the umbrella organisation representing **35 National Tourism Organisations** in Europe. Its core mandate is to **promote Europe as a tourist destination in key long-haul markets**, and it undertakes public relations, market intelligence, best-practice promotion, and knowledge exchange. ETC also conducts **benchmark research** to guide governments on visa policy and cross-border mobility. The contrast is instructive: PATA is a **broad membership association** spanning Asia and the Pacific, while ETC is a **continental NTO alliance** focused on promoting Europe as a unified destination.

### 2.1.5 National Tourism Organizations (NTOs) and National Tourism Administrations (NTAs)

An **NTO** is the national body responsible for **destination branding, marketing, and promotion** in overseas markets, often operating promotional offices abroad. An **NTA** is the national body responsible for **policy setting, regulation, and coordination** of the tourism sector. In many countries the two functions are combined in a single organisation; in others they are separated. The distinction matters because **marketing** and **regulation** require different capabilities and can conflict: an NTO promoting arrivals may have little authority over the environmental or labour standards that an NTA is expected to enforce.

### 2.1.6 Bangladesh National Organizations: BTB and BPC

**Bangladesh Tourism Board (BTB).** BTB was established under the **Bangladesh Tourism Board Act 2010 (Act No. 37 of 2010)** as the designated **National Tourism Organization (NTO)** of Bangladesh. Its statutory mandate is the **overall development, regulation, coordination, and administration** of the tourism industry: to formulate tourism policies, rules, and regulations; to make recommendations and assist in implementing existing policies; to identify, conserve, develop, and grow tourism attractions; and to create public awareness on tourism issues. BTB is responsible for **promoting and marketing Bangladesh tourism domestically and internationally**, formulating tourism plans, and attracting foreign investment. It operates under a **Governing Body** and is empowered to constitute committees to assist in its functions. Its enabling framework includes the **Bangladesh Tourism Master Plan (2021–2042)** and related strategies. However, institutional assessments note that **implementation has not been fully effective**: private-sector engagement remains weak, stakeholder coordination is slow, and master-plan preparation has taken longer than expected. This gap between statutory mandate and operational capacity is a central challenge for Bangladesh tourism governance.

**Bangladesh Parjatan Corporation (BPC).** BPC was established by **Presidential Order No. 143 of 1972** (27 November 1972) and began operations in 1973. Its statutory functions are broad: to **promote and develop tourism**, provide facilities, and carry out all activities connected with or ancillary to tourism. Specifically, BPC is empowered to **acquire, establish, construct, and run hotels, restaurants, rest houses, picnic spots, camping sites, theatres, amusement parks, casinos, and duty-free shops**; to **establish travel agencies and act as agents** for railways, shipping, airlines, and road transport for group tours; to **establish institutes for training** tourism personnel; and to conduct research and publish tourism literature. BPC operates the **National Hotel and Tourism Training Institute (NHTTI)**, which trains manpower for the tourism industry. It is, in effect, the **state commercial developer and operator**, whereas BTB is the **policy, planning, and marketing body**.

**Table 2.1: BTB versus BPC (Mandate, Function, Governance, Funding)**

| Dimension | Bangladesh Tourism Board (BTB) | Bangladesh Parjatan Corporation (BPC) |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal basis** | Bangladesh Tourism Board Act 2010 (Act No. 37) | Presidential Order No. 143 of 1972 |
| **Mandate** | NTO: policy formulation, planning, regulation, coordination, branding, marketing, investment promotion | NTA/commercial developer: promotion, infrastructure development, operation of hotels, tours, training |
| **Core functions** | Tourism master planning; domestic and international marketing; awareness creation; attraction identification and conservation | Hotel/restaurant/motel operation; travel agency and tour operation; duty-free shops; NHTTI training |
| **Governance** | Governing Body constituted under the Act; committees as needed | Board/Corporation structure under the Presidential Order; reports to Ministry of Civil Aviation and Tourism |
| **Funding** | Government grant and allocated resources | Commercial revenue from hotels, tours, duty-free operations, and government support |
| **Principal limitation** | Implementation gaps; weak private-sector engagement; slow coordination | Commercial orientation can conflict with developmental mandate; ageing assets; competitive pressure from private sector |

### 2.1.7 Regional, State, and Community Organizations

**State tourism departments** (in federal systems such as India, the United States, or Australia) manage destination marketing and product development at the provincial level. **Regional Tourism Organizations (RTOs)** coordinate marketing and development across a cluster of local government areas within a state or province. **Destination Management Organizations (DMOs)** are place-based entities—often public–private partnerships—responsible for **destination planning, management, and marketing**; unlike a pure marketing body, a DMO is increasingly expected to manage **visitor flows, community wellbeing, and sustainability**. **Convention and Visitors Bureaus (CVBs)** are a specialised form of DMO focused on **business events**: they bid for conventions, provide delegate services, and market the destination to meeting planners. The global trend is from **destination marketing** to **destination management**, reflecting the recognition that promotion without planning can generate overtourism and community backlash.

## 2.2 Passenger Transportation

### 2.2.1 The Airline Industry

**History of civil aviation.** Commercial aviation emerged from military technology after the Second World War, with surplus propeller aircraft converted to civilian use. The **Boeing 707 (1958)** inaugurated the Jet Age, and **deregulation** (US Airline Deregulation Act, 1978) and **low-cost carriers** (from the 1990s) progressively democratised air travel. Aviation is the **transit route** par excellence in Leiper's model: it determines which destination regions are accessible, at what cost, and to which generating regions.

**Scheduled FSCs versus LCCs.** The two dominant business models differ along a series of structural dimensions. **Full-service carriers (FSCs)** operate **hub-and-spoke networks**, typically at main airports, offering domestic, international, and intercontinental coverage; they sell **round-trip tickets** through multiple distribution channels; they configure aircraft with **premium and economy cabins**; they operate **diversified fleets**; they **bundle frills** (meals, baggage, lounges); and they manage customers through **frequent-flyer programmes (FFPs)**. **Low-cost carriers (LCCs)** operate **point-to-point networks**, often between secondary airports, with domestic and continental coverage; they sell **one-way tickets direct online**; they operate **single-class cabins** and **uniform fleets**; they offer **no frills**; and they typically have **no FFP**. LCCs have lower operating costs (higher seating density, higher daily aircraft utilisation) but also lower profit margins, which requires **high load factors**. Worldwide, LCCs account for approximately **26% of total seat capacity** and **22% of total frequency**. The hub-and-spoke model allows FSCs to exploit **economies of scale, scope, and density** by consolidating flows through a central airport; the point-to-point model allows LCCs to avoid the **complexity and irregularity risk** of connecting traffic.

**Table 2.2: Low-Cost Carriers (LCCs) versus Full-Service Network Carriers (FSCs)**

| Feature | FSCs | LCCs |
|---|---|---|
| **Core business** | Passenger and cargo | Passenger |
| **Consumer base** | Economy and business | Economy |
| **Network** | Hub-and-spoke at main airports | Point-to-point between secondary airports |
| **Coverage** | Domestic, international, intercontinental | Domestic and continental |
| **Ticketing** | Round-trip | One-way |
| **Distribution** | Multiple channels (agency, direct) | Direct online |
| **Cabin configuration** | Economy and premium (first/business) | Single class |
| **Fleet** | Diversified | Uniform |
| **Product bundling** | Frills included | No frills |
| **Customer management** | Frequent-flyer programme | No FFP |
| **Cost position** | Higher operating cost, higher margin potential | Lower operating cost, lower margin, high load factor required |

**Global airline alliances.** **Star Alliance**, **SkyTeam**, and **Oneworld** are multilateral alliances that allow member carriers to offer seamless connections, coordinated schedules, shared lounges, and reciprocal frequent-flyer benefits. Alliances are a response to the **network economics** of aviation: they allow FSCs to extend their global reach without merging or purchasing additional aircraft, and they create **switching costs** for premium travellers.

**Regulatory bodies.** **ICAO (International Civil Aviation Organization)** is the **UN specialized agency** that develops **internationally agreed standards and recommended practices** for aviation safety, navigation, airports, and rules of the air. It is a **technical and governmental** organisation whose standards are implemented by member states. **IATA (International Air Transport Association)** is the **airline industry trade association**, headquartered in Montreal, that coordinates industry standards (ticketing, baggage, dangerous goods), provides settlement services, and advocates for airlines in regulatory forums. IATA works closely with ICAO: it provides airline input to ICAO standard-setting and contributes to ICAO activities on air navigation, air transport, air law, and security. The distinction is **governmental (ICAO) versus industry (IATA)** .

**Freedoms of the Air.** The **nine Freedoms of the Air** are the **traffic rights** that states grant to foreign airlines, originating in the 1944 Chicago Convention and elaborated through bilateral air services agreements. They range from the **First Freedom** (right to fly over another country without landing) and the **Second Freedom** (right to land for non-traffic purposes, such as refuelling) to the **Third** (right to carry traffic from the home country to another), **Fourth** (right to carry traffic from another country to the home country), **Fifth** (right to carry traffic between two foreign countries as part of a service originating in the home country), **Sixth** (right to carry traffic between two foreign countries via the home country), **Seventh** (right to carry traffic between two foreign countries without going through the home country), **Eighth** (right to carry traffic between two points within a foreign country on a service originating in the home country), and **Ninth** (right to carry traffic between two points within a foreign country without continuing to the home country). In practice, the first five freedoms are the most commonly negotiated; the seventh, eighth, and ninth are rare and politically sensitive because they approach **cabotage** (domestic carriage by a foreign carrier).

### 2.2.2 The Rail Industry

**High-speed passenger rail.** Japan's **Shinkansen** (1964) demonstrated that rail could compete with aviation on medium-distance corridors; France's **TGV** (1981) and the **Eurostar** (1994) extended the model across Europe. High-speed rail competes effectively with short-haul aviation when journey times are under approximately three hours and city-centre access is superior to airport access. For tourism, high-speed rail **enlarges the destination region's catchment** and enables **day-trip and short-break markets**.

**Luxury and experiential rail tourism.** Trains such as the **Venice Simplon-Orient-Express** and India's **Palace on Wheels** convert transport into a **primary attraction**: the journey itself, not merely the arrival, is the product. These services combine **heritage rolling stock, fine dining, and interpretive hosting** to create a **slow tourism** experience that contrasts with the speed and standardisation of aviation.

**Commuter and scenic rail.** Commuter rail moves residents and visitors within metropolitan areas; scenic rail (mountain railways, heritage lines) functions as an **attraction in its own right**. The common thread is that rail offers **spatial integration**—it connects destinations along a corridor rather than simply delivering passengers to a single point.

### 2.2.3 The Motorcoach Industry

**Scheduled intercity motorcoaches** provide low-cost intercity transport, often serving destinations not connected by rail or air. **Charter coach services** are commissioned by tour operators, schools, corporations, and associations for group travel. **Sightseeing tour coaches** operate hop-on-hop-off circuits and guided city tours. The motorcoach is the **workhorse of group tourism**: it offers door-to-door flexibility, carries baggage, and allows interpretive commentary en route. Its economics depend on **high load factors** and **driver productivity**, and its environmental performance per passenger-kilometre is favourable relative to private cars when occupancy is high.

### 2.2.4 The Automobile Sector

**Highway networks**—the German Autobahn, the US Interstate Highway System (1956), and national road programmes—created a **parallel car-based tourism system**. **Personal vehicle touring** offers flexibility, privacy, and door-to-door mobility, and it dominates domestic tourism in large countries. **Fly-drive tourism** combines air travel to a gateway with a rental car for onward exploration, and it is a major segment of inbound tourism in destinations such as New Zealand, Australia, and the western United States. **Global car rental enterprises**—**Hertz**, **Avis**, and **Enterprise**—operate through airport and downtown locations, franchise networks, and corporate accounts; their revenue models combine **time-and-mileage charges, fuel policies, insurance products, and ancillary sales**. For tourism managers, the automobile sector is significant because it determines the **spatial dispersal** of visitors and the demand for **roadside accommodation, signage, parking, and attractions**.

### 2.2.5 The Cruise Industry

**Evolution from ocean liner to floating mega-resort.** The cruise industry began as a **passenger transport** business (transatlantic liners), but as aviation captured the point-to-point market, cruise lines repositioned the **onboard experience itself as the destination**. Modern cruise ships are **floating mega-resorts** with multiple restaurants, theatres, casinos, spas, water parks, and retail arcades. The industry's economics rest on **high occupancy** (often exceeding 100% of double-berth capacity because of upper berths) and **onboard revenue** (beverages, speciality dining, shore excursions, retail, gambling).

**Major cruise conglomerates.** The industry is highly concentrated. **Carnival Corporation** and **Royal Caribbean Group** together represent **over 59% of global supply in berths** (38% in number of ships), with Carnival holding a market share of almost **36%**; together with **MSC** and **Norwegian Cruise Line Holdings**, the top four hold approximately **80% of total supply**. In 2025, Carnival and Royal Caribbean carried over **23 million passengers**, and onboard revenue grew faster than ticket revenue. The market is segmented: Carnival and Royal Caribbean are **mass-market** operators (approximately $1,900–$2,000 revenue per passenger), while **Norwegian** commands over **$3,200 per passenger** through its premium and luxury brands. Cruise lines are increasingly investing in **private destinations**—owned or exclusive onshore sites that offer pricing power, onboard spending synergies, and guest satisfaction benefits at lower cost than building new ships.

**River cruising.** River cruises on the **Rhine, Danube, Nile, and Mekong** offer a different product: smaller ships, port-intensive itineraries, and a focus on **cultural immersion and scenic passage**. River cruising has grown rapidly because it serves an **ageing, affluent, experience-oriented market** that values comfort, low mobility demands, and destination depth over onboard spectacle.

**Onboard revenue and shore excursion dynamics.** Cruise lines earn a significant and growing share of revenue from **onboard spending** and from **shore excursions**, which are often sold as packaged add-ons. This creates a distinctive **value chain**: the cruise line captures revenue from the excursion, while the local operator receives a contracted payment, and the destination bears the **congestion and environmental costs** of large-volume, time-compressed visitation. For port destinations, the cruise business is high-volume but often **low-yield per visitor**, and the leakage of excursion revenue to the cruise line is a persistent concern.

## 2.3 Hospitality and Related Services

### 2.3.1 The Lodging Industry

**Classification of accommodations.** Lodging establishments can be classified by **size** (number of rooms), **target market** (business, leisure, extended-stay, budget, luxury), **service level** (full-service, select-service, limited-service, economy), and **rating systems** (star ratings, diamond ratings, guest-review scores). Rating systems vary by country and by provider; no single global standard exists, which is why international comparisons require caution. Other accommodation categories include **guesthouses, hostels, serviced apartments, resorts, motels, and alternative accommodations** (Airbnb-style short-term rentals).

**Business operating structures.** The four principal models are **independent ownership** (the owner operates the property and bears all risk and reward), **franchising** (the owner operates under a brand's system, paying initial and ongoing fees in exchange for reservation systems, standards, and marketing), **management contracts** (the owner retains the asset while a professional management company operates the property for a fee, often tied to performance), and **hotel chains** (corporate ownership or lease of multiple properties). **Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)** are a specialised ownership form: they own hotel real estate and lease it to operators, benefiting from **tax pass-through** treatment while separating **asset ownership** from **brand and management**.

**Table 2.3: Hotel Operating Models: Independent vs. Franchise vs. Management Contract**

| Dimension | Independent | Franchise | Management Contract |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who operates** | Owner | Owner, under brand standards | Management company |
| **Brand** | Own or none | Franchisor's brand | Owner's or manager's brand |
| **Owner's role** | Full operational control | Operational control, subject to standards | Asset ownership and strategic oversight |
| **Fees** | None (but no brand support) | Initial fee plus royalty and marketing fees | Base management fee plus incentive fee |
| **Risk allocation** | Owner bears all | Owner bears operational and market risk | Owner bears asset risk; manager bears performance risk (partially) |
| **Key advantage** | Flexibility, independence | Brand recognition, reservation systems, standards | Professional expertise, global distribution, performance incentives |
| **Key disadvantage** | Limited distribution and brand power | Loss of autonomy, fee burden | Potential principal–agent conflict; owner bears costs |

**Key lodging performance indicators.** Three metrics are indispensable for lodging management.

**Occupancy Rate (OCC%)** measures the proportion of available rooms that are sold: \(\text{Occupancy Rate} = (\text{Rooms Sold} \div \text{Rooms Available}) \times 100\). A hotel with 100 rooms that sells 75 rooms on a given night has an occupancy rate of 75%.

**Average Daily Rate (ADR)** measures the average price paid per room sold: \(\text{ADR} = \text{Total Room Revenue} \div \text{Rooms Sold}\). If the hotel above generates $7,500 in room revenue from 75 rooms, the ADR is $100.

**Revenue Per Available Room (RevPAR)** combines price and volume: \(\text{RevPAR} = \text{ADR} \times \text{Occupancy Rate}\), or equivalently \(\text{RevPAR} = \text{Total Room Revenue} \div \text{Rooms Available}\). For the same hotel, RevPAR = $100 × 0.75 = $75. RevPAR is the **single most widely used revenue-efficiency KPI** in the lodging industry because it captures both **pricing effectiveness** and **inventory utilisation**. A hotel can raise RevPAR by raising ADR, by raising occupancy, or by both; the optimal mix depends on market segmentation, seasonality, and cost structure.

### 2.3.2 The Food Service Industry

**The spectrum of food service.** Food service ranges from **fine dining** (high-price, high-service, chef-driven) through **casual dining** (table service at moderate prices) to **quick-service restaurants (QSRs)** (limited service, low price, high volume, standardised menus). **Banqueting** serves large functions—weddings, conferences, galas—and is a major revenue stream for hotels. **Institutional catering** serves hospitals, schools, prisons, and corporate canteens, where the customer is the institution rather than the individual diner. For tourism, food service is not merely a support function: it is increasingly a **primary motivator**.

**The rise of culinary/gastronomy tourism.** Gastronomy tourism—travel motivated by food and drink experiences—has grown rapidly as destinations recognise that **local cuisine, food markets, vineyards, and cooking traditions** are distinctive, authentic, and difficult to replicate elsewhere. Food tourism supports **local agricultural supply chains**, **artisanal producers**, and **small restaurants**, and it can disperse visitors beyond honeypot attractions. The management challenge is to **professionalise food experiences** (hygiene, storytelling, service quality) without **homogenising** the culinary heritage that makes them attractive.

### 2.3.3 Meeting Planners, Meetings, and Conventions

**The MICE industry.** MICE stands for **Meetings, Incentives, Conferences, and Exhibitions** (also called *business events*). MICE visitors are **high-yield**: they typically spend **3–6 times more than leisure visitors**, travel in groups, and are less seasonal than leisure demand. The global MICE market is large and growing, with countries such as Vietnam and Malaysia explicitly targeting it as a strategic development direction. MICE generates **high-value, stable jobs** and supports **knowledge exchange, investment, and global connectivity**.

**Roles in the MICE chain.** **Corporate meeting planners** organise internal meetings, training events, and incentive trips for companies. **Association executives** manage conferences for professional and trade associations, often through competitive **bidding processes** in which destinations compete to host. **Professional Congress Organisers (PCOs)** are specialist firms that manage the logistics, programme, and finances of large conferences on behalf of associations. **Destination management companies (DMCs)** handle ground arrangements—transport, venues, excursions—for MICE groups.

**Convention centres and economic impact.** Convention centres are **purpose-built facilities** that anchor business events and stimulate surrounding hotel, restaurant, and retail development. Their economic impact is **counter-cyclical** (business events fill midweek and shoulder-season capacity) and **high-multiplier** (delegates spend on accommodation, meals, and entertainment). However, convention centres are often **publicly subsidised** because their direct operating revenue may not cover capital costs; the justification rests on **wider destination benefits**, which must be rigorously measured rather than assumed.

## 2.4 Organizations in the Distribution Process

### 2.4.1 Intermediary Roles: Bridging Suppliers and Tourists

**Direct distribution** occurs when a supplier sells directly to the tourist (an airline's website, a hotel's reservations desk). **Indirect distribution** occurs through intermediaries—travel agents, OTAs, tour operators, GDSs—who add **information, convenience, risk-bearing, and bundling** to the transaction. Intermediaries exist because tourism products are **intangible, perishable, geographically dispersed, and complex to assemble**; the tourist cannot inspect a hotel room before purchase, cannot store an unsold airline seat, and often lacks the time or expertise to combine transport, accommodation, and activities into a coherent itinerary.

### 2.4.2 Travel Agents

**Traditional retail travel agents** sell travel products on behalf of suppliers, earning **commissions** (typically a percentage of the sale) or **service fees** from clients. They provide advice, handle bookings, and often act as **advocates** when disruption occurs. **Online Travel Agencies (OTAs)** perform the same intermediary function through digital platforms, but with **greater scale, price transparency, and automation**. The global OTA market is dominated by **Booking Holdings** and **Expedia Group**, whose business models combine **agency (commission) models** (the OTA facilitates the booking and the supplier pays a commission) and **merchant models** (the OTA takes payment from the traveller and settles with the supplier, sometimes earning a margin on the markup).

In Bangladesh, the OTA landscape illustrates both models. **ShareTrip** and **GoZayaan** are the leading domestic players, offering flights, hotels, and buses, and earning commissions from airlines and partners (the agency/commission model). **Triplover**, **Travel Champ**, and **Flight Expert** use the **merchant model**, where travellers pay the OTA at booking and the OTA manages payments with airlines or bus operators. ShareTrip was founded in 2014 (formerly Travel Booking Bangladesh), and GoZayaan was founded in 2017; both have served significant volumes of Bangladeshi travellers, and foreign-invested platforms have collectively served over **30 lakh (3 million) travellers** without major fraud incidents, attracting foreign direct investment from Singapore and Japan. The emergence of these platforms represents a **structural shift** in Bangladesh's distribution system: from traditional agency counters to **digital intermediation**, with implications for supplier margins, price transparency, and consumer protection.

### 2.4.3 Internet and Electronic Distribution Systems

**Global Distribution Systems (GDS)** are **computerised networks** that connect travel sellers—agencies, corporate travel managers, and some OTAs—to airline, car rental, and hotel inventory through a single connection. The three major GDS platforms are **Amadeus**, **Sabre**, and **Travelport** (which includes Galileo and Worldspan), and they account for approximately **80% of the GDS market**. Sabre was the first computerised reservation system, launched in the 1960s, and the modern GDSs grew out of airline reservation technology. Their coverage is substantial: Amadeus connects over **421,000 hotel properties**, Sabre links **125,000 hotels with 400,000+ travel agents**, and Travelport connects **650,000+ hotel properties** to agencies in **180+ countries**. GDSs remain the backbone of **corporate travel** and **complex international itineraries**, even as direct online distribution has grown.

**Central Reservation Systems (CRS)** are the supplier-side systems that manage inventory and rates; a hotel chain's CRS feeds availability to the GDSs and to OTAs. **Property Management Systems (PMS)** are the on-property systems that manage front desk, housekeeping, billing, and guest history. **Channel Managers** are software tools that synchronise rates and availability across multiple distribution channels—direct, GDS, OTA, metasearch—in real time, reducing the risk of **overbooking or rate disparity**.

### 2.4.4 Tour Wholesalers and Tour Operators

**Tour operators** create tourism products—typically **inclusive package tours**—by assembling transport, accommodation, ground services, and activities, and selling them directly or through travel agents. The tour operator is the **principal** in relation to the package: it bears the risk of unsold inventory and is responsible for the components it sells. **Travel agents**, by contrast, **sell** products created by others and typically act as **agents** earning commission. The distinction is fundamental: the operator **creates** the product; the agent **distributes** it.

**Inbound versus outbound operators.** **Outbound operators** assemble packages for residents travelling abroad; **inbound operators** (or **receiving operators**) assemble packages for foreign visitors to a destination, often working with overseas tour operators who sell the package in the generating region. **Domestic operators** serve residents travelling within their own country.

**Mechanics of assembling inclusive package tours.** The operator **charters flights** (committing to a block of seats or an entire aircraft), **block-books hotel rooms** (committing to a specified number of room-nights at a negotiated rate), and **contracts ground transport** (coaches, transfers, guides). These commitments are made **months in advance** of demand, which is why tour operation is a **risk-bearing business**: if demand falls short, the operator bears the loss; if demand exceeds expectations, the operator may lack capacity. The package price must cover the **committed costs, overhead, marketing, and a margin**, while remaining competitive against **dynamic online pricing**.

**Table 2.4: Tour Operator versus Retail Travel Agency**

| Dimension | Tour Operator | Retail Travel Agency |
|---|---|---|
| **Core function** | Creates and assembles package tours | Sells travel products created by others |
| **Legal role** | Principal (responsible for the package) | Agent (acts on behalf of the supplier) |
| **Risk** | Bears risk of unsold inventory and component failure | Limited risk; earns commission or fee |
| **Inventory commitment** | Charters flights, block-books hotels, contracts transport | Books on demand from supplier inventory |
| **Revenue model** | Package margin (price minus component costs) | Commission or service fee |
| **Customer relationship** | Often business-to-business (sells through agents) and direct | Direct to consumer |
| **Examples** | Thomas Cook (historically), TUI, specialist inbound operators | Traditional high-street agencies, OTAs |

### 2.4.5 Consolidators and Specialty Channelers

**Consolidators** aggregate inventory—particularly airline seats—from multiple suppliers and resell it at discounted net fares to travel agents or directly to consumers. They profit from **volume discounts** and **inventory risk absorption**. **Corporate travel managers** are specialty channelers who manage a company's travel programme: they negotiate corporate rates, enforce travel policy, ensure duty-of-care, and use **GDS and online booking tools** to control costs. **Incentive travel planners** design motivational travel rewards for employees or channel partners, combining **luxury experiences, group activities, and recognition events**; they are a specialised segment of the MICE industry.

### 2.4.6 Channel Selection Decision

Suppliers choose distribution channels based on several criteria: **reach** (access to target markets), **cost of distribution** (commission, transaction fees, and channel-management overhead), **control** (ability to manage pricing, branding, and the guest relationship), **risk** (inventory commitment and cancellation exposure), and **strategic fit** (alignment with the brand's positioning). A luxury hotel may prioritise **direct and GDS distribution** to protect rate integrity and guest relationships, while a budget hotel may rely heavily on **OTAs** for volume. The rise of OTAs has shifted bargaining power toward intermediaries, prompting suppliers to invest in **direct-booking incentives, loyalty programmes, and channel managers** that reduce dependence on commission-heavy channels.

## 2.5 Attractions, Entertainment, Recreation, and Other Sectors

### 2.5.1 Attractions

**Definition and classification.** An attraction is a **permanent or temporary feature** that motivates visitation and provides **enjoyment, education, or edification**. Attractions are conventionally classified as **natural** (landscapes, wildlife, national parks) or **cultural/man-made** (heritage sites, museums, monuments, theme parks). The distinction is useful but imperfect: many cultural attractions are embedded in natural settings, and many natural attractions are interpreted through cultural infrastructure.

**Heritage sites and UNESCO World Heritage monuments.** UNESCO World Heritage designation confers **global recognition** and often stimulates visitation. Bangladesh has **three UNESCO World Heritage sites**: **Somapura Mahavihara (Paharpur)**, the **Shat Gambuj Mosque (Bagerhat)**, and the **Sundarbans**. Paharpur, South Asia's oldest archaeological site and a UNESCO site since 1985, attracts thousands of visitors daily and generates over **Tk 1 crore annually**. The **Shat Gambuj Mosque complex** in Bagerhat, also inscribed in 1985, includes the Sixty Dome Mosque, the Nine Dome Mosque, and associated monuments, and is a major domestic tourism draw during Eid holidays. These sites illustrate the **conservation–visitation tension**: increased visitor numbers generate revenue and awareness, but they also impose wear, congestion, and management costs.

**Museums and iconic landmarks.** Museums preserve and interpret cultural and natural heritage; iconic landmarks (the Eiffel Tower, the Taj Mahal, the Statue of Liberty) function as **destination symbols** that anchor marketing and generate **place identity**. In Bangladesh, the **National Museum**, the **Liberation War Museum**, and the **Lalbagh Fort** are significant cultural attractions, though their tourism infrastructure and interpretive quality vary.

### 2.5.2 Gaming

**Casino resorts** are integrated hospitality and entertainment complexes in which gaming is a primary revenue generator. **Macau** overtook **Las Vegas** as the world's largest gambling hub within five years of liberalising its casino sector, and it remains the only place in China where casino gambling is legal. **Las Vegas** has diversified beyond gaming into entertainment, conventions, dining, and retail, with gaming-related taxes accounting for around **35% of government revenue**. Macau, by contrast, has struggled to diversify beyond gaming, and its heavy specialisation has produced **social costs**—problem gambling, over-tourism, and land-pressure—that require substantial urban planning responses.

**Economic impacts and social controversies.** Casino resorts generate **tax revenue, employment, and tourism expenditure**, and they can anchor urban regeneration. However, they also generate **social costs** (gambling addiction, crime, family disruption), **regulatory capture risk**, and **economic dependence** on a single volatile sector. The Las Vegas model—diversification into non-gaming entertainment—is often held up as the more sustainable path, while the Macau model illustrates the risks of **monocultural specialisation**.

### 2.5.3 Recreation and Outdoor Leisure

**National parks and wilderness areas** are protected landscapes that provide **nature-based recreation**: hiking, wildlife viewing, camping, and interpretation. They are managed for **conservation and public enjoyment**, and their tourism value depends on **carrying capacity management**—the ability to absorb visitors without degrading the resource. **Adventure tourism** involves **physical activity, risk, and specialised skills**: trekking, rafting, rock climbing, scuba diving, and mountaineering. It attracts **high-yield, experience-seeking travellers** and often supports remote communities, but it requires **safety regulation, guide certification, and environmental safeguards**. **Water sports** and **ski resorts** are activity-based recreation sectors with pronounced **seasonality** and **capital intensity**; ski resorts in particular face existential risks from **climate change**, which is shortening seasons and forcing diversification into summer activities.

### 2.5.4 Entertainment

**Live theatre** districts—**Broadway** in New York and the **West End** in London—are major tourism magnets that combine **cultural production, hospitality, and retail** in dense urban clusters. **Music concerts** and **mega nightlife destinations** (Ibiza, Las Vegas, Berlin) generate **high-volume, high-spend** visitation, often with significant **seasonality and noise/land-use externalities**. Entertainment is a **primary trip motivator** for many segments, and it increasingly overlaps with **events** and **festivals**.

### 2.5.5 Festivals and Special Events

**Mega-events** such as the **FIFA World Cup** and the **Olympic Games** are often promoted as catalysts for **urban regeneration, tourism growth, and job creation**. The evidence is mixed: hosting countries experience GDP growth in the years before and after the event, but the **net economic return is frequently negative** once infrastructure costs, displaced investment, and debt service are accounted for. The 1994 Football World Cup in the United States, for example, was predicted to generate a **net gain of USD 4 billion** for host cities; independent examination after the event found a **negative net impact** in the range of **USD 5.5–9.3 billion**. The 2014 World Cup cost Brazil **USD 15–20 billion**, and Beijing reportedly spent **USD 40 billion** before the 2008 Olympics. Positive legacies are most evident where **infrastructure readiness, governance quality, and legacy planning** are high. **Cultural and religious celebrations**, local **food and art festivals**, and **community events** are lower-cost, higher-frequency alternatives that can generate **dispersal, seasonality smoothing, and community pride**, but they require **event management cycles**—concept development, feasibility, planning, execution, and evaluation—to avoid **crowding, waste, and community fatigue**.

### 2.5.6 Shopping Tourism

**Shopping tourism** is a distinct form of travel in which **purchasing is the primary motivation**, spanning **luxury districts, duty-free islands, outlet villages, heritage markets, and experiential retail**. **Duty-free shopping** at airports and border crossings captures **captive traveller expenditure**, while **outlet malls** and **downtown duty-free complexes** extend shopping into the destination. The **Haikou International Duty-Free City** in Hainan, opened in 2022, is the world's largest single duty-free outlet, illustrating the scale of the sector. **Traditional bazaars and artisan markets**—such as Dhaka's **New Market** or the **Sundarban honey and handicraft markets**—offer **cultural authenticity and local economic participation**, but they often lack the **quality assurance, payment infrastructure, and visitor management** that international shoppers expect. Shopping tourism is strategically valuable because it **extends dwell time, raises per-visitor spend, and supports local retail and craft sectors**, but it can also **displace local residents** and **commodify cultural products**.

### 2.5.7 Support Services

**Tourism education and training institutes** supply the sector's human capital. BPC's **NHTTI** is Bangladesh's principal public training institute; private institutions and universities also offer hospitality and tourism programmes. **Travel publishing** (guidebooks, maps, magazines, digital content) shapes **destination image and visitor expectations**. **Tourism marketing and PR agencies** specialise in destination branding, crisis communication, and influencer engagement. **Travel insurance** provides **risk transfer** for medical emergencies, cancellation, baggage loss, and repatriation; it is a **condition of visa issuance** in many jurisdictions and a **duty-of-care requirement** for corporate travel programmes. **Foreign currency exchange providers**—banks, bureaux de change, and fintech platforms—enable **cross-border payments** and are a **revenue centre** for airports and travel agencies. These support services are the **invisible infrastructure** of tourism: they do not attract visitors directly, but their absence raises **transaction costs and risk**, and their quality shapes the **ease and confidence** with which travel occurs.

## 2.6 Bangladesh Sectoral Vignette: Transport and Hospitality Matrix

**Aviation.** Bangladesh's aviation sector is duopolistic at the flag-carrier level and increasingly competitive in the private segment. **Biman Bangladesh Airlines**, the state-owned flag carrier, operates a fleet of approximately **19–21 aircraft**, dominated by Boeing models (five B737-800s, four B777-300ERs, four B787-8s, two B787-9s, and five DHC-8-Q400s), and has signed agreements for additional Boeing aircraft to modernise and expand. **US-Bangla Airlines**, a private carrier, has expanded rapidly to **25 aircraft** (including ATR 72-600 turboprops, Boeing 737-800s, and Airbus A330-300s), overtaking Biman as Bangladesh's largest airline by fleet size, and has announced a **$1.1–1.5 billion fleet expansion** with 21 Boeing aircraft to be delivered by 2027. Other private carriers include **Novoair** and **Air Astra**. The competitive dynamic is significant: private carriers acquire aircraft more quickly, while Biman faces **procurement bottlenecks** and financial pressures. For tourism, fleet expansion and route development determine **air connectivity**, which is the binding constraint on inbound tourism growth.

**Luxury hotels.** Dhaka's luxury hotel market is anchored by international brands. **Pan Pacific Sonargaon Dhaka** (278 rooms, Karwan Bazar) is a five-star convention hotel reflecting Mughal architectural themes, with extensive pool, spa, and dining facilities. **InterContinental Dhaka** (Minto Road) is a five-star property with a strong city-centre location and high guest ratings. **Radisson Blu Dhaka Water Garden** is another major five-star property, known for its water-garden setting and business facilities. These hotels serve **business travellers, MICE delegates, diplomats, and high-end leisure visitors**, and they function as **nodes of the transit route and destination region** for Bangladesh's inbound tourism. Their performance is closely tied to **corporate travel demand, security perceptions, and aviation connectivity**.

**OTAs and digital distribution.** Bangladesh's digital travel market is consolidating around a few strong platforms. **ShareTrip** (founded 2014) and **GoZayaan** (founded 2017) are the leading domestic OTAs, offering flights, hotels, and buses on **commission models**; **Triplover**, **Travel Champ**, and **Flight Expert** operate on **merchant models**. The entry of foreign investment into GoZayaan and ShareTrip—raising over **USD 2 crore** from Singapore and Japan—signals international confidence in Bangladesh's digital travel market, and these platforms have collectively served over **30 lakh travellers**. The **US-Bangla Group** has also launched platforms including **TripLover**, **TravelChamp**, **First Trip**, and **Take Trip**, integrating airline inventory with OTA distribution. This digital shift is **disintermediating traditional travel agents**, increasing **price transparency**, and creating new **consumer-protection challenges** that regulators are only beginning to address.

## 2.7 Mathematical Worked Examples: Hotel Performance Metrics

### Worked Example 1: Single Hotel, Single Night

A hotel has **200 rooms available**. On a given night, it sells **150 rooms** and generates **$22,500 in room revenue**.

- \(\text{Occupancy Rate} = (150 \div 200) \times 100 = 75\%\)
- \(\text{ADR} = \$22,500 \div 150 = \$150\)
- \(\text{RevPAR} = \$150 \times 0.75 = \$112.50\)

Check: \(\text{RevPAR} = \$22,500 \div 200 = \$112.50\). The two methods agree.

### Worked Example 2: Comparing Three Hotels

Three hotels in the same market report the following data for a month (30 days):

| Hotel | Rooms | Room-Nights Available | Room-Nights Sold | Room Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hotel A** | 100 | 3,000 | 2,100 | $189,000 |
| **Hotel B** | 150 | 4,500 | 2,700 | $270,000 |
| **Hotel C** | 200 | 6,000 | 3,000 | $360,000 |

**Step 1: Calculate occupancy.**

- Hotel A: \(2,100 \div 3,000 = 70.0\%\)
- Hotel B: \(2,700 \div 4,500 = 60.0\%\)
- Hotel C: \(3,000 \div 6,000 = 50.0\%\)

**Step 2: Calculate ADR.**

- Hotel A: \(\$189,000 \div 2,100 = \$90.00\)
- Hotel B: \(\$270,000 \div 2,700 = \$100.00\)
- Hotel C: \(\$360,000 \div 3,000 = \$120.00\)

**Step 3: Calculate RevPAR.**

- Hotel A: \(0.70 \times \$90 = \$63.00\) (or \(\$189,000 \div 3,000 = \$63.00\))
- Hotel B: \(0.60 \times \$100 = \$60.00\) (or \(\$270,000 \div 4,500 = \$60.00\))
- Hotel C: \(0.50 \times \$120 = \$60.00\) (or \(\$360,000 \div 6,000 = \$60.00\))

**Interpretation.** Hotel A has the **highest occupancy** and the **highest RevPAR**, despite having the **lowest ADR**. Hotels B and C have identical RevPAR ($60) but **different strategies**: B pursues a **balanced approach** (60% occupancy at $100 ADR), while C pursues a **high-price, low-occupancy** strategy (50% at $120). Neither strategy is inherently superior; the optimal choice depends on the hotel's **cost structure, market segment, and brand positioning**. A hotel with high fixed costs may prioritise occupancy; a luxury hotel with strong brand equity may prioritise ADR.

### Worked Example 3: Impact of a Distribution Channel on Net RevPAR

A hotel has **100 rooms**, sells **80 rooms** at an **ADR of $200**, generating **$16,000 in room revenue**. Of the 80 rooms sold, **40 are booked through an OTA charging 18% commission**, and **40 are direct bookings with no commission**.

- Gross RevPAR \(= \$16,000 \div 100 = \$160.00\)
- OTA commission \(= 40 \times \$200 \times 0.18 = \$1,440\)
- Net room revenue \(= \$16,000 - \$1,440 = \$14,560\)
- **Net RevPAR** \(= \$14,560 \div 100 = \$145.60\)

The **distribution cost** of $14.40 per available room (the difference between gross and net RevPAR) is a **channel-management signal**: the hotel may seek to **shift demand toward direct booking** through loyalty incentives, best-rate guarantees, or improved direct-booking technology.

## 2.8 Formula Reference Cheat-Sheet

| Metric | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Occupancy Rate (OCC%)** | (Rooms Sold ÷ Rooms Available) × 100 | Utilisation of room inventory |
| **Average Daily Rate (ADR)** | Total Room Revenue ÷ Rooms Sold | Average price per occupied room |
| **Revenue Per Available Room (RevPAR)** | ADR × Occupancy Rate; or Total Room Revenue ÷ Rooms Available | Revenue efficiency per available room |
| **Total Revenue Per Available Room (TRevPAR)** | (Room + F&B + Other Revenue) ÷ Rooms Available | Contribution of all revenue departments |
| **Gross Operating Profit Per Available Room (GOPPAR)** | Gross Operating Profit ÷ Rooms Available | Profitability after operating costs |
| **Net RevPAR** | (Total Room Revenue − Distribution Costs) ÷ Rooms Available | Revenue efficiency after channel commissions |
| **Rooms Available** | Total Rooms × Days in Period | Inventory denominator |
| **Rooms Sold** | Rooms Occupied × Nights | Volume numerator |
| **Tourism Multiplier** | Total Economic Effect ÷ Direct Tourist Expenditure | Amplification of initial spending |
| **Leakage Rate** | (Leakage ÷ Gross Tourism Receipts) × 100 | Share of receipts leaving the economy |

### 2.9.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions (with Model Answers)

**Question 1. Distinguish between the roles of UN Tourism and the WTTC in global tourism governance, and explain why both are necessary.**

**Model Answer.** UN Tourism is the **UN specialized agency** for tourism, with an **intergovernmental** mandate: it convenes member states, sets **statistical standards** (IRTS 2008, TSA:RMF 2008), promotes the **Global Code of Ethics for Tourism**, and provides **technical assistance** to governments. The WTTC is a **private-sector forum** that conducts **economic impact research** across 185 countries, advocates for **industry-friendly policy**, and promotes **public–private collaboration**. Both are necessary because tourism requires **two distinct forms of coordination**: **governmental standard-setting and ethical governance** (UN Tourism) and **industry advocacy and economic measurement** (WTTC). UN Tourism cannot compel private firms to adopt sustainable practices; WTTC cannot negotiate intergovernmental agreements or harmonise statistics. The two organisations are **complementary**, not duplicative: one speaks for states, the other for industry, and effective global governance requires both voices.

---

**Question 2. Explain the difference between hub-and-spoke and point-to-point airline networks, and identify which model is associated with FSCs and which with LCCs.**

**Model Answer.** A **hub-and-spoke network** consolidates traffic through a central airport (the hub), with secondary airports (spokes) feeding passengers into and out of the hub. This allows an airline to **exploit economies of scale, scope, and density**, offer **connecting itineraries** across a large network, and fill **long-haul aircraft** with passengers drawn from many short-haul origins. It is the **dominant model for full-service carriers (FSCs)** , which operate at main airports and offer domestic, international, and intercontinental coverage. A **point-to-point network** connects city pairs directly, without consolidation through a hub. It is **simpler, cheaper, and less prone to irregularity risk**, and it is the **dominant model for low-cost carriers (LCCs)** , which typically operate between secondary airports on short-haul routes. The trade-off is **network reach versus cost and simplicity**: hub-and-spoke maximises connectivity; point-to-point minimises cost and complexity.

---

**Question 3. Define Occupancy Rate, ADR, and RevPAR, and explain why RevPAR is considered the single most important lodging performance metric.**

**Model Answer.** **Occupancy Rate** is the percentage of available rooms sold: (Rooms Sold ÷ Rooms Available) × 100. **ADR** is the average room revenue per room sold: Total Room Revenue ÷ Rooms Sold. **RevPAR** is revenue per available room: ADR × Occupancy Rate, or Total Room Revenue ÷ Rooms Available. RevPAR is the **single most important metric** because it captures **both pricing and volume** in one number: a hotel can improve RevPAR by raising ADR, by raising occupancy, or by a combination of the two. Occupancy alone ignores price; ADR alone ignores volume. RevPAR therefore provides a **balanced measure of revenue efficiency** and is the **core KPI for daily, weekly, and monthly performance reviews** in the lodging industry. It also allows **comparison across hotels of different sizes**, because it is expressed per available room rather than in absolute revenue.

---

**Question 4. Distinguish between a tour operator and a retail travel agency in terms of legal role, risk, and revenue model.**

**Model Answer.** A **tour operator** is a **principal**: it creates and assembles package tours by committing to charter flights, block-booking hotel rooms, and contracting ground transport, and it bears the **risk** of unsold inventory and component failure. Its revenue model is the **package margin**—the difference between the package price and the cost of components. A **retail travel agency** is an **agent**: it sells products created by others, typically earning a **commission** or service fee, and it bears **limited risk** because it does not commit to inventory in advance. The operator **creates** the product; the agent **distributes** it. In practice, large operators may sell directly to consumers as well as through agents, and some agencies create their own packages, blurring the boundary—but the **legal and risk distinction** remains analytically fundamental.

**Question 5. What are the nine Freedoms of the Air, and why are the seventh, eighth, and ninth freedoms rarely granted?**

**Model Answer.** The nine Freedoms are **traffic rights** granted by states to foreign airlines, originating in the 1944 Chicago Convention. The **First Freedom** is the right to fly over another country without landing; the **Second**, the right to land for non-traffic purposes (e.g., refuelling); the **Third**, the right to carry traffic from the home country to another; the **Fourth**, the right to carry traffic from another country to the home country; the **Fifth**, the right to carry traffic between two foreign countries as part of a service originating in the home country; the **Sixth**, the right to carry traffic between two foreign countries via the home country; the **Seventh**, the right to carry traffic between two foreign countries without going through the home country; the **Eighth**, the right to carry traffic between two points within a foreign country on a service originating in the home country; and the **Ninth**, the right to carry traffic between two points within a foreign country without continuing to the home country. The **seventh, eighth, and ninth freedoms** are rarely granted because they approach **cabotage**—the carriage of domestic traffic by a foreign carrier—which is politically sensitive and is protected by most states as a matter of **national sovereignty and domestic airline viability**. Granting these freedoms would allow foreign airlines to compete directly in domestic markets, which incumbent carriers and labour unions typically resist.

**Question 6. Explain the difference between the agency (commission) model and the merchant model in online travel distribution, with examples from Bangladesh.**

**Model Answer.** In the **agency (commission) model**, the traveller books through the OTA but **pays the airline or operator directly**, and the OTA earns a **commission** on the booking. In Bangladesh, **GoZayaan** and **ShareTrip** are the leading examples of this model, offering flights, hotels, and buses while earning commissions from airlines and partners. In the **merchant model**, the traveller **pays the OTA at the time of booking**, and the OTA manages payments with the airline or operator, often earning a **margin** on the transaction. **Triplover**, **Travel Champ**, and **Flight Expert** use this model in Bangladesh. The merchant model gives the OTA **more control over pricing and cash flow** but exposes it to **inventory and settlement risk**; the agency model is **lower-risk** but offers **thinner margins**. The coexistence of both models in Bangladesh reflects the **segmented nature** of the market: some consumers prioritise **price and convenience** (merchant), while others prioritise **direct supplier relationships and flexibility** (agency).

### 2.9.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions (with Full Structural Outlines)

**Essay Question 1. "The distribution of tourism products has been transformed by digital intermediation, but the fundamental functions of intermediaries have not disappeared." Critically evaluate this statement with reference to GDSs, OTAs, tour operators, and channel management.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** State the thesis: digital technology has **changed the form and scale** of intermediation but has not eliminated its **economic functions**—information, risk-bearing, bundling, and convenience.
- **Section 1: The functions of intermediaries.** Information asymmetry (the tourist cannot inspect the product); perishability (unsold inventory expires); complexity (assembling multi-component trips); risk (supplier failure, disruption); and convenience (search and transaction costs).
- **Section 2: The GDS layer.** Sabre, Amadeus, and Travelport connect suppliers to agencies and corporate bookers; they remain the **backbone of complex itineraries and corporate travel**, even as direct online distribution has grown. GDSs perform **inventory aggregation and settlement** functions that no single supplier can replicate.
- **Section 3: The OTA layer.** Booking Holdings and Expedia Group dominate consumer-facing distribution; they have **shifted bargaining power** toward intermediaries and created **rate-parity and commission pressures** for suppliers. But they have not eliminated the **need for advice and advocacy** in complex or high-stakes trips.
- **Section 4: Tour operators.** The package tour **internalises** the functions of information, risk-bearing, and bundling within a single firm. Digital technology has **lowered the cost of direct selling** for operators (dynamic packaging) but has also **increased price transparency** and **disintermediated** some traditional operators.
- **Section 5: Channel management and the supplier response.** Hotels and airlines invest in **direct-booking incentives, loyalty programmes, and channel managers** to reduce dependence on commission-heavy channels. The result is **hybrid distribution**: suppliers use OTAs for reach but try to **convert OTA guests to direct relationships**.
- **Conclusion.** Digital intermediation has **democratised access and increased transparency**, but the **underlying functions** of intermediation—reducing uncertainty, bearing risk, and assembling complex products—remain. The intermediaries that survive are those that **add value beyond price comparison**.

**Essay Question 2. "Mega-events are sold to host cities as engines of tourism and economic growth, but the evidence suggests that the benefits are often overstated and the costs underestimated." Discuss.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** State the paradox: mega-events (Olympics, FIFA World Cup) generate **visible spending and civic pride**, but ex-post evaluations frequently find **negative net economic returns**.
- **Section 1: The promoters' case.** Visitor spending, job creation, urban regeneration, infrastructure legacy, and **destination branding** (the "showcase" effect).
- **Section 2: The ex-post evidence.** The 1994 US World Cup: predicted **+$4 billion**, actual **−$5.5 to −$9.3 billion**. Brazil 2014: **$15–20 billion** in costs. Beijing 2008: reportedly **$40 billion**. The pattern: **cost overruns, underused venues, displaced investment, and debt service**.
- **Section 3: Why the benefits are overstated.** **Substitution effect** (visitors who would have come anyway); **crowding-out** of regular tourists and local spending; **leakage** of event revenue to international sponsors and construction firms; **double-counting** in economic impact studies; and **optimism bias** in forecasts.
- **Section 4: Why the costs are underestimated.** **Infrastructure costs** (transport, security, venues); **maintenance costs** after the event; **social costs** (displacement, policing, opportunity cost of public funds); and **environmental costs**.
- **Section 5: Conditions for positive legacy.** **Infrastructure readiness**, **governance quality**, **legacy planning**, and **alignment with long-term development strategy**. Events that fit existing plans and use **existing or temporary venues** are more likely to generate net benefits.
- **Conclusion.** Mega-events are **high-risk, high-visibility investments** whose net impact is **conditional on governance and planning**. They should be evaluated as **public investments**, not as marketing expenditures, and their benefits should be **measured ex post**, not asserted ex ante.

**Essay Question 3. Compare the economic and social consequences of the Las Vegas and Macau casino resort models, and draw lessons for tourism diversification.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** Both cities built economies around casino resorts, but they diverged in **diversification trajectories**: Las Vegas diversified into entertainment, conventions, dining, and retail; Macau remained heavily dependent on gaming.
- **Section 1: The Las Vegas model.** Gaming taxes around **35% of government revenue**; diversification into **non-gaming entertainment, conventions, and retail**; a **mass-market and premium** mix; and a **domestic and international** visitor base.
- **Section 2: The Macau model.** Overtook Las Vegas in gaming revenue within five years of liberalisation; **monocultural specialisation**; **social costs** (problem gambling, over-tourism, land pressure) and **economic vulnerability** to regulatory and demand shocks.
- **Section 3: Comparative consequences.** Las Vegas: **broader economic base, greater resilience, higher non-gaming revenue**. Macau: **higher gaming revenue per capita but greater concentration risk**, and a **diversification challenge** that has prompted efforts in healthcare tourism and other sectors.
- **Section 4: Lessons for tourism diversification.** (i) **Avoid monoculture**: a single-sector economy is vulnerable to demand and regulatory shocks; (ii) **Invest in non-gaming attractions and MICE** to broaden the visitor base and extend dwell time; (iii) **Regulate social costs** (gambling addiction, crime, displacement) proactively; (iv) **Plan land use** to prevent over-tourism and housing pressure; and (v) **Measure net benefits**, not gross gaming revenue.
- **Conclusion.** Casino resorts can generate substantial revenue, but **sustainable development requires diversification** and **active management of social costs**. The Las Vegas path is more resilient; the Macau path illustrates the risks of **specialisation without diversification**.

**Essay Question 4. "The organisation of tourism is a response to market failure: no single firm can supply the collective goods—standards, statistics, safety, and destination promotion—on which the industry depends." Discuss with reference to at least three levels of organisation.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** Define **collective goods** in tourism: aviation safety standards, statistical harmonisation, ethical frameworks, destination promotion, and infrastructure coordination. These are **non-excludable or non-rivalrous** to some degree, and markets underprovide them. Organisation at multiple levels is the response.
- **Section 1: Global level.** **ICAO** sets safety and navigation standards that no single airline could impose; **IATA** coordinates industry standards and settlement; **UN Tourism** harmonises statistics and ethics; **WTTC** measures economic impact. Each addresses a **coordination failure** that markets cannot solve.
- **Section 2: Regional level.** **PATA** and **ETC** promote regions as destinations and coordinate marketing that individual countries cannot fund at the same scale. They also provide **research and advocacy** that member NTOs use in domestic policy debates.
- **Section 3: National level.** **NTOs and NTAs** (BTB and BPC in Bangladesh) supply **destination branding, policy, regulation, and training**—functions that private firms cannot appropriate because the benefits accrue to the destination as a whole. The **BTB–BPC division** illustrates the **separation of marketing/policy from commercial operation**.
- **Section 4: Sub-national level.** **DMOs, RTOs, and CVBs** manage **place-based promotion and visitor management**, addressing **local coordination failures** (signage, events, congestion) that neither national nor global bodies can handle effectively.
- **Section 5: The limits of organisation.** Inter-organisational governance is **slow, political, and resource-constrained**. BTB's implementation gaps and BPC's commercial pressures illustrate the **gap between mandate and capacity**. Organisation is necessary but not sufficient; **governance quality** determines outcomes.
- **Conclusion.** Tourism's organisational architecture is a **rational response to collective-action problems**, but it is **imperfect and uneven**. The task for professionals is to **work within and improve** these organisations, not to assume that their existence guarantees effective coordination.

### 2.9.4 Applied Managerial Scenarios (with Complete Solutions)

**Scenario 1: The Mid-Market Hotel's Channel Dilemma**

*Riverside Hotel* is a 120-room mid-market property in Dhaka. Last month it sold **90 rooms per night on average** at an **ADR of $80**. Of those 90 rooms, **50 were booked through OTAs at 20% commission**, **30 were direct bookings**, and **10 were corporate accounts at a negotiated rate of $70 with no commission**. The hotel's fixed costs are **$1,800 per night**, and variable costs are **$15 per occupied room**.

**Questions:**
(a) Calculate the hotel's occupancy rate, ADR, gross RevPAR, and net RevPAR (after OTA commission).
(b) Calculate the hotel's daily gross operating profit.
(c) The hotel is considering a **direct-booking campaign** that would shift 15 of the 50 OTA bookings to direct bookings at the same ADR. What would be the impact on net RevPAR and daily gross operating profit?
(d) What **non-financial factors** should the hotel consider before implementing the campaign?

**Model Solution.**

(a) **Occupancy** = 90 ÷ 120 = **75%**. **ADR** = (50 × $80 + 30 × $80 + 10 × $70) ÷ 90 = ($4,000 + $2,400 + $700) ÷ 90 = $7,100 ÷ 90 = **$78.89**. **Gross RevPAR** = $78.89 × 0.75 = **$59.17** (or $7,100 ÷ 120 = $59.17). **OTA commission** = 50 × $80 × 0.20 = **$800**. **Net room revenue** = $7,100 − $800 = **$6,300**. **Net RevPAR** = $6,300 ÷ 120 = **$52.50**.

(b) **Revenue** = $7,100. **Variable costs** = 90 × $15 = $1,350. **Fixed costs** = $1,800. **Gross operating profit** = $7,100 − $1,350 − $1,800 = **$3,950 per night**.

(c) If 15 OTA bookings shift to direct at the same ADR: **OTA bookings** = 35; **direct bookings** = 45. **Revenue** remains $7,100 (same ADR for the shifted rooms). **OTA commission** = 35 × $80 × 0.20 = **$560**. **Net room revenue** = $7,100 − $560 = **$6,540**. **Net RevPAR** = $6,540 ÷ 120 = **$54.50** (an increase of **$2.00**). **Gross operating profit** = $6,540 − $1,350 − $1,800 = **$3,390** (an increase of **$440 per night**).

(d) **Non-financial factors**: (i) **OTA visibility and reach**—reducing OTA reliance may reduce exposure to new markets; (ii) **rate parity**—direct bookings at the same ADR may violate OTA rate-parity clauses; (iii) **customer relationship**—direct bookings enable **upselling, loyalty enrolment, and data capture**; (iv) **campaign cost**—the direct-booking campaign itself has marketing and technology costs that must be netted against the commission savings; (v) **channel conflict**—OTAs may **reduce visibility or demote** the hotel in search results if it aggressively pursues direct bookings.

**Scenario 2: The National Tourism Organisation's Coordination Challenge**

*Country X* has a newly established **NTO** (under a Tourism Act) and a **state-owned tourism corporation** that operates hotels and tours (under a Presidential Order). The NTO is responsible for **policy, branding, and marketing**; the corporation is responsible for **commercial operations and training**. The government has asked you to advise on how to improve coordination between the two bodies and with the private sector.

**Questions:**
(a) Identify the **principal coordination failures** that are likely to arise from this dual structure.
(b) Propose **three concrete mechanisms** to improve coordination, specifying who would be responsible for each.
(c) Explain how a **Tourism Satellite Account (TSA)** could support better coordination.

**Model Solution.**

(a) **Coordination failures**: (i) **Mandate overlap and confusion**—both bodies may claim to represent the national tourism interest, leading to **duplication or contradiction** in marketing messages; (ii) **Policy–operations disconnect**—the NTO may design policies that the corporation cannot implement commercially, or the corporation may operate in ways that undermine policy (e.g., undercutting private hotels with subsidised rates); (iii) **Data fragmentation**—the NTO may lack access to the corporation's operational data, and neither may have a complete picture of the sector; (iv) **Private-sector distrust**—the corporation's commercial role may create a **conflict of interest** when it competes with private firms while the NTO is supposed to champion the whole sector.

(b) **Mechanisms**: (i) **A joint steering committee** chaired by the Ministry, with representation from the NTO, the corporation, and private-sector associations, to **align annual marketing and investment plans** and resolve conflicts. Responsibility: **Ministry of Tourism**. (ii) **A shared destination data platform** that aggregates **visitor arrivals, expenditure, accommodation occupancy, and attraction admissions** from both public and private sources, with agreed data-sharing protocols. Responsibility: **NTO, with technical support from the national statistics office**. (iii) **A private-sector advisory council** that meets quarterly with the NTO and corporation to review **policy proposals, marketing campaigns, and investment incentives**, ensuring that private firms have a formal voice. Responsibility: **NTO, with secretariat support from the corporation**.

(c) A **TSA** would provide a **common evidentiary language** for all three parties. It would measure **tourism's direct value added, employment, and tax contribution** in a way that is consistent with national accounts, allowing the NTO to argue for **budget allocations** on the basis of **measured economic significance**, the corporation to **benchmark its performance** against the sector, and the private sector to **identify leakage and supply-chain gaps** that require policy attention. By making **trade-offs visible**, the TSA reduces the scope for **anecdotal claims** and **institutional rivalry**, and it supports **evidence-based coordination**.

## 2.10 Chapter Summary

This chapter has mapped the organisational architecture and sectoral composition of the tourism system.

**Organisational architecture.** Tourism requires **inter-organisational governance** because no single firm can supply the collective goods—standards, statistics, safety, ethics, and destination promotion—on which the industry depends. **UN Tourism** provides intergovernmental leadership, statistical harmonisation, and ethical frameworks; **WTTC** provides private-sector advocacy and economic impact measurement. **Developmental banks** finance infrastructure; **PATA** and **ETC** coordinate regional marketing and research. **NTOs and NTAs** provide national policy, branding, and regulation. In Bangladesh, **BTB** is the NTO responsible for policy, planning, and marketing, while **BPC** is the state commercial developer and operator, including the **NHTTI** training institute. **DMOs, RTOs, and CVBs** manage destinations and business events at the sub-national level.

**Transportation.** **Airlines** operate two dominant models: **FSCs** with hub-and-spoke networks, premium cabins, and global alliances; and **LCCs** with point-to-point networks, single-class cabins, and direct online distribution. **ICAO** sets governmental standards; **IATA** coordinates industry standards; the **nine Freedoms of the Air** define traffic rights. **Rail** competes on medium-distance corridors and offers luxury experiential products; **motorcoaches** serve group and scheduled intercity travel; **automobiles** enable flexible domestic and fly-drive tourism; and **cruise lines**—dominated by **Carnival** and **Royal Caribbean**—operate floating mega-resorts with high onboard revenue and growing private-destination investments.

**Hospitality.** **Lodging** is classified by size, market, service level, and rating, and operated through **independent, franchise, management-contract, chain, or REIT** structures. **Occupancy Rate, ADR, and RevPAR** are the core performance metrics. **Food service** spans fine dining to QSRs, and **gastronomy tourism** is a growing motivator. **MICE** generates high-yield business events through **corporate planners, association executives, PCOs, and convention centres**.

**Distribution.** Intermediaries perform **information, risk-bearing, bundling, and convenience** functions. **GDSs** (Amadeus, Sabre, Travelport) connect suppliers to agencies and corporate bookers; **OTAs** (Booking Holdings, Expedia Group) dominate consumer-facing distribution; **tour operators** create packages and bear inventory risk; **travel agents** sell on commission. In Bangladesh, **ShareTrip** and **GoZayaan** lead the OTA market, using **agency** and **merchant** models respectively.

**Attractions and support sectors.** **Attractions** (natural and cultural), **gaming** (Las Vegas and Macau models), **recreation** (national parks, adventure, ski), **entertainment** (theatre, concerts), **events** (mega-events and festivals), **shopping tourism** (duty-free, outlets, bazaars), and **support services** (education, insurance, forex, publishing) complete the sectoral map. **Mega-events** frequently fail to deliver the promised economic returns, and **casino resort monoculture** creates social and economic vulnerabilities; **diversification and governance quality** are the decisive variables.

## 2.11 Key Terms Glossary

1. **NTO (National Tourism Organization):** The national body responsible for destination branding, marketing, and promotion in overseas markets; in Bangladesh, the BTB.
2. **NTA (National Tourism Administration):** The national body responsible for tourism policy, regulation, and coordination; in Bangladesh, BPC performs commercial and developmental functions under its statutory mandate.
3. **Hub-and-spoke network:** An airline network that consolidates traffic through a central hub airport, feeding passengers from spoke airports into connecting flights.
4. **Point-to-point network:** An airline network that connects city pairs directly without consolidation through a hub; characteristic of LCCs.
5. **Freedoms of the Air:** The nine categories of traffic rights granted by states to foreign airlines, ranging from overflight to cabotage.
6. **GDS (Global Distribution System):** A computerised network (Amadeus, Sabre, Travelport) connecting travel sellers to airline, hotel, and car rental inventory.
7. **OTA (Online Travel Agency):** A digital intermediary that sells travel products on behalf of suppliers, typically on commission or merchant models.
8. **Tour operator:** A firm that creates package tours by assembling transport, accommodation, and ground services, and bears the risk of unsold inventory.
9. **Travel agent:** An intermediary that sells travel products created by others, typically earning a commission or service fee.
10. **Occupancy Rate (OCC%):** The percentage of available rooms sold in a given period.
11. **ADR (Average Daily Rate):** Total room revenue divided by rooms sold; the average price per occupied room.
12. **RevPAR (Revenue Per Available Room):** ADR multiplied by occupancy rate; the standard measure of revenue efficiency per available room.
13. **MICE:** Meetings, Incentives, Conferences, and Exhibitions; the business events segment of tourism.
14. **DMO (Destination Management Organization):** A place-based entity responsible for destination planning, management, and marketing.
15. **Franchising:** A lodging operating model in which the owner operates under a brand's system, paying fees for brand standards, reservation systems, and marketing.
