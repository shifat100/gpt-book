## 3.1 Motivation for Pleasure Travel

### 3.1.1 Consumer Behavior Foundations: Why Do People Travel?

Tourism is, at its core, a **purchase of experience**. Unlike a physical product, an experience cannot be inspected before purchase, cannot be stored, and is evaluated retrospectively through memory and storytelling. This makes the **psychology of the tourist** central to tourism management. Kotler et al. remind us that consumer behaviour is driven by a **hierarchy of needs, wants, and motivations**, and that tourism marketers must understand not only *who* travels but *why*. A demographic profile—age, income, gender, occupation—tells us who *can* travel; it does not tell us who *will* travel, or what they will seek when they arrive.

The **need for a theory** arises because simplistic demographic lists fail to explain the **heterogeneity of tourist behaviour**. Two travellers of identical age, income, and education may choose radically different holidays: one seeks a familiar beach resort with predictable service; the other seeks an off-the-beaten-path trek through unfamiliar terrain. Demographics describe **capacity**; motivation explains **direction**. Robust psychological models are therefore indispensable for segmentation, product design, and destination positioning.

### 3.1.2 Classic Travel Motivation Models

#### Graham Dann’s Push and Pull Factors

Dann (1977, 1981) proposed that tourist motivation comprises two complementary forces. **Push factors** are **internal, socio-psychological desires** that predispose an individual to travel: the need to **escape** from a perceived environment (home or work), to **relax**, to gain **prestige** or **ego-enhancement**, and to satisfy **anomie**—a sense of meaninglessness or isolation that travel is expected to remedy. Dann identified two principal push dimensions: **anomie** and **ego-enhancement**. **Pull factors**, by contrast, are **external, destination-driven attractions**: beaches, heritage sites, facilities, climate, and cultural offerings that induce the traveller to choose a particular destination *once the prior decision to travel has been made*.

The critical insight is **directionality**. Push factors originate in the **home environment** and push the tourist outward; pull factors originate in the **destination** and pull the tourist toward it. A traveller may be pushed by work stress (anomie/escape) and pulled by the promise of a quiet beach (destination attribute). The two forces operate in sequence: push creates the **general disposition to travel**; pull determines **where**.

**Table 3.1: Dann’s Push Factors versus Pull Factors**

| Dimension | Push Factors | Pull Factors |
|---|---|---|
| **Locus** | Internal to the individual | External to the individual (destination attributes) |
| **Origin** | Home/work environment | Destination region |
| **Nature** | Socio-psychological desires | Specific attractions and facilities |
| **Examples** | Escape, relaxation, prestige, ego-enhancement, anomie, novelty-seeking | Beaches, heritage sites, climate, nightlife, infrastructure, cultural events |
| **Function** | Create the disposition to travel | Determine destination choice |
| **Management implication** | Marketing must resonate with emotional needs | Destination must supply tangible attractions that match the push profile |

#### Seppo Iso-Ahola’s Motivational Model

Iso-Ahola (1982) proposed a **two-dimensional leisure motivation theory** built on the interplay of **escaping** and **seeking**. **Escaping** refers to the desire to leave behind **routine environments, everyday problems, stress, and familiar surroundings**; **seeking** refers to the pursuit of **intrinsic rewards** in the new environment—novelty, stimulation, learning, and social contact. Crucially, Iso-Ahola crossed these two dimensions with a **personal–interpersonal** axis, yielding four motivational cells: **personal escape** (getting away from one’s own routine and problems), **personal seeking** (seeking personal growth or intrinsic reward), **interpersonal escape** (getting away from routine social obligations), and **interpersonal seeking** (seeking new social relationships).

The model’s elegance lies in its recognition that tourism is **not merely flight from the mundane but also pursuit of the rewarding**. A tourist trekking in the Himalayas may be simultaneously escaping office routine (personal escape) and seeking a sense of achievement (personal seeking). The four-cell structure allows managers to segment markets by the **dominant motivational combination**, rather than by a single motive.

#### Philip Pearce’s Travel Career Ladder (TCL) and Travel Career Patterns (TCP)

Pearce adapted **Maslow’s hierarchy of needs** to tourism, proposing that tourists ascend a **Travel Career Ladder** as their travel experience accumulates. The TCL comprises five levels: **relaxation needs**, **safety/security needs**, **relationship needs**, **self-esteem and development needs**, and **self-actualization/fulfilment needs**. The core idea is developmental: novice travellers are preoccupied with **relaxation and safety**, while experienced travellers seek **relationship deepening, self-development, and fulfilment**.

The TCL was later refined into the **Travel Career Pattern (TCP)** , which relaxes the strict ladder metaphor. The TCP identifies **three layers of motivation**. At the **core**, three motives are important for **all travellers regardless of experience**: **novelty**, **escape/relaxation**, and **relationships**. The **middle layer** contains motives that vary with travel experience: **self-actualisation and self-enhancement** are associated with **lower** travel experience, while **kinship, nature, and self-development** are associated with **higher** travel experience. The **outer layer** comprises **14 motivational factors**, including autonomy, stimulation, isolation, nostalgia, romance, and recognition. The TCP is more empirically robust than the TCL because it recognises that travellers **move within** motivational layers rather than simply ascending a fixed hierarchy, and that core motives remain salient throughout the travel career.

#### Erik Cohen’s Typology of Tourist Experiences

Cohen (1979) proposed a **phenomenological typology** of five tourist experience modes, arranged along a continuum of **distance from the traveller’s familiar “centre”** —the home world of meanings, values, and routines. The five modes are:

- **Recreational mode:** The tourist seeks **entertainment, enjoyment, and well-being**; travel is a pleasant extension of the familiar, not a search for meaning.
- **Diversionary mode:** The tourist travels to **escape the boredom and stress of everyday reality**; travel is a diversion, a temporary release from routine, without a deeper quest.
- **Experiential mode:** The tourist feels a **lack of authentic and enriching experience** in daily life and seeks **renewed contact with social life, culture, and nature elsewhere**; the search is for **authenticity** in other places.
- **Experimental mode:** The tourist feels **alienated from their authentic self** and departs in search of **self-rediscovery** in a different social or natural environment; the search is **inward** as much as outward.
- **Existential mode:** The tourist experiences such **strong estrangement** from daily life that they seek a **better world elsewhere**; the destination becomes a **spiritual centre** to which the traveller may return repeatedly.

The typology is not a personality classification but a **mode of experience**: the same individual may travel in a recreational mode on one trip and an experimental mode on another.

**Table 3.2: Cohen’s Five Modes of Tourist Experience**

| Mode | Relationship to the “Centre” | Core Quest | Typical Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Recreational** | Centre is intact; travel is an extension of the familiar | Entertainment and well-being | Resort holidays, theme parks, familiar destinations |
| **Diversionary** | Centre is boring or stressful; travel is an escape | Forgetting routine | Short breaks, party tourism, escapist resorts |
| **Experiential** | Centre is deficient in authenticity; travel seeks it elsewhere | Authenticity in other cultures | Cultural tours, heritage visits, immersion travel |
| **Experimental** | Centre is alienating; travel seeks self-rediscovery | Self-discovery through experimentation | Backpacking, alternative lifestyles, extended stays |
| **Existential** | Centre is estranged; travel seeks a better world | Spiritual meaning and belonging | Pilgrimage, repeated return to a sacred or beloved place |

#### Stanley Plog’s Psychographic Continuum

Plog (1974, 2001) proposed a **psychographic continuum** of traveller personality types, ranging from **psychocentrics** (also called **dependables**) to **allocentrics** (also called **venturers**), with **midcentrics** in the middle. **Psychocentrics** prefer **familiar, low-risk destinations**, predictable experiences, and structured itineraries; they are **cautious** and prefer destinations that are **well-developed and accessible**. **Allocentrics** are **adventurous, self-confident, and novelty-seeking**; they prefer **unfamiliar, off-the-beaten-path destinations** and are willing to accept **higher risk and lower comfort** in exchange for **authenticity and discovery**~. **Midcentrics** combine elements of both and constitute the **largest market segment**.

Plog linked this continuum to the **destination life cycle**. A destination is “discovered” by **allocentrics**; as it becomes known and developed, it attracts **near-allocentrics and midcentrics**; eventually, it becomes dominated by **psychocentrics**, and its appeal to allocentrics declines. Critics note that the model **does not reliably predict** destination life cycles, that it ignores **physical and cultural distance**, and that **psychocentric destinations can thrive** rather than decline. The model is therefore best used as a **descriptive segmentation tool**, not as a deterministic law of destination evolution.

**Table 3.3: Plog’s Allocentrics versus Psychocentrics**

| Dimension | Allocentrics (Venturers) | Psychocentrics (Dependables) |
|---|---|---|
| **Personality** | Adventurous, self-confident, novelty-seeking, risk-tolerant | Cautious, routine-oriented, risk-averse, prefer familiarity |
| **Destination preference** | Undiscovered, exotic, off-the-beaten-path | Familiar, well-developed, accessible, predictable |
| **Activity choices** | Independent travel, exploration, cultural immersion, adventure | Package tours, resorts, guided sightseeing, structured activities |
| **Spending pattern** | Willing to accept lower comfort for authenticity; spend on experiences | Prefer predictable value; spend on comfort and convenience |
| **Life-cycle role** | Discover new destinations | Sustain mature destinations |
| **Management implication** | Market authenticity, novelty, and challenge | Market safety, reliability, and value |

## 3.2 Cultural and International Tourism for Life’s Enrichment

### 3.2.1 The Concept and Importance of Cultural Tourism

**Cultural tourism** is travel motivated by the desire to **learn, discover, experience, and consume the tangible and intangible cultural attractions of a destination**—heritage sites, museums, festivals, folklore, cuisine, and ways of life. It is important for three reasons. First, it **broadens horizons**: exposure to other cultures challenges ethnocentrism and fosters intercultural understanding. Second, it **enriches the traveller personally**: cultural travel is associated with **self-development, identity formation, and perspective transformation**. Third, it supports **cultural preservation**: when communities see that their heritage has economic and social value, they have stronger incentives to maintain it—provided that commodification does not hollow out the culture it purports to preserve.

### 3.2.2 Anthropography: The Geography of Humankind

**Anthropography** is the **geography of humankind**—a branch of anthropology that describes the **varieties of human communities, their cultural practices, folklore, living styles, and geographical distribution**. For tourism, anthropography is the **descriptive foundation** of cultural tourism: it tells us where communities live, what they do, how they organise their material and symbolic worlds, and what makes their way of life distinctive. Travel agents and destination managers who understand anthropography can **recommend cultural experiences** that match a client’s interests, and they can **anticipate cultural sensitivities** that might otherwise cause offence.

### 3.2.3 “Life-Seeing Tourism”

**Life-seeing tourism**, a concept attributed to **Axel Dessau of Denmark**, moves beyond the **superficial “sightseeing”** of monuments and highlights to a **purposeful immersion in the daily work, community activities, and domestic life of local people**. The life-seeing tourist does not merely photograph a fishing village; they may spend a morning with a fisher, learn about the tides and the catch, and share a meal prepared with the day’s harvest. The **Bahamas People-to-People Programme** is a classic example: it matches visitors with Bahamian volunteers for local activities, turning a beach holiday into a **cross-cultural encounter**. Life-seeing tourism is attractive because it promises **authenticity, connection, and learning**, but it must be managed carefully to avoid **intrusion, voyeurism, and the staging of “authentic” poverty** for consumption.

### 3.2.4 The Romance, Nostalgia, and Novelty of Pleasure Travel

Pleasure travel is saturated with **romance** (the anticipation of escape and adventure), **nostalgia** (the search for a lost or imagined past, often associated with rural life, heritage, and childhood memories), and **novelty** (the desire for the unfamiliar and stimulating). These emotional registers are responses, in part, to **industrial urbanisation**: the modern traveller seeks in the countryside, the heritage town, or the exotic destination a **counter-image** of the rationalised, commodified, and hurried world of everyday life. This is the **search for authenticity** that MacCannell and Cohen identified as central to modern tourism. The management challenge is to supply **romance, nostalgia, and novelty** without **fabricating** a past that never existed or **freezing** living cultures in a museum-like “traditional” state.

### 3.2.5 Developmental and Promotional Measures for Cultural Tourism

Destinations can develop cultural tourism through several instruments:

- **Heritage trails** that link sites into a coherent narrative and disperse visitors across a region.
- **Living history museums** that use interpretation, demonstration, and participatory activity to bring the past to life.
- **Authentic artisan villages** where crafts are produced and sold, with the **producer visible** and fairly remunerated.
- **Cultural festivals** that celebrate local music, dance, cuisine, and craft, preferably **co-curated with the community** rather than staged solely for tourists.

The guiding principle is **community participation**: cultural tourism that is **done to** a community rather than **done with** it is more likely to produce resentment, commodification, and the erosion of the very culture it seeks to showcase.

### 3.2.6 Types of Destinations and Travel Experiences

Cultural tourism destinations include **heritage cities** (Rome, Kyoto, Dhaka’s old town), **rural farming hamlets** (agritourism, farm stays), **tribal and indigenous regions** (the Chittagong Hill Tracts, the Amazon, Sarawak), **cultural capitals** (designated cities that showcase arts and heritage), and **culinary trails** (food and wine routes that connect producers, markets, and restaurants). Each type has a distinctive **carrying capacity** and **management requirement**: heritage cities face congestion and conservation pressure; indigenous regions face **representation and consent** challenges; culinary trails require **supply-chain development** and **quality assurance**.

### 3.2.7 Other Tourist Appeals

Beyond the cultural core, destinations appeal through **architecture** (buildings, urban form, vernacular design), **history** (monuments, battlefields, museums), **cuisine** (gastronomy, street food, markets), **religion** (pilgrimage sites, festivals, sacred landscapes), and **art and literature** (literary trails, artist homes, performance traditions). These appeals are not mutually exclusive: a single destination may combine architectural, historical, culinary, religious, and artistic attractions into a **layered cultural product**.

## 3.3 Sociology of Tourism

### 3.3.1 Social Impacts on the Individual

Tourism affects the traveller as a **psychological and social being**. It can provide **psychological rejuvenation**—relief from stress, restoration of attention, and a sense of wellbeing. It can contribute to **identity formation**: travel experiences become part of the **narrative of the self**, and the traveller returns with stories that confirm or revise their sense of who they are. But travel also produces **anxiety**: fear of the unknown, language barriers, safety concerns, and the disorientation of unfamiliar social codes.

**Culture shock**, conceptualised by **Kalervo Oberg**, is the **disorientation and distress** experienced when entering an unfamiliar cultural environment. Oberg’s stages are commonly described as **honeymoon** (initial fascination), **disintegration** (confusion and withdrawal), **reintegration** (gradual adjustment and assertiveness), and **autonomy** (effective functioning and enjoyment). Not every traveller passes through all stages, and the severity depends on **cultural distance, prior experience, personality, and social support**. Culture shock is not merely a cost: it can be a **learning experience** that produces **perspective transformation**—a durable shift in the traveller’s values, beliefs, and sense of self.

### 3.3.2 Social Impacts on the Family

Tourism can act as a **catalyst for family cohesion**: shared travel experiences create **memories, rituals, and narratives** that strengthen family bonds, particularly in families whose daily lives are fragmented by work and schooling. The **structures** of family travel are changing. **Single-parent travel** is growing as family forms diversify. **Multi-generational or “skip-gen” travel**—grandparents travelling with grandchildren, sometimes without the middle generation—reflects longer healthy lifespans, grandparental affluence, and the desire for **intergenerational bonding**. Tourism providers must adapt products, pricing, and accessibility to these **new family configurations**.

### 3.3.3 Social Impacts on Society

At the societal level, tourism can promote **social modernisation**—the adoption of new technologies, organisational forms, and norms—and **intercultural dialogue**, as hosts and guests encounter each other’s worlds. But it can also generate **social tension**. The **demonstration effect** describes the process by which **local residents, especially youth, imitate the consumption patterns and behaviours of affluent tourists**, leading to **rising material aspirations, changed diets, and altered social values**. The demonstration effect can stimulate entrepreneurship, but it can also produce **frustration** when aspirations cannot be met, and it can **erode local traditions** that are perceived as backward relative to tourist lifestyles.

**Commodification of culture** occurs when **sacred rituals, festivals, crafts, and performances are packaged and sold as tourism products**, often stripped of their original meaning. A religious ceremony performed on schedule for paying tourists is no longer the same ceremony; it has become a **performance** of itself. Commodification can generate income and incentivise cultural transmission, but it can also produce **“staged authenticity”** —the substitution of a tourist-friendly simulacrum for a living tradition—and **cultural alienation** among the young, who may come to see their heritage primarily as a commodity.

### 3.3.4 Life Characteristics and Travel Patterns

Travel propensity is shaped by **life characteristics**:

- **Age**: younger travellers often seek novelty, adventure, and social contact; older travellers may prioritise comfort, safety, and cultural depth, though the stereotype of the sedentary senior is increasingly inaccurate.
- **Education**: higher education is associated with **greater interest in cultural tourism**, **more independent travel**, and **greater tolerance for cultural difference**.
- **Household income**: income determines **discretionary capacity**, but the relationship is not linear; **relative income** and **aspiration** also matter.
- **Gender**: gender shapes **perceived safety, mobility constraints, and activity preferences**, and the tourism industry has historically been slow to recognise the needs of **women travellers, solo parents, and LGBTQ+ travellers**.
- **Family life-cycle stage**: **youth** (high novelty-seeking, budget constraints), **married with young children** (family-oriented, safety-conscious, school-holiday constrained), **empty-nesters** (higher disposable income, cultural and comfort-oriented), and **retirees** (time-rich, health-constrained, often seasonally flexible). The **empty-nester and retiree segments** are particularly important for **cultural tourism and off-season travel**.

### 3.3.5 Emergence of Group Travel Patterns

The **inclusive package group tour** emerged in the nineteenth century (Chapter 1) and remains a dominant mode for **first-time travellers, older travellers, and markets where language or logistical complexity is a barrier**. The **social dynamics of travelling with strangers** are distinctive: the group becomes a **temporary social system** with norms, roles, and conflicts, and the tour guide functions as a **boundary-spanner** between the group and the host culture. **Affinity group travel**—groups organised around a shared identity, interest, or affiliation (religious congregations, alumni associations, hobby clubs, professional bodies)—is a growing segment because it combines **social connection** with **specialised content**.

### 3.3.6 Social (Subsidized/Accessible) Tourism

**Social tourism** is the **organised provision of travel opportunities to people who would otherwise be excluded** by income, disability, age, or social circumstance. Its philosophy rests on the claim that **travel is a human right** and a component of wellbeing, not a luxury reserved for the affluent. In Europe, **holiday voucher systems** (such as France’s *chèques-vacances* and Belgium’s *vakantiecheques*) subsidise holidays for workers and low-income households, and **state welfare programmes** fund breaks for disadvantaged children, carers, and people with disabilities. In East Asia, **subsidised domestic tourism programmes** have been used to stimulate demand during downturns and to broaden access. In Bangladesh, **formal social tourism policy is limited**, but **charitable and community initiatives**—such as subsidised trips for underprivileged children, free bus tickets for Eid travel for low-income families, and community-based tourism trusts—provide **informal social tourism** functions. The case for social tourism is **normative** (equity, wellbeing, social inclusion) as well as **economic** (seasonality smoothing, domestic demand stabilisation).

### 3.3.7 Summary of Principal Social Effects of International Tourism: The Host–Guest Relationship and Doxey’s Irridex

The **host–guest relationship** is the core sociological encounter of tourism. Guests bring **money, curiosity, and expectations**; hosts bring **homes, culture, and labour**. The relationship is structured by **power asymmetries**—guests are typically wealthier and more mobile; hosts are typically dependent on guest expenditure—and by **cultural distance**. Where the relationship is **well managed**, it can produce **mutual respect, cultural pride, and economic benefit**; where it is **poorly managed**, it can produce **exploitation, resentment, and hostility**.

**George Doxey’s Irritation Index (Irridex)** models the **evolution of host attitudes** across four stages. In **Stage 1: Euphoria**, the number of tourists is small, and the local community **welcomes** tourism with enthusiasm; visitors are seen as a source of income and prestige. In **Stage 2: Apathy**, tourist numbers increase, and the relationship becomes **formalised and commercial**; residents are indifferent rather than enthusiastic. In **Stage 3: Annoyance (Irritation)** , residents become **concerned** about tourism as arrivals grow and **competition for resources** (land, water, infrastructure) intensifies; doubts are expressed about the value of tourism. In **Stage 4: Antagonism**, tourists are **blamed for everything bad** that has happened in the community, and **overt hostility** may emerge. Later versions add a **fifth stage** of **final resignation**, in which residents accept the situation because tourism is indispensable to the local economy.

The Irridex is a **diagnostic tool**, not a law. It has been criticised for assuming **homogeneity** within the host community, for ignoring the **multidimensionality** of resident attitudes, and for lacking a detailed empirical foundation. In practice, different segments of a community may be at **different stages simultaneously**: entrepreneurs may remain in euphoria while residents displaced by rising rents have already reached antagonism. The management task is to **monitor resident sentiment, distribute benefits equitably, and intervene before annoyance becomes antagonism**.

**Table 3.4: Doxey’s Irridex Model**

| Stage | Local Sentiment | Manifestation | Tourism Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Euphoria** | Welcome, enthusiasm, curiosity | Hospitality, pride, early entrepreneurship | Small, exploratory |
| **2. Apathy** | Indifference, formality | Commercial transactions replace social encounters | Growing, organised |
| **3. Annoyance (Irritation)** | Concern, doubt, frustration | Complaints about crowding, inflation, and resource competition | Approaching saturation |
| **4. Antagonism** | Hostility, blame | Overt opposition, protest, degradation of visitor experience | Saturation or over-capacity |
| **5. Resignation (later addition)** | Acceptance, fatalism | Adaptation to tourism as unavoidable | Entrenched mass tourism |

### 3.3.8 The International Tourist: Profile, Behavioural Expectations, and Consumer Responsibilities

The **international tourist** is not a homogeneous category. **Profiles** vary by **source market, purpose, age, and psychographic type**: the European cultural traveller, the Gulf leisure family, the South Asian pilgrimage group, and the East Asian shopping tourist are distinct in motivation, behaviour, and expectation. **Behavioural expectations** in host territories are shaped by **cultural scripts**: expectations of service, punctuality, bargaining, personal space, photography, and dress vary widely, and **misreading them produces friction**. **Consumer responsibilities** include **cultural respect, environmental care, legal compliance, and economic fairness**—paying fair prices, avoiding exploitative activities, and respecting sacred sites. The responsible international tourist is not merely a consumer but a **guest** in a **host–guest relationship** that carries obligations as well as rights.

## 3.4 Bangladesh Sociological Vignette: The Chittagong Hill Tracts and Sreemangal

**The Chittagong Hill Tracts (CHT)** —comprising **Rangamati, Bandarban, and Khagrachhari**—is home to **eleven indigenous communities** (the Jumma or Pahari peoples), whose history, culture, religion, language, and economy are distinct from the Bengali majority. Tourism development in the CHT accelerated after the **CHT Peace Accord of 1997**, and it has been promoted by state agencies as a instrument of **peacebuilding, regional integration, and economic development**. The sociological dynamics are complex. On one hand, indigenous communities have used tourism as a **platform for cultural entrepreneurship and self-representation**: they brand their **festivals, traditional foods, and homesteads** as cultural products, and in doing so they assert **cultural custodianship** against external portrayals of “primitiveness”. On the other hand, state and private actors have often staged the CHT through **false images of primitiveness**, and tourism has reproduced **“othering”** representations that indigenous communities must then **negotiate and resist**. The concept of **“neolocal tourism”** has been proposed to describe this dual process: tourism offers a **safeguard against cultural rootlessness** and a **constructed self-space for wellbeing**, but it operates within **unequal market relations** that constrain indigenous agency.

**Sreemangal** (Moulvibazar district) illustrates a different configuration. Known as the **“tea capital”** of Bangladesh, Sreemangal is home to **Khasi, Manipuri, Tripura, and other indigenous communities**, whose **handloom weaving, betel-leaf cultivation, pottery, and crafts** have become tourism attractions. **Ramnagar**, a small Manipuri village, has become a popular destination where residents **sell handicrafts** to visitors. The **Harmony Festival** in Sreemangal, organised to celebrate the cultures of **27 indigenous communities**, features **live demonstrations** of Khasi *paan* cultivation, Tripura waist-loom weaving, Manipuri handloom work, and traditional pottery. The sociological risks are well documented: **cultural commodification, environmental pollution, and social conflict** are identified as the main concerns associated with tourism’s effects on Khasia cultural identity. The **demonstration effect** is visible in changing consumption patterns and aspirations among youth, and the **staging of culture** for festival audiences raises questions about **authenticity and consent**.

The Bangladesh case demonstrates that **life-seeing tourism** is both an opportunity and a risk. Where it is **community-led**—where indigenous hosts control representation, capture a fair share of revenue, and set the terms of encounter—it can strengthen cultural pride and economic agency. Where it is **externally staged**—where tour operators, state agencies, or outside investors determine the narrative and capture the surplus—it can reproduce **commodification, othering, and the demonstration effect**. The policy implication is that cultural tourism in the CHT and Sreemangal must be governed by **free, prior, and informed consent, benefit-sharing, and community participation** in planning and interpretation.

## 3.5 Applied Behavioral Case Framework: Profiling a Tourist Segment

**Segment:** *“Heritage Seekers”* —a group of 35–55-year-old, university-educated, mid-to-high-income travellers from Dhaka who book weekend trips to Sreemangal and the CHT.

**Diagnostic using Plog.** The segment is **near-allocentric to midcentric**. They are not pure venturers—they require **comfortable transport, reliable accommodation, and a degree of structure**—but they seek **novelty, cultural depth, and “real” experiences** beyond the standard sightseeing circuit. Their destination preferences are **emerging rather than mass-developed**: they are attracted to Sreemangal’s tea gardens and indigenous villages, and to Bandarban’s hill landscapes, precisely because these places are **not yet fully commodified**.

**Diagnostic using Dann.** The **push factors** are **escape from Dhaka’s congestion and routine** (anomie/escape), **restoration and relaxation** (the tea-garden landscape as a restorative environment), and **ego-enhancement** (the prestige of “discovering” authentic indigenous culture and sharing it on social media). The **pull factors** are the **natural landscape** (tea gardens, hills, rivers), **cultural difference** (indigenous weaving, festivals, cuisine), and the **promise of authenticity** (life-seeing encounters with host communities).

**Diagnostic using Iso-Ahola.** **Personal escape** is strong: the segment wants to leave behind work stress and urban noise. **Personal seeking** is also strong: they want **learning, cultural enrichment, and a sense of personal growth**. **Interpersonal seeking** is moderate: the trip is often undertaken with a partner or small group of friends, and the encounter with hosts is valued as a **social connection**. **Interpersonal escape** is weaker: they are not primarily fleeing social obligations.

**Management implications.** The segment is best served by **small-group, community-partnered itineraries** that combine **comfort with authenticity**, **interpretation with free time**, and **cultural respect with photography and storytelling**. Marketing should emphasise **restoration, learning, and genuine encounter**, not luxury or spectacle. Pricing should be **premium but fair**, with a **transparent community benefit component**. The experience should be **co-designed with host communities** to avoid staged authenticity and to ensure that the “heritage” being consumed is **living, respected, and fairly remunerated**.

## 3.6 End-of-Chapter Exam Preparation Pack

### 3.6.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions (with Model Answers)

**Question 1. Distinguish between Dann’s push factors and pull factors, and explain why the distinction matters for destination marketing.**

**Model Answer.** **Push factors** are **internal socio-psychological desires**—escape, relaxation, prestige, ego-enhancement, and anomie—that originate in the **home environment** and create the **general disposition to travel**. **Pull factors** are **external destination attributes**—beaches, heritage, climate, facilities—that determine **where** the traveller goes, once the decision to travel has been made. The distinction matters because **push and pull operate at different stages** of the decision process. Push factors are the **raw material of motivation**; pull factors are the **specific magnets** that convert motivation into destination choice. Marketing that addresses only pull factors (e.g., “come to our beach”) will fail to activate travellers who lack the **push** disposition; marketing that addresses only push factors (e.g., “you need a break”) will fail to differentiate the destination. Effective destination marketing **aligns pull attributes with the dominant push profiles** of target markets.

---

**Question 2. Explain Iso-Ahola’s seeking and escaping model and its four motivational cells.**

**Model Answer.** Iso-Ahola (1982) proposed that tourism motivation is a **two-dimensional** phenomenon: **escaping** from routine environments and **seeking** intrinsic rewards in new environments. Crossing these dimensions with a **personal–interpersonal** axis yields four cells: **personal escape** (leaving one’s own routine and problems), **personal seeking** (seeking personal growth or intrinsic reward), **interpersonal escape** (leaving routine social obligations), and **interpersonal seeking** (seeking new social relationships). The model’s strength is that it recognises tourism as **both flight and pursuit**: a traveller may be simultaneously escaping work stress and seeking a sense of achievement. It allows managers to segment markets by **motivational combination** rather than by a single motive, and to design products that satisfy multiple cells simultaneously (e.g., a trek that offers escape from routine and personal seeking through challenge).

---

**Question 3. Summarise Pearce’s Travel Career Ladder and explain how the Travel Career Pattern refined it.**

**Model Answer.** The **Travel Career Ladder (TCL)** adapts Maslow’s hierarchy to tourism, proposing five levels of motivation: **relaxation, safety/security, relationships, self-esteem and development, and self-actualization/fulfilment**. The idea is **developmental**: as travellers gain experience, they ascend the ladder from basic relaxation and safety toward relationship deepening and self-fulfilment. The **Travel Career Pattern (TCP)** refined the TCL in two ways. First, it replaced the **strict ladder** with a **layered pattern**: a **core** of motives—**novelty, escape/relaxation, and relationships**—that are important for **all travellers regardless of experience**; a **middle layer** of motives that vary with experience (self-actualisation and self-enhancement for **lower** experience; kinship, nature, and self-development for **higher** experience); and an **outer layer** of 14 specific factors. Second, it recognised that travellers **move within** motivational layers rather than simply ascending a fixed hierarchy. The TCP is more empirically robust and is the preferred framework in contemporary tourism research.

---

**Question 4. Outline Cohen’s five modes of tourist experience and explain the continuum on which they are arranged.**

**Model Answer.** Cohen (1979) arranged five modes along a continuum of **distance from the traveller’s familiar “centre”** —the home world of meanings and routines. The **recreational** mode seeks **entertainment and well-being**; travel is an extension of the familiar. The **diversionary** mode seeks **escape from boredom and stress**; travel is a diversion without a deeper quest. The **experiential** mode seeks **authenticity** because daily life feels lacking in enriching experience. The **experimental** mode seeks **self-rediscovery** in a different environment, driven by alienation from the authentic self. The **existential** mode seeks a **better world elsewhere**, driven by deep estrangement from daily life; the destination becomes a **spiritual centre**. The continuum runs from **low distance** (recreational) to **high distance** (existential): the further the traveller moves from the familiar centre, the more the experience becomes a **quest for meaning** rather than mere enjoyment.

---

**Question 5. Define culture shock using Oberg’s stages and explain how it can produce perspective transformation.**

**Model Answer.** **Culture shock** is the **disorientation and distress** experienced when entering an unfamiliar cultural environment. Oberg’s stages are **honeymoon** (fascination), **disintegration** (confusion and withdrawal), **reintegration** (gradual adjustment and assertiveness), and **autonomy** (effective functioning and enjoyment). Not every traveller passes through all stages, and severity depends on **cultural distance, prior experience, personality, and social support**. Culture shock can produce **perspective transformation**—a durable shift in values, beliefs, and self-understanding—because the **disorientation of the unfamiliar** forces the traveller to **examine taken-for-granted assumptions** about their own culture. The traveller who successfully navigates culture shock may return with **greater cultural relativism, empathy, and self-awareness**. This is why **well-managed cultural tourism** can be a **developmental experience**, not merely a consumption of difference.

---

**Question 6. Explain Doxey’s Irridex model and identify its principal limitations.**

**Model Answer.** Doxey’s **Irritation Index (Irridex)** models the evolution of **host attitudes** across four stages: **euphoria** (welcome and enthusiasm when tourist numbers are small), **apathy** (indifference and formalisation as numbers grow), **annoyance/irritation** (concern about crowding, inflation, and resource competition), and **antagonism** (hostility and blame when tourism is perceived as the source of all problems). A **fifth stage of resignation** was later added. The model’s **limitations** are that it assumes **homogeneity** within the host community, ignores the **multidimensionality** of resident attitudes, and lacks a detailed **empirical foundation**. In practice, different segments of a community may be at **different stages simultaneously**: entrepreneurs may remain euphoric while displaced residents are already antagonistic. The model is therefore best used as a **diagnostic heuristic**, not as a deterministic prediction.

### 3.6.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions (with Full Structural Outlines)

**Essay Question 1. “Motivation theories in tourism are complementary rather than competing.” Critically evaluate this statement with reference to Dann, Iso-Ahola, Pearce, Cohen, and Plog.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** State the thesis: the five theories address **different aspects** of tourist motivation—**content** (what motivates), **process** (how motivation is structured), **development** (how motivation changes over the travel career), **experience** (what the tourist seeks), and **personality** (who the tourist is). They are **complementary lenses**, not rival explanations.
- **Section 1: Dann (content).** Push and pull factors identify the **substantive motives** (escape, prestige, destination attributes). Strength: parsimonious and widely used. Limitation: **static**; does not explain how motives change.
- **Section 2: Iso-Ahola (process).** Seeking and escaping identify the **psychological mechanism**: tourism is both flight and pursuit. Strength: captures **simultaneity** of motives. Limitation: does not specify **which** rewards are sought.
- **Section 3: Pearce (development).** TCL and TCP explain how motives **evolve with travel experience**. Strength: **dynamic** and empirically refined. Limitation: the **ladder** metaphor was too rigid and was revised.
- **Section 4: Cohen (experience).** The five modes explain the **meaning** the tourist attributes to the experience. Strength: **phenomenological depth**. Limitation: a **typology**, not a causal theory.
- **Section 5: Plog (personality).** The psychocentric–allocentric continuum explains **who** chooses which destinations. Strength: useful for **segmentation**. Limitation: **weak predictive validity** for destination life cycles.
- **Section 6: Integration.** A **layered framework**: Plog identifies the **traveller type**; Dann identifies the **push/pull content**; Iso-Ahola identifies the **escaping/seeking mechanism**; Pearce identifies the **developmental stage**; Cohen identifies the **experiential meaning**. Used together, they provide a **rich diagnostic profile** for destination management.
- **Conclusion.** The theories are **complementary** because they answer different questions. The professional task is **triangulation**: using multiple models to build a **robust understanding** of a segment, rather than treating any single model as exhaustive.

---

**Essay Question 2. “Cultural tourism is a double-edged sword: it can preserve culture or commodify it.” Discuss with reference to the Chittagong Hill Tracts and Sreemangal.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** State the paradox: cultural tourism can generate **income, pride, and preservation incentives**, but it can also produce **commodification, staged authenticity, and cultural erosion**.
- **Section 1: The preservation case.** Tourism gives communities a **market reason** to maintain crafts, festivals, and languages; it can strengthen **cultural pride** and **intergenerational transmission**; and it can fund **conservation and education**.
- **Section 2: The commodification case.** Sacred rituals become **scheduled performances**; crafts become **souvenirs** stripped of meaning; communities are staged as **“primitive”** for external consumption. In the CHT, state and private actors have reproduced **false images of primitiveness**, and indigenous communities must **resist and reframe** these representations.
- **Section 3: The CHT experience.** Tourism after the **1997 Peace Accord** has been used for **peacebuilding and integration**, but it has also reproduced **othering** and **unequal market relations**. Indigenous communities have responded with **self-commodification** as a **livelihood strategy and a site of struggle**.
- **Section 4: The Sreemangal experience.** Khasi, Manipuri, and Tripura communities have become tourism attractions through **handloom weaving, betel-leaf cultivation, and crafts**. The **Harmony Festival** celebrates 27 indigenous communities, but **cultural commodification, environmental pollution, and social conflict** are documented concerns.
- **Section 5: Governance conditions for preservation.** **Community ownership and consent**; **benefit-sharing**; **fair pricing**; **interpretation that respects meaning**; **limits on visitor volumes**; and **monitoring of social impacts**.
- **Conclusion.** Cultural tourism is **not inherently preservative or commodifying**: its outcome depends on **who controls representation, who captures value, and whether the community sets the terms of encounter**.

---

**Essay Question 3. “The host–guest relationship is the central sociological encounter of tourism.” Evaluate this claim using Doxey’s Irridex and the concepts of the demonstration effect and commodification.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** The host–guest relationship is central because tourism is a **social encounter** structured by **power, culture, and exchange**. It shapes **resident attitudes, visitor satisfaction, and the sustainability** of the destination.
- **Section 1: The structure of the encounter.** Guests bring **money, curiosity, and expectations**; hosts bring **homes, culture, and labour**. The relationship is marked by **asymmetry** (wealth, mobility) and **cultural distance**.
- **Section 2: Doxey’s Irridex.** The four (or five) stages trace the **evolution of host sentiment** from **euphoria to antagonism/resignation**. The model is a **diagnostic tool**, but it oversimplifies **community heterogeneity**.
- **Section 3: The demonstration effect.** Local youth imitate **affluent tourist behaviours**, leading to **rising aspirations, changed diets, and altered values**. This can stimulate **entrepreneurship** but also produce **frustration and cultural erosion**.
- **Section 4: Commodification.** Sacred rituals, crafts, and festivals are **packaged for sale**, producing **staged authenticity** and **cultural alienation**. The **CHT and Sreemangal** cases illustrate both the **resistance** and the **self-commodification** responses.
- **Section 5: Mitigating friction.** **Community participation** in planning; **equitable benefit-sharing**; **visitor education**; **carrying-capacity limits**; **support for cultural transmission**; and **monitoring of resident sentiment**.
- **Conclusion.** The host–guest relationship is central because it is where the **costs and benefits of tourism are most directly experienced**. Managing it requires **sociological imagination**, not only economic calculation.

---

**Essay Question 4. “Social tourism is a right, not a privilege.” Discuss the philosophy and practice of subsidised travel, with reference to European voucher systems and Bangladesh’s informal initiatives.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** State the normative claim: **travel is a component of wellbeing and social inclusion**, and access should not be determined solely by ability to pay.
- **Section 1: The philosophy of social tourism.** **Equity, wellbeing, social inclusion, and the right to leisure** (recognised in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 24). Travel provides **restoration, learning, family cohesion, and social participation**.
- **Section 2: European models.** **France’s chèques-vacances**, **Belgium’s vakantiecheques**, and state programmes for **disadvantaged children, carers, and people with disabilities**. These systems subsidise demand, support **seasonality smoothing**, and sustain **social tourism accommodation**.
- **Section 3: East Asian models.** **Subsidised domestic tourism** used to **stimulate demand** during downturns and to **broaden access**.
- **Section 4: Bangladesh’s informal social tourism.** Formal policy is limited, but **charitable initiatives**—subsidised trips for underprivileged children, free Eid bus tickets, community tourism trusts—provide **informal social tourism** functions. These are **fragmented and donor-dependent**, not rights-based.
- **Section 5: The case for formalisation.** A **national social tourism policy** could **target low-income households, persons with disabilities, and marginalised communities**, with **voucher systems, accessible infrastructure, and partnerships with community tourism enterprises**.
- **Conclusion.** Social tourism is a **rights-based complement** to commercial tourism, not a substitute. It **humanises** the sector and **broadens** its developmental reach.

### 3.6.4 Applied Sociological Scenarios (with Model Solutions)

**Scenario 1: The Hill Village at the Tipping Point**

*Hill Village* in Bandarban has seen tourist arrivals grow from a few hundred to over 20,000 per year in five years. Residents initially welcomed visitors, but now complain about **litter, noise, and rising land prices**. Some young people have adopted **tourist dress and music**, and a **sacred annual ritual** is now performed **weekly for paying tourists**. A local entrepreneur has built a **guesthouse and souvenir shop**; other residents feel excluded from the benefits.

**Questions:**
(a) Using Doxey’s Irridex, identify the stage(s) the community is likely passing through, and explain why different segments may be at different stages.
(b) Identify and explain the sociological processes visible in the scenario (demonstration effect, commodification, inequity).
(c) Propose a management strategy to mitigate conflict and move toward a more sustainable host–guest relationship.

**Model Solution.**

(a) The community is likely moving from **euphoria** into **annoyance/irritation**. Initial welcome has given way to **concern about litter, noise, and rising land prices**—classic irritation-stage symptoms. However, the entrepreneur who owns the guesthouse and shop is probably still in **euphoria** (benefiting economically), while residents displaced from land or excluded from revenue may be approaching **antagonism**. The Irridex’s assumption of homogeneity is therefore **violated**: different segments are at different stages simultaneously.

(b) The **demonstration effect** is visible in young people adopting **tourist dress and music**, indicating **imitation of affluent visitor lifestyles**. **Commodification** is evident in the **sacred ritual** being performed **weekly for paying tourists**, stripping it of its original calendrical and spiritual meaning. **Inequity** is visible in the **exclusion of other residents** from tourism benefits, which is likely to deepen resentment and accelerate the move toward antagonism.

(c) A **participatory destination management plan** should: (i) **establish a community tourism committee** with representation from all segments, including those excluded from current benefits; (ii) **introduce a visitor management framework**—entry fees, waste management, noise limits, and a **cap on visitor numbers** during peak periods; (iii) **negotiate a benefit-sharing agreement** that channels a proportion of tourism revenue into **community infrastructure and cultural preservation**; (iv) **support alternative livelihoods** so that tourism is not the only economic option; (v) **work with cultural custodians** to restore the sacred ritual to its **original timing and meaning**, and to develop **alternative cultural experiences** that do not commodify the sacred; and (vi) **monitor resident sentiment** regularly to detect the shift from irritation to antagonism before it becomes entrenched.

---

**Scenario 2: The Sreemangal Handloom Cooperative**

A **Manipuri handloom weaving cooperative** in Sreemangal has seen demand for its textiles rise as tourism grows. The cooperative sells **authentic handwoven fabrics** at fair prices, but tour operators are pressuring it to **produce cheaper, simplified “tourist versions”** in larger volumes. Some younger members want to **modernise designs** and use **power looms** to increase output; older members insist on **traditional techniques and patterns**.

**Questions:**
(a) Analyse the tension using the concepts of **commodification, authenticity, and intergenerational change**.
(b) What are the **risks** of accepting the tour operators’ demands, and what are the **risks** of refusing them?
(c) Propose a **negotiated strategy** that balances cultural preservation, economic viability, and intergenerational equity.

**Model Solution.**

(a) The tension is a classic **commodification** dilemma. The tour operators want **cheaper, simplified products** for mass consumption, which would **dilute authenticity** and potentially **reduce the cooperative’s control over its cultural product**. The **intergenerational dimension** is also significant: younger members see **modernisation** (power looms, new designs) as a path to **higher income and market relevance**, while older members see **traditional techniques** as the **core of cultural identity**. Both positions have validity: **authenticity is not static**, but **commodification can hollow out the very craft that makes the product distinctive**.

(b) **Accepting the demands** risks: loss of **authenticity and cultural meaning**; **price competition** that drives down margins; **dependence on a single buyer**; and **intergenerational conflict** as older weavers feel their knowledge is devalued. **Refusing** risks: **loss of income** for cooperative members; **declining demand**; and **younger members leaving** the cooperative for other work.

(c) A **negotiated strategy** could include: (i) **market segmentation**—continue producing **high-quality traditional textiles** for discerning buyers and cultural tourists, while developing a **separate, clearly labelled “modern” line** for volume buyers, so that the two product tiers do not cannibalise each other; (ii) **certification and storytelling**—label products with the **weaver’s name, technique, and cultural significance**, turning authenticity into a **price premium**; (iii) **direct-to-consumer sales** through **cooperative-owned retail and online channels**, reducing dependence on tour operators; (iv) **intergenerational dialogue**—formalise a **skills-transfer programme** in which older weavers teach traditional techniques to younger members, while younger members contribute **design and marketing innovation**; and (v) **collective bargaining** with tour operators through a **cooperative federation**, rather than accepting terms individually.

## 3.7 Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the psychological and sociological foundations of tourism.

**Motivation.** Tourist motivation is explained by **complementary models**. **Dann** distinguishes **push** (internal, socio-psychological) from **pull** (external, destination) factors. **Iso-Ahola** identifies the **seeking–escaping** dynamic across personal and interpersonal dimensions. **Pearce** traces the **travel career** from relaxation and safety toward relationship and self-development, with the **Travel Career Pattern** identifying **novelty, escape/relaxation, and relationships** as universal core motives. **Cohen** classifies five **modes of experience**—recreational, diversionary, experiential, experimental, and existential—along a continuum of distance from the familiar centre. **Plog** segments travellers along a **psychocentric–allocentric** continuum, with implications for destination evolution, though the model has limited predictive validity.

**Cultural tourism.** Cultural tourism—travel motivated by **learning, discovery, and consumption of cultural attractions**—can broaden horizons, enrich travellers, and support cultural preservation. **Anthropography** provides the descriptive geography of human communities; **life-seeing tourism** moves beyond sightseeing to **purposeful immersion in local life**. The **romance, nostalgia, and novelty** of pleasure travel reflect a search for **authenticity** in response to industrial urbanisation. Cultural tourism development requires **community participation** and **fair benefit-sharing** to avoid commodification.

**Sociology.** Tourism affects **individuals** (rejuvenation, identity formation, culture shock, perspective transformation), **families** (cohesion, changing structures), and **societies** (modernisation, intercultural dialogue, the demonstration effect, and commodification). **Life characteristics**—age, income, education, gender, and family life-cycle stage—shape travel propensity. **Group travel** creates temporary social systems. **Social tourism** treats travel as a **right**, with European voucher systems and informal Bangladeshi initiatives as examples. The **host–guest relationship** is analysed through **Doxey’s Irridex**, which traces attitudes from **euphoria** to **antagonism** (and **resignation**), though it oversimplifies community heterogeneity.

**Bangladesh.** The **Chittagong Hill Tracts** and **Sreemangal** illustrate both the **opportunities** of life-seeing and cultural tourism (self-representation, cultural entrepreneurship, income) and its **risks** (commodification, othering, demonstration effects, and inequity). The governing principle is **community-led, consent-based, and benefit-sharing** cultural tourism.

## 3.8 Key Terms Glossary

1. **Push factors:** Internal socio-psychological desires (escape, relaxation, prestige, ego-enhancement, anomie) that create the disposition to travel.
2. **Pull factors:** External destination attributes (beaches, heritage, climate, facilities) that determine destination choice.
3. **Seeking and escaping:** Iso-Ahola’s two-dimensional model of tourism motivation: escaping routine environments and seeking intrinsic rewards in new environments.
4. **Travel Career Ladder (TCL):** Pearce’s adaptation of Maslow’s hierarchy to tourism, comprising relaxation, safety, relationships, self-esteem/development, and self-actualisation needs.
5. **Travel Career Pattern (TCP):** A refinement of the TCL identifying core motives (novelty, escape/relaxation, relationships) and middle-layer motives that vary with travel experience.
6. **Recreational mode:** Cohen’s mode in which the tourist seeks entertainment and well-being; travel is an extension of the familiar.
7. **Existential mode:** Cohen’s mode in which the tourist seeks a better world elsewhere; the destination becomes a spiritual centre.
8. **Psychocentric:** Plog’s term for cautious, routine-oriented travellers who prefer familiar, low-risk destinations.
9. **Allocentric:** Plog’s term for adventurous, novelty-seeking travellers who prefer unfamiliar, off-the-beaten-path destinations.
10. **Anthropography:** The geography of humankind; the descriptive study of human communities, cultural practices, and their geographical distribution.
11. **Life-seeing tourism:** Tourism that seeks purposeful immersion in the daily life, work, and community activities of local people, rather than superficial sightseeing.
12. **Culture shock:** The disorientation and distress experienced when entering an unfamiliar cultural environment; Oberg’s stages are honeymoon, disintegration, reintegration, and autonomy.
13. **Demonstration effect:** The process by which local residents imitate the consumption patterns and behaviours of affluent tourists, leading to changed aspirations and values.
14. **Commodification:** The packaging and sale of cultural practices, rituals, and artefacts as tourism products, often stripping them of original meaning.
15. **Irridex (Irritation Index):** Doxey’s model of host attitude change across the stages of euphoria, apathy, annoyance/irritation, and antagonism (with a later fifth stage of resignation).
16. **Social tourism:** The organised provision of travel opportunities to people who would otherwise be excluded by income, disability, age, or social circumstance.
