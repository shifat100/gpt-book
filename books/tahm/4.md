## 4.1 Tourism Components and Supply

### 4.1.1 The Supply Side of Tourism: The Four Primary Supply Components

Tourism supply is not a single product but a **composite of four interdependent components**. A destination is viable only when all four are present and aligned; weakness in any one component constrains the performance of the others.

**Component 1: Natural Resources and Environment.** The natural environment is the **foundational supply component**: topography, climate, water bodies (oceans, rivers, lakes), beaches, wildlife, and aesthetic landscapes. These resources are largely **non-renewable** in tourism terms—a degraded coral reef or an eroded beach cannot be quickly recreated—and they are often **public goods** that no single firm has an incentive to protect. The natural environment supplies the **primary pull factors** (Chapter 3) that motivate visitation, and it is the component most vulnerable to the **environmental paradox** examined later in this chapter.

**Component 2: The Built Environment.** The built environment comprises two distinct layers. **Infrastructure** consists of the **basic subterranean and above-ground utilities** that serve residents and visitors alike: highways, water supply, sewage systems, electricity, telecommunications, and drainage. Infrastructure is typically **publicly provided** or regulated, and its capacity sets the **absolute ceiling** on tourism development. **Superstructure** consists of the **facilities constructed specifically for visitors**: hotels, passenger terminals, restaurants, souvenir shops, convention centres, and attractions. Superstructure is typically **privately financed**, and its viability depends on the adequacy of infrastructure.

**Table 4.1: Infrastructure versus Superstructure**

| Dimension | Infrastructure | Superstructure |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Basic utilities and systems serving the whole community | Facilities constructed specifically for visitors |
| **Examples** | Highways, water supply, sewage, electricity, telecommunications, airports (airside) | Hotels, restaurants, passenger terminals, souvenir shops, convention centres, theme parks |
| **Primary provider** | Public sector (or regulated utilities) | Private sector (with public incentives) |
| **Primary users** | Residents and visitors | Visitors (primarily) |
| **Capacity role** | Sets the absolute ceiling on development | Determines the quality and volume of visitor experience |
| **Financing logic** | Tax-funded or user-charged; long payback periods | Commercial investment; shorter payback periods |

**Component 3: Operating Sectors.** The operating sectors are the **firms and agencies that deliver services to visitors**: transportation providers (airlines, rail, motorcoach, cruise, car rental), food and beverage facilities, tour guiding and interpretation services, and receptive attractions. These sectors convert the **potential** of natural and built resources into an **actual experience**. Their performance depends on **labour quality, service standards, and supply-chain linkages**—the same linkages that determine the size of the tourism multiplier (Section 4.3).

**Component 4: Spirit of Hospitality and Cultural Resources.** The fourth component is **intangible but decisive**: the **friendliness, warmth, and service orientation of the host community**, together with **local traditions, heritage, performing arts, and ways of life**. A destination with world-class infrastructure and spectacular scenery can fail if visitors feel unwelcome; a destination with modest facilities can succeed if hospitality is genuine and cultural resources are rich. This component is **difficult to build quickly**—it is the product of history, social norms, and community pride—and it is easily damaged by **overtourism, inequitable benefit distribution, and the commodification of culture** (Chapter 3).

### 4.1.2 Matching Supply with Demand: The Core Challenge

Tourism supply is **rigid in the short run**: a hotel has a fixed number of rooms, an aircraft has a fixed number of seats, and a beach has a fixed physical capacity. Demand, by contrast, is **fluctuating, seasonal, and shock-sensitive**. The mismatch between rigid supply and volatile demand produces three management problems: **seasonality** (idle capacity in low season, congestion in high season), **capacity utilisation** (the proportion of available capacity actually sold), and **peak-load pricing** (the use of price to ration scarce capacity at peak times and stimulate demand in troughs).

**Seasonality mitigation strategies** include: **product diversification** (developing business tourism, cultural events, and wellness offerings that are less seasonally concentrated); **market diversification** (targeting source markets with different holiday calendars); **pricing incentives** (discounts and packages in shoulder and low seasons); and **domestic market development** (domestic demand can fill troughs that international demand leaves empty). **Overbooking control** is a **revenue-management technique**: accepting more reservations than available capacity to compensate for expected no-shows and cancellations, calibrated to the **cost of walking a guest** (relocating them to another property) versus the **revenue lost from empty rooms**. Overbooking is a rational response to **demand uncertainty**, but it must be managed within **consumer-protection and reputational constraints**.

## 4.2 Measuring and Forecasting Tourism Demand

### 4.2.1 Why Demand Measurement is Important

Demand measurement is the **evidentiary foundation** of tourism policy and investment. It guides **public infrastructure capital investment** (an airport expansion requires forecasts of future arrivals); **private hotel financing** (lenders require occupancy and ADR projections); **route planning** by airlines and transport operators; and **tax forecasting** by finance ministries. Without reliable demand data, investment decisions are made on **anecdote and optimism**, which is precisely how **over-capacity, stranded assets, and fiscal stress** arise.

### 4.2.2 Measuring Demand to a Destination

Three measures are fundamental.

**Visitor Arrivals (volume).** The **count of visitors** arriving at a destination in a period. Arrivals measure **reach** but not **depth**: a destination with 1 million arrivals of one-night visitors has a very different tourism economy from one with 1 million arrivals of seven-night visitors.

**Visitor-Days or Visitor-Nights (volume × duration).** The **total number of days or nights spent** by visitors in the destination. The formula is:

\[
D = \sum_{i} (v_i \times l_i)
\]

where \(v_i\) is the number of visitors in segment \(i\) and \(l_i\) is the average length of stay (in days or nights) for that segment. Visitor-days capture **both volume and duration**, and they are the appropriate denominator for **per-day expenditure, carrying capacity, and infrastructure load** calculations. A destination with 500,000 arrivals and an average stay of 2 nights generates **1,000,000 visitor-nights**; a destination with 200,000 arrivals and an average stay of 7 nights generates **1,400,000 visitor-nights**—more total tourism activity despite fewer arrivals.

**Total Visitor Expenditures.** The **total amount spent** by visitors in the destination, disaggregated by category (accommodation, food and beverage, transport, attractions, shopping). Expenditure is the **lifeblood of the economic impact** (Section 4.3), and its **composition** matters: expenditure on locally produced goods and services circulates within the economy, while expenditure on imported goods leaks out.

### 4.2.3 Demand Projection and Forecasting Methodologies

Forecasting methods divide into **quantitative** and **qualitative** approaches.

**Quantitative methods** use **historical data and statistical models**. **Trend analysis (extrapolation)** fits a trend line to historical arrivals or expenditure and projects it forward; it is simple and transparent but **assumes that the future will resemble the past**, which is unsafe in shock-prone tourism markets. **Simple linear regression** models demand as a function of a single independent variable—typically **income in the generating market** or **relative price**—while **multiple linear regression** incorporates several predictors (income, price, exchange rate, marketing expenditure, and dummy variables for shocks). Regression models provide **elasticity estimates** that are valuable for policy, but they require **long, reliable time series** and are vulnerable to **structural breaks** (pandemics, security crises, regulatory changes).

**Qualitative methods** rely on **judgement and expert opinion**. **Executive judgment** aggregates the views of senior managers; it is fast and cheap but susceptible to **optimism bias and groupthink**. **Sales force composite** aggregates the forecasts of front-line sales staff, who are close to demand but may lack a **strategic view**. The **Delphi Technique** is the most rigorous qualitative method: a **panel of independent experts** is surveyed **anonymously** across **iterative rounds**, with controlled feedback between rounds, until **consensus** emerges. Anonymity reduces the influence of **dominant personalities and status hierarchies**; iteration allows experts to **revise their views** in light of others’ reasoning without the pressure of face-to-face confrontation. Delphi is particularly useful where **historical data are sparse or unreliable**—precisely the situation in many emerging destinations.

## 4.3 Tourism’s Economic Impact

### 4.3.1 Tourist Expenditures and Economic Optimisation

Incoming tourist money **circulates** through the host economy in successive rounds. A hotel receives payment from a guest; it pays wages, purchases food and laundry services, and remits taxes; employees spend their wages in local shops; suppliers pay their own employees and buy inputs. Each round generates **income and employment**, but each round also **leaks**—some money is spent on imports, repatriated as profit, or saved rather than spent locally.

**Economic optimisation** in tourism requires balancing three objectives: **tourist satisfaction** (the experience must be good enough to sustain demand), **business profits** (firms must earn a return sufficient to reinvest and maintain quality), and **government tax and employment targets** (the public interest in revenue and jobs). These objectives can conflict: maximising short-run profit may degrade the resource base; maximising visitor numbers may reduce satisfaction and accelerate wear; maximising tax revenue may deter investment. Optimisation is therefore **multi-objective and political**, not a single mathematical maximum.

### 4.3.2 The Tourism Economic Multiplier Concept

The **tourism multiplier** is the number by which an initial injection of tourist expenditure is multiplied to yield the **total** (direct plus indirect plus induced) effect on output, income, or employment.

**Direct impact** is the **first-round expenditure** in tourism-characteristic businesses—hotels, restaurants, transport providers, attractions.

**Indirect impact** arises when direct tourism businesses purchase **inputs from other firms** in the domestic supply chain: a hotel buys vegetables from a farmer, laundry services from a supplier, and furniture from a manufacturer. These inter-industry purchases generate further output and employment.

**Induced impact** arises when the **households** that earn income from direct and indirect tourism activity **spend that income** on local consumer goods and services—housing, food, education, and healthcare.

**Table 4.2: Direct, Indirect, and Induced Economic Impacts**

| Impact type | Definition | Example | Measurement note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | First-round tourist expenditure in tourism-characteristic businesses | A tourist pays $200 for a hotel room | Captured by the Tourism Satellite Account (TSA) as direct tourism value added |
| **Indirect** | Supply-chain purchases by direct tourism businesses | The hotel buys $50 of vegetables from a local farmer | Estimated through input–output tables and tourism ratios |
| **Induced** | Household spending of income earned in direct and indirect activity | Hotel staff and farmers spend wages in local shops | Estimated through household expenditure propensities |

### 4.3.3 Types of Multipliers

**Output multipliers** measure the total value of production generated per unit of tourist expenditure. **Transaction multipliers** (also called **sales multipliers**) measure the total business turnover generated. **Income multipliers** measure the total household income generated per unit of tourist expenditure. **Employment multipliers** measure the total number of jobs (full-time equivalents) generated per unit of expenditure or per unit of output. Income and employment multipliers are typically **smaller** than output multipliers because wages and jobs are only a fraction of total turnover.

### 4.3.4 Mathematical Formulations: The Simple Keynesian Tourism Multiplier

The **simple Keynesian tourism multiplier** captures the **successive rounds of spending** as income circulates and leaks. In its basic form:

\[
k = \frac{1}{1 - c + m}
\]

where \(c\) is the **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)** locally and \(m\) is the **marginal propensity to import (MPM)** . Equivalently, where \(L\) is the **proportion of expenditure leaking** in the first round and \(c\) is the local propensity to consume:

\[
k = \frac{1 - L}{1 - c(1 - t) + m}
\]

where \(t\) is the **tax rate** on income. The logic is that each round of spending is **dampened by leakages**—imports, savings, and taxes—so the multiplier is larger when the local economy has **strong intersectoral linkages** (low \(m\)) and residents **spend a high proportion of income locally** (high \(c\)). A higher propensity to import **lowers** the multiplier, and hence the benefit to the economy.

The **total income generated** in the destination from an initial tourist expenditure \(E\) is:

\[
\Delta Y = E \times k
\]

The multiplier process is **not infinite**: leakages bring the chain to a halt, and the total effect is the **sum of a geometric progression** of spending rounds.

### 4.3.5 The Problem of Economic Leakages and Strategies for Local Retention

**Leakages** reduce the multiplier and the net developmental benefit of tourism. **Import leakage** occurs when tourism businesses purchase goods and services from abroad—food, beverages, fuel, construction materials, furniture, and sometimes labour—because local supply is inadequate or uncompetitive. **Corporate profit repatriation** occurs when foreign-owned hotels, airlines, and tour operators remit profits and management fees to headquarters. **Foreign debt servicing** absorbs tourism earnings where infrastructure has been financed by external borrowing.

Strategies to **optimise local economic retention** include: **supply-chain development** (assisting local farmers, food processors, and manufacturers to meet hotel procurement standards); **local content requirements** (mandating a minimum share of domestic sourcing for large tourism businesses); **local ownership and joint-venture incentives** (structuring investment approvals to favour domestic equity participation and profit retention); **skills development and localisation mandates** (training local managers to replace expatriate staff); and **community benefit-sharing agreements** (channelling a proportion of tourism revenue into local infrastructure and enterprises).

### 4.3.6 Comparing International versus Domestic Tourism Economic Impacts

**International tourism** generates **foreign exchange**, which is valuable for financing imports and servicing external debt. However, it is prone to **higher leakage** because foreign-owned firms repatriate profits and because international tourists often demand imported goods and services. It is also **more volatile**, subject to exchange-rate movements, security perceptions, and source-market recessions.

**Domestic tourism** generates expenditure in **local currency**, is **less seasonal** in aggregate (different regions and social groups holiday at different times), and typically has **lower leakage** because domestic tourists consume more locally produced goods and services. However, it does not generate foreign exchange, and its growth is constrained by **domestic income levels and holiday entitlements**. The optimal strategy for most destinations is a **balanced portfolio**: international tourism for foreign exchange and global visibility, domestic tourism for **stability, seasonality smoothing, and inclusive access** (Chapter 3).

## 4.4 Destination Tourism Policy

### 4.4.1 Definition and Nature of Tourism Policy

**Tourism policy** is a set of **regulations, rules, guidelines, directives, and development or promotion objectives** that provide a **framework within which collective and individual decisions affecting tourism are taken**. It is the **authoritative allocation of values** for the tourism sector: it determines **who gets what, when, and how**—which destinations are developed, which markets are targeted, which standards are enforced, and which stakeholders are consulted.

### 4.4.2 Focus of Tourism Policy: Competitiveness and Sustainability

Contemporary tourism policy pursues two overarching goals: **destination competitiveness** and **long-term sustainability**. The **Crouch and Ritchie (Ritchie and Crouch, 2003) model of destination competitiveness/sustainability** identifies **seven components** that determine a destination’s ability to compete and sustain its performance: **core resources and attractors** (the primary motivations for visitation), **supporting factors and resources** (infrastructure, accessibility, hospitality), **destination management** (planning, marketing, organisation), **destination policy, planning and development** (the policy framework itself), **qualifying and amplifying determinants** (situational conditions such as location, safety, and price competitiveness), and the **competitive (micro) and global (macro) environments**. The model’s key insight is that competitiveness is **multidimensional**: a destination cannot compensate for weak core resources with strong marketing, nor for poor management with natural endowments. Sustainability is not an **add-on** to competitiveness; it is a **condition** of long-term competitiveness, because a destination that degrades its resource base destroys the very attributes that make it attractive.

### 4.4.3 Tourism Policy Structure, Content, and Process

Tourism policy has four core phases:

1. **Definition** — identifying the problem, the values at stake, and the scope of policy action.
2. **Formulation** — setting objectives, selecting instruments (regulation, incentives, investment, marketing), and allocating responsibilities.
3. **Implementation** — translating policy into **operational plans, budgets, regulations, and institutional arrangements**.
4. **Evaluation** — monitoring outcomes against objectives and feeding lessons back into policy revision.

The **content** of tourism policy typically includes: **visa and entry policy**; **aviation and transport policy**; **investment incentives and taxation**; **quality and safety standards**; **environmental regulation**; **destination marketing and branding**; **human resource development**; and **community participation mechanisms**.

### 4.4.4 Translating Broad Policy Directives into Ground Reality

The gap between **policy on paper** and **implementation on the ground** is the central challenge of tourism governance. Broad directives—“develop sustainable tourism,” “enhance community participation,” “protect the environment”—are **aspirational** until they are translated into **specific, funded, enforceable actions**: zoning rules with maps and penalties; budget lines for waste management and training; procurement rules that favour local suppliers; and monitoring systems that track outcomes. **Implementation failure** is common where policy lacks **institutional capacity, budget, political backing, or stakeholder buy-in**. The Bangladesh case (Section 4.7) illustrates both the ambition of policy and the difficulty of implementation.

## 4.5 Tourism Planning, Development, and Social Considerations

### 4.5.1 The Relationship between Tourism Policy and Tourism Planning

**Policy** is the **“big picture” vision and philosophy**: the values, objectives, and framework within which decisions are made. **Planning** is the **detailed operational roadmap and blueprint**: the specific land-use designations, infrastructure phasing, product development schedules, and budget allocations that translate policy into action.

**Table 4.3: Tourism Policy versus Tourism Planning**

| Dimension | Tourism Policy | Tourism Planning |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | Vision, philosophy, framework | Operational roadmap, blueprint |
| **Time horizon** | Long-term, strategic | Medium-to-short-term, tactical |
| **Output** | Objectives, regulations, directives | Land-use plans, infrastructure schedules, budgets |
| **Key actors** | Government, legislature, peak bodies | Planners, agencies, developers, communities |
| **Success criterion** | Coherence, legitimacy, political support | Feasibility, implementation, measurable outcomes |

### 4.5.2 Why Tourism Planning is Necessary

Unplanned tourism development produces predictable pathologies: **unplanned sprawl** along coastlines and scenic corridors; **overtourism** in honeypot sites; **congestion** of roads, beaches, and attractions; **inflation** of land and housing prices; **architectural pollution** from insensitive development that destroys the aesthetic resource; and **community resentment** when residents bear costs without sharing benefits (Chapter 3). Planning is the **instrument** through which these pathologies are anticipated and mitigated—not through prohibition alone, but through **zoning, design standards, carrying-capacity limits, infrastructure phasing, and community consultation**.

### 4.5.3 The Phased Tourism Planning Process

A robust tourism planning process proceeds through eight phases:

1. **Define the system and goals** — establish the planning boundary (region, destination, site), the stakeholders, and the objectives (economic, social, environmental).
2. **Gather data** — visitor statistics, resource inventories, infrastructure capacity, community profiles, market intelligence.
3. **Analyse data** — assess demand trends, resource constraints, carrying capacity, and competitive position.
4. **Formulate a preliminary plan** — draft land-use designations, development scenarios, and implementation schedules.
5. **Stakeholder consultation** — present the preliminary plan to communities, businesses, and agencies; incorporate feedback and resolve conflicts.
6. **Finalise the master plan** — produce the authoritative document with zoning, standards, budgets, and responsibilities.
7. **Implementation** — enact regulations, allocate budgets, and sequence investment.
8. **Monitoring and evaluation** — track outcomes, detect unintended consequences, and revise the plan adaptively.

The process is **iterative, not linear**: monitoring feeds back into data gathering and analysis, and stakeholder consultation may recur at multiple stages.

### 4.5.4 Goals of Tourism Development

Tourism development pursues three **interlinked goals**: **enhancing resident quality of life** (income, employment, infrastructure, cultural pride); **preserving natural and cultural integrity** (conservation of the resource base on which tourism depends); and **maximising economic yields** (foreign exchange, tax revenue, and multiplier effects). These goals can **conflict**: maximising yields may degrade integrity; preserving integrity may constrain yields. Sustainable planning is the **management of these trade-offs**, not the maximisation of any single goal.

### 4.5.5 Political Aspects of Tourism Development

Tourism development is **inherently political**. Government stakeholders—tourism ministries, finance ministries, environment agencies, local governments—have **different interests and constituencies**. **Power dynamics** determine whose voice is heard: large investors typically have more influence than community groups; national agencies may override local preferences; and zoning laws may be **captured** by incumbent interests. **Public–private conflicts** arise over land use, infrastructure costs, and profit-sharing. **Local community empowerment** is the counterweight: participatory planning, community benefit agreements, and legal recognition of community rights can ensure that development serves **residents as well as visitors**.

### 4.5.6 Development of Tourist Potential

Evaluating tourist potential requires three assessments: **resource endowments** (what natural and cultural assets exist, and how distinctive are they?); **accessibility** (can visitors reach the destination, at what cost and convenience?); and **market appeal** (is there a segment of demand that values what the destination offers, and is the destination competitive against alternatives?). A destination with rich resources but poor accessibility has **latent potential**; one with good accessibility but undifferentiated resources has **commodity potential**; one with both has **competitive potential**—provided that planning ensures that development does not destroy the resources on which it depends.

## 4.6 Tourism and the Natural Environment

### 4.6.1 The Environmental Paradox

Tourism depends on **clean beaches, intact reefs, healthy wildlife, and scenic landscapes**—yet tourism itself **threatens** these very assets through **pollution, waste generation, biodiversity loss, beach erosion, and carbon emissions**. This is the **environmental paradox**: the industry consumes the resource that sustains it. The paradox is not inevitable: well-managed tourism can **fund conservation, create incentives for protection, and build environmental awareness**. But unmanaged tourism follows a predictable trajectory of **degradation**—the “tragedy of the commons” in which individual firms externalise environmental costs onto the destination as a whole.

### 4.6.2 The WTTC Position on Environment and Sustainable Development

The **World Travel & Tourism Council (WTTC)** has positioned sustainability as **central to the sector’s long-term viability**. WTTC is a **signatory of the Glasgow Declaration on Climate Action in Tourism**, committing to **cut global tourism emissions by at least half over the next decade and reach net zero as soon as possible before 2050**. WTTC’s **Net Zero Roadmap** underscores the urgent need for climate action and highlights a **27% increase in climate target-setting** across the sector over three years. WTTC research indicates that the sector can **grow while reducing its environmental impact**, but that achieving this requires **sustainable fuels, renewable energy, low-carbon infrastructure, and effective public–private partnerships**. WTTC has also partnered with UNEP and UN Tourism on **nature-positive tourism**, aiming to **halt and reverse biodiversity loss by 2030**.

### 4.6.3 Core Concepts of Sustainable Tourism Development

The **Brundtland Commission (1987)** defined sustainable development as **“development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.”** Applied to tourism, **sustainable tourism development** requires balancing **economic prosperity, social equity, and environmental conservation**—the “triple bottom line.” It is not a **product type** (a niche called “sustainable tourism”) but a **principle** that should govern **all** tourism development: mass tourism can be more or less sustainable depending on how it is managed, and ecotourism can be unsustainable if it is poorly planned.

### 4.6.4 Ecotourism Principles and Practices

**Definition and core tenets.** **Ecotourism** is **responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of local people, and involves interpretation and education**. Its core tenets are: **non-consumptive use** (wildlife viewing rather than hunting); **conservation support** (revenue and political support for protected areas); **education and interpretation** (visitors learn about ecology and culture); and **community benefit** (local people share in revenue and decision-making).

**Carrying capacity concepts.** Carrying capacity is the **maximum number of visitors a site can absorb without unacceptable degradation** of its physical, ecological, social, or managerial systems. Three nested concepts are used:

- **Physical Carrying Capacity (PCC)** — the **maximum number of visitors that can physically fit** into a site at a given time, given its area and the space required per visitor. The general formula is \(\text{PCC} = A \times U / a \times R_f\), where \(A\) is area, \(U\) is the area per user, \(a\) is the minimum space per visitor, and \(R_f\) is the **rotation factor** (visits per day).
- **Real Carrying Capacity (RCC)** — the **permissible number of visits after applying corrective (reductive) factors** derived from the site’s **biophysical, ecological, social, and managerial characteristics**. \(\text{RCC} = \text{PCC} \times (100 - C_{f1})/100 \times (100 - C_{f2})/100 \times \ldots\), where each \(C_f\) is a **corrective factor expressed as a percentage**.
- **Effective Carrying Capacity (ECC)** — the **maximum number of visitors a site can sustain given the management capacity (MC) actually available**. \(\text{ECC} = \text{RCC} \times \text{MC}\), where MC is the **proportion of management conditions actually met** (staffing, infrastructure, enforcement). ECC is typically **much lower** than PCC because management capacity is rarely 100%.

**Community-based ecotourism (CBET) and ecolodge certification.** **CBET** models place **community ownership, control, and benefit-sharing** at the centre of ecotourism: local people are not merely employees or performers but **decision-makers and beneficiaries**. **Ecolodge certification** provides **third-party verification** that a lodge meets standards for **energy, water, waste, materials, and community engagement**; certification reduces **greenwashing** and allows discerning travellers to reward genuine sustainability.

### 4.6.5 Current Tourism Industry Practices

**Green hotel certifications** such as **LEED (Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design)** provide **verifiable standards** for building performance—energy efficiency, water conservation, materials, and indoor environmental quality. **Carbon offsetting in aviation** allows travellers and airlines to **purchase credits** that fund emission-reduction projects elsewhere; offsets are **controversial** because they can **substitute for direct emission reductions**, but they remain a **transitional tool**. **Zero-waste initiatives** in tourism aim to **eliminate single-use plastics, compost organic waste, and recycle**; they require **supplier engagement, staff training, and visitor education**. **Bans on single-use plastics in coastal resorts** are increasingly common—Saint Martin’s Island in Bangladesh is a leading example (Section 4.7).

**Table 4.4: Mass Tourism versus Ecotourism / Sustainable Tourism**

| Dimension | Mass Tourism | Ecotourism / Sustainable Tourism |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary motivation** | Leisure, entertainment, escape | Nature, learning, conservation, community |
| **Scale** | Large-volume, standardised | Small-scale, differentiated |
| **Resource use** | High energy, water, and waste intensity | Low-impact, conservation-oriented |
| **Community role** | Often marginal; employment at low wages | Central; ownership, benefit-sharing, decision-making |
| **Environmental orientation** | Externalises environmental costs | Internalises environmental costs |
| **Management focus** | Volume and revenue maximisation | Carrying capacity, equity, and long-term viability |
| **Typical destination** | Resort enclaves, theme parks, cruise ports | Protected areas, community lodges, nature trails |

## 4.7 Bangladesh Field Vignette: Sustainable Tourism Planning and Carrying Capacity in Cox’s Bazar and Saint Martin’s Island

**Cox’s Bazar: The Overtourism Challenge.** Cox’s Bazar is home to the **world’s longest unbroken natural sea beach**, and it is Bangladesh’s most popular tourist destination. The scale of visitation is extraordinary: **2–2.5 million Bangladeshis** travel to the beach during peak season, according to the Cox’s Bazar Chamber of Commerce and Industry, and some estimates put annual visitation at **near 3.7 million**. In November–December alone, approximately **2.1 million tourists** visited, with another **600,000–700,000** in January–February; during Eid holidays, arrivals can exceed **1 million**. This is **short-lived, high-tide tourism**: demand is concentrated into narrow windows, imposing an **unreasonable burden on local infrastructure, waste collection services, and coastal ecologies**. Overcrowded roads, failed waste management, and stressed water resources **aggravate the ordeal of infrastructure**, and the region is confronting **overtourism that has pushed its finely poised ecosystems and infrastructures over the edge**.

The planning response has been the **Destination Development Plan for Cox’s Bazar (2025–2027)** , published by the **International Labour Organization (ILO)** in 2025. The plan is a **three-year roadmap** for transforming Cox’s Bazar into a **more sustainable and inclusive destination**, aligned with **social and environmental priorities** and structured around **four pillars**: strengthened governance and collaboration, environmental sustainability, gender responsiveness, and youth development. It covers **eight key destinations** in the district and recommends **eco-resorts, waste management, zoning enforcement, and climate-resilient facilities** through a **Cox’s Bazar and Southeast Coastal Tourism Upgrading Program** (FY 2028–2030). Implementation is entrusted to **District and Upazila Tourism Development Committees**, with leadership from the **District Administration** and collaboration across government departments, the private sector, and civil society. The plan’s governance architecture is **multi-tiered**, linking national policy to local implementation through **District Tourism Development Committees (DTDCs) and Upazila Tourism Development Committees (UTDCs)** .

Carrying capacity assessment has been applied at the site level. A study of **Patuartek Sea Beach** in Cox’s Bazar District—famous for coral stones, sandy beach, and red crabs—used a **modified Cifuentes Method** incorporating **physical, environmental, socio-cultural, economic, and managerial parameters** to estimate the optimal number of visitors. The study found an **effective tourism carrying capacity of 201 visitors at a given time**—a striking contrast to the millions of annual arrivals at the district level. The finding illustrates the **scale of the mismatch** between demand and ecological capacity in Cox’s Bazar’s sensitive coastal sites.

**Saint Martin’s Island: Coral, Plastic, and Restriction.** Saint Martin’s Island is Bangladesh’s **only coral island**, a **12-square-kilometre** site in the Bay of Bengal with **46 species of corals**, declared an **Environmentally Critical Area in 1999**. It shelters about **10,000 people** who rely on fishing, seaweed farming, and tourism. The environmental trajectory has been stark: between 2005 and 2019, **concrete structures accommodating tourists increased by 175%**, while **coral reef volume decreased by 38%**. **Aggressive tourism has destroyed the island**, according to researchers who have studied its biodiversity since 1981.

The government response has been a **regime of controlled tourism**. In **October 2025**, the environment ministry issued a **12-point directive** imposing **limitations on tourist arrivals, daytime-only travel, and bans on single-use plastics and noisy activities**. Tourism was **banned during February–October** to protect biodiversity, with a **maximum of 2,000 tourists per day** during the November season and **overnight stays permitted only in December and January**. Travellers must purchase **online tickets with a travel pass and QR code** through a portal authorised by the **Bangladesh Tourism Board**; the **Bangladesh Inland Water Transport Authority** cannot permit any vessel without prior approval from the environment ministry; and **entry into the Keya forest and harming wildlife are strictly banned**. In a sustainability measure, each tourist is **handed a free aluminium water bottle** by the Department of Environment to discourage single-use plastics, and **polythene items and plastic bottles (500–1000 ml) are prohibited**.

The restrictions have been **contested**. Ship owners argue that the **day-trip format is unviable**: a round trip from Cox’s Bazar takes **14–15 hours**, leaving only **one hour on the island**, and at least **350 passengers are needed to run a ship** at a cost of around **10 million taka per journey**. On reopening day in November 2025, **only four passengers** arrived at the jetty, and none boarded. Resort owners argue that **overnight stays are crucial** to enjoying the island and that the rules **harm tourism rather than help it**. Conservationists counter that **controlled tourism must continue**, because **aggressive tourism destroyed the island** and the restrictions are intended to **discourage visitation during the breeding season of wildlife**. The dispute crystallises the **core tension of sustainable tourism planning**: the ecological carrying capacity of a fragile island versus the **livelihoods and economic expectations** of the tourism industry and local community.

**Policy framework.** Bangladesh’s **National Tourism Policy 2010** aims to establish tourism as a **developing and sustainable sector** through **employment generation, socio-economic development involving local people, ecological balance, and biodiversity protection**. It emphasises **green, low-impact, nature-related and soft tourism**, and calls for **tourism development plans within national development strategies within a sustainability perspective**. However, implementation has been criticised: the policy’s strategies mostly centre on **bureaucratic decision-making structures**, and there is **no emphasis on sustainability in practice**. The Cox’s Bazar and Saint Martin’s cases show both the **ambition** of Bangladesh’s sustainable tourism policy and the **implementation gap** between directive and ground reality.

## 4.8 Worked Numerical Problems

### Worked Problem 1: Visitor-Days Calculation

A destination receives **400,000 international visitors** with an average length of stay of **5 nights**, and **600,000 domestic visitors** with an average length of stay of **2 nights**.

**Calculate total visitor-nights.**

\[
D = (400{,}000 \times 5) + (600{,}000 \times 2) = 2{,}000{,}000 + 1{,}200{,}000 = 3{,}200{,}000 \text{ visitor-nights}
\]

**Interpretation.** Although domestic arrivals exceed international arrivals (600,000 vs. 400,000), international visitors account for **62.5%** of total visitor-nights because of their longer stay. A destination planning accommodation, waste management, or water supply must plan for **3.2 million visitor-nights**, not 1 million arrivals.

### Worked Problem 2: Tourism Income Multiplier and Destination Income

An initial tourist expenditure of **$10,000,000** enters a destination economy. The marginal propensity to consume locally is **\(c = 0.70\)**, and the marginal propensity to import is **\(m = 0.15\)**.

**(a) Calculate the Keynesian tourism income multiplier.**

\[
k = \frac{1}{1 - c + m} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.70 + 0.15} = \frac{1}{0.45} \approx 2.22
\]

**(b) Calculate the total income generated in the destination.**

\[
\Delta Y = E \times k = 10{,}000{,}000 \times 2.22 \approx \$22{,}200{,}000
\]

**(c) Recalculate if the propensity to import rises to \(m = 0.30\).**

\[
k = \frac{1}{1 - 0.70 + 0.30} = \frac{1}{0.60} \approx 1.67
\]

\[
\Delta Y = 10{,}000{,}000 \times 1.67 \approx \$16{,}700{,}000
\]

**Interpretation.** A **doubling of the import propensity** (from 0.15 to 0.30) reduces the multiplier from **2.22 to 1.67** and total destination income from **$22.2 million to $16.7 million**—a loss of **$5.5 million** in economic impact. This is why **supply-chain development and local sourcing** are central to tourism policy: reducing \(m\) is the most direct lever for increasing the developmental dividend of tourist expenditure.

### Worked Problem 3: Physical, Real, and Effective Carrying Capacity

A coastal nature reserve has a **visitor-accessible area of 50,000 \(m^2\)**. The standard space requirement is **10 \(m^2\) per visitor**. The reserve is open for **8 hours per day**, and the average visit duration is **2 hours**, giving a rotation factor of \(8/2 = 4\).

**(a) Calculate Physical Carrying Capacity (PCC).**

\[
\text{PCC} = \frac{A}{a} \times R_f = \frac{50{,}000}{10} \times 4 = 5{,}000 \times 4 = 20{,}000 \text{ visits/day}
\]

**(b) Apply corrective factors: 30% for erosion susceptibility and 20% for wildlife disturbance.**

\[
\text{RCC} = 20{,}000 \times \frac{100 - 30}{100} \times \frac{100 - 20}{100} = 20{,}000 \times 0.70 \times 0.80 = 11{,}200 \text{ visits/day}
\]

**(c) If management capacity is 40%, calculate Effective Carrying Capacity (ECC).**

\[
\text{ECC} = \text{RCC} \times \text{MC} = 11{,}200 \times 0.40 = 4{,}480 \text{ visits/day}
\]

**Interpretation.** The **physical capacity** of 20,000 visits/day is **more than four times** the **effective capacity** of 4,480 visits/day. The gap represents the **management deficit**: without adequate staffing, infrastructure, and enforcement, the site cannot sustainably absorb even its real carrying capacity. Investment in **management capacity**—rangers, waste systems, signage, and monitoring—raises ECC without expanding the physical footprint.

| Concept | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Visitor-days** | \(D = \sum (v_i \times l_i)\) | Sum over segments; \(v\) = visitors, \(l\) = average length of stay |
| **Keynesian tourism multiplier (basic)** | \(k = \frac{1}{1 - c + m}\) | \(c\) = marginal propensity to consume locally; \(m\) = marginal propensity to import |
| **Keynesian multiplier (with leakage and tax)** | \(k = \frac{1 - L}{1 - c(1 - t) + m}\) | \(L\) = first-round leakage proportion; \(t\) = tax rate |
| **Total destination income** | \(\Delta Y = E \times k\) | \(E\) = initial tourist expenditure |

### 4.10.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions (with Model Answers)

**Question 1. Distinguish between infrastructure and superstructure, and explain why the distinction matters for tourism planning.**

**Model Answer.** **Infrastructure** comprises the **basic utilities and systems** that serve the whole community—highways, water supply, sewage, electricity, telecommunications, and drainage. **Superstructure** comprises the **facilities constructed specifically for visitors**—hotels, restaurants, passenger terminals, souvenir shops, and convention centres. The distinction matters because **infrastructure sets the absolute ceiling on tourism development**, while **superstructure determines the quality and volume of the visitor experience**. A destination can attract hotel investment only if water, power, and waste systems can support the additional load; building superstructure without adequate infrastructure produces **service failures, environmental degradation, and community resentment**. Planning must therefore **sequence infrastructure investment ahead of superstructure** and ensure that the **public costs of infrastructure** are matched by **private investment and tax revenue** from superstructure.

---

**Question 2. Explain the difference between visitor arrivals and visitor-days, and why visitor-days is the more appropriate measure for planning purposes.**

**Model Answer.** **Visitor arrivals** count the **number of visitors** entering a destination in a period; **visitor-days** (or visitor-nights) measure the **total number of days or nights spent** by visitors, calculated as \(\sum (v_i \times l_i)\). Visitor-days is more appropriate for planning because it captures **both volume and duration**. A destination with 1 million arrivals of one-night visitors has 1 million visitor-nights; one with 500,000 arrivals of four-night visitors has 2 million visitor-nights—**double the total tourism activity** despite half the arrivals. Infrastructure load, waste generation, water demand, and carrying capacity are all functions of **visitor-days**, not arrivals. Using arrivals alone would **understate** the pressure on infrastructure and the economic contribution of long-stay markets.

---

**Question 3. Define the tourism income multiplier and explain the difference between direct, indirect, and induced impacts.**

**Model Answer.** The **tourism income multiplier** is the number by which an initial injection of tourist expenditure is multiplied to yield the **total income** generated in the destination economy. **Direct impact** is the **first-round expenditure** in tourism-characteristic businesses—hotels, restaurants, and attractions. **Indirect impact** arises when those businesses purchase **inputs from other domestic firms**—food, laundry, furniture—generating further output and employment in the supply chain. **Induced impact** arises when the **households** earning income from direct and indirect activity **spend that income** on local consumer goods and services. The multiplier is larger when **leakages are low** and **local linkages are strong**, and smaller when imports and profit repatriation drain expenditure from the economy.

---

**Question 4. Distinguish between tourism policy and tourism planning, and explain why both are necessary.**

**Model Answer.** **Tourism policy** is the **vision, philosophy, and framework**—the regulations, rules, guidelines, and development objectives within which decisions are taken. **Tourism planning** is the **detailed operational roadmap**—the land-use designations, infrastructure schedules, and implementation budgets that translate policy into action. Both are necessary because **policy without planning remains aspirational** (a vision with no operational mechanism), while **planning without policy lacks coherence and legitimacy** (a blueprint with no guiding values or authority). Policy provides the **legitimacy and direction**; planning provides the **feasibility and sequencing**. Effective tourism development requires **alignment** between the two: the plan must implement the policy, and the policy must be revised in light of planning experience.

---

**Question 5. Explain the environmental paradox of tourism and evaluate whether sustainable tourism can resolve it.**

**Model Answer.** The **environmental paradox** is that tourism depends on **clean beaches, intact reefs, healthy wildlife, and scenic landscapes**, yet tourism itself **threatens these assets** through pollution, waste, biodiversity loss, erosion, and carbon emissions. The paradox is **not inevitable**: sustainable tourism—governed by the principles of **economic prosperity, social equity, and environmental conservation**—can **fund conservation, create incentives for protection, and build environmental awareness**. However, sustainability **resolves the paradox only under specific conditions**: **carrying capacity limits** must be enforced, **leakages must be reduced** so that local communities benefit from conservation, **management capacity** must be adequate, and **political will** must withstand short-term economic pressures. Where these conditions are absent, “sustainable tourism” becomes a **label** rather than a practice, and the paradox persists.

---

**Question 6. What is the Delphi Technique, and why is it useful in tourism forecasting?**

**Model Answer.** The **Delphi Technique** is a **qualitative forecasting method** that surveys a **panel of independent experts** across **iterative rounds**, with **anonymous** responses and **controlled feedback** between rounds, until **consensus** emerges. Anonymity reduces the influence of **dominant personalities and status hierarchies**; iteration allows experts to **revise their views** in light of others’ reasoning without face-to-face pressure. Delphi is useful in tourism forecasting where **historical data are sparse, unreliable, or structurally broken**—for example, in emerging destinations, post-crisis recovery, or new market segments. Its limitations are that it is **time-consuming, dependent on expert selection, and vulnerable to false consensus**; it is therefore best used **alongside quantitative methods**, not as a substitute.

**Essay Question 2. “The tourism multiplier is a powerful tool for advocacy but a weak guide to policy.” Discuss.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** State the paradox: multipliers produce **large numbers** that are politically attractive, but their **assumptions and limitations** make them unreliable for precise policy decisions.
- **Section 1: The advocacy power of multipliers.** A multiplier of 2.5 turns $10 million into $25 million; politicians and industry associations use such figures to justify **investment incentives and infrastructure spending**.
- **Section 2: The methodological weaknesses.** Keynesian multipliers assume **constant propensities**, ignore **capacity constraints**, and treat **leakages as fixed**; they are **static** and do not account for **price effects** or **crowding out**.
- **Section 3: The leakage problem.** High import propensities in small or undiversified economies mean that **gross receipts overstate net benefit**; multiplier estimates that ignore leakage **overstate** the developmental dividend.
- **Section 4: The TSA alternative.** The **Tourism Satellite Account** provides a **rigorous, SNA-consistent** measure of **direct** contribution; indirect and induced effects are estimated separately with **transparent assumptions**.
- **Section 5: Appropriate use.** Multipliers are useful for **order-of-magnitude comparison** and **awareness-raising**, not for **precise forecasting** or **cost–benefit analysis**. Policy should rely on **TSA data, input–output tables, and CGE models** for decisions with large fiscal consequences.
- **Conclusion.** The multiplier is a **communication tool**, not a **decision tool**. Its power to persuade must be matched by **humility about its precision**.

### 4.10.4 Applied Planning Problems (with Complete Solutions)

**Planning Problem 1: The Coastal Resort’s Carrying Capacity and Management Deficit**

A coastal resort beach has an **accessible area of 80,000 \(m^2\)**. The standard space requirement is **20 \(m^2\) per visitor**, and the beach is open for **10 hours per day** with an average visit of **2.5 hours** (rotation factor = 4). Corrective factors are **25% for erosion** and **15% for ecological sensitivity**. Management capacity is assessed at **35%**.

**(a) Calculate PCC, RCC, and ECC.**

\[
\text{PCC} = \frac{80{,}000}{20} \times 4 = 4{,}000 \times 4 = 16{,}000 \text{ visits/day}
\]

\[
\text{RCC} = 16{,}000 \times 0.75 \times 0.85 = 16{,}000 \times 0.6375 = 10{,}200 \text{ visits/day}
\]

\[
\text{ECC} = 10{,}200 \times 0.35 = 3{,}570 \text{ visits/day}
\]

**(b) The resort currently receives 12,000 visits per day in peak season. What is the management implication?**

Peak-season visitation **(12,000)** **exceeds RCC (10,200)** and is **more than three times ECC (3,570)** . The beach is being **used beyond its ecological capacity**, and the **management deficit** (MC = 35%) means that even the **real capacity is not being protected**. Management priorities are: **raise MC** through staffing, waste collection, signage, and enforcement; **reduce peak arrivals** through pricing, timed entry, or dispersal to alternative sites; and **monitor erosion and ecological indicators** to detect degradation before it becomes irreversible.

**Planning Problem 2: The Destination’s Multiplier and Leakage Reduction Strategy**

A destination receives **$50 million** in annual tourist expenditure. The local MPC is **0.65**, the MPM is **0.25**, and the tax rate on income is **0.15**.

**(a) Calculate the multiplier using \(k = 1/(1 - c + m)\) (ignore tax for this part).**

\[
k = \frac{1}{1 - 0.65 + 0.25} = \frac{1}{0.60} \approx 1.67
\]

**(b) Calculate total destination income.**

\[
\Delta Y = 50{,}000{,}000 \times 1.67 \approx \$83{,}500{,}000
\]

**(c) A supply-chain programme reduces the MPM from 0.25 to 0.15. Recalculate the multiplier and total income.**

\[
k = \frac{1}{1 - 0.65 + 0.15} = \frac{1}{0.50} = 2.00
\]

\[
\Delta Y = 50{,}000{,}000 \times 2.00 = \$100{,}000{,}000
\]

**(d) What is the incremental income gain, and how should the destination evaluate the cost of the supply-chain programme?**

The gain is **$100 million − $83.5 million = $16.5 million** in additional destination income. The programme should be evaluated as an **investment**: if its **cost is less than the present value of the incremental income stream**, it is economically justified. Non-economic benefits—**local employment, food security, reduced import dependence**—should also be weighed. ## 4.11 Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the supply, demand, policy, planning, and sustainability dimensions of tourism.

**Supply.** Tourism supply comprises **four interdependent components**: **natural resources and environment**, the **built environment** (infrastructure and superstructure), **operating sectors**, and the **spirit of hospitality and cultural resources**. Supply is **rigid in the short run**, while demand is **volatile and seasonal**; the resulting mismatch is managed through **pricing, diversification, and capacity planning**.

**Demand.** Demand is measured through **arrivals, visitor-days, and expenditure**. **Quantitative forecasting** (trend extrapolation, regression) uses historical data; **qualitative forecasting** (executive judgment, sales force composite, Delphi) relies on expert judgement, with Delphi offering the most rigorous consensus mechanism.

**Economic impact.** Tourist expenditure generates **direct, indirect, and induced** effects. The **Keynesian multiplier** captures the successive rounds of spending, and its size depends on **local consumption propensities and import leakages**. **Leakage reduction** through supply-chain development and local ownership is the principal lever for increasing the developmental dividend. **Domestic tourism** offers stability and lower leakage; **international tourism** offers foreign exchange but higher volatility.

**Policy and planning.** **Tourism policy** is the vision and framework; **planning** is the operational roadmap. Policy pursues **competitiveness and sustainability** through the **Crouch–Ritchie framework**. Planning proceeds through **eight phases**, from goal definition to monitoring, and must navigate **political dynamics and community empowerment**.

**Sustainability.** The **environmental paradox**—tourism depends on the environment it threatens—is addressed through **sustainable tourism principles** (economic, social, environmental balance), **ecotourism**, and **carrying capacity** (PCC, RCC, ECC). Industry practices include **green certifications, carbon offsetting, zero-waste initiatives, and plastic bans**.

**Bangladesh.** **Cox’s Bazar** faces **overtourism** (2–3.7 million annual visitors) and infrastructure stress, but the **ILO Destination Development Plan (2025–2027)** offers a multi-tiered governance response. **Saint Martin’s Island** illustrates the **carrying capacity crisis** (175% increase in structures, 38% coral decline) and the **contested politics** of controlled tourism (2,000 daily cap, plastic bans, seasonal closures). The **National Tourism Policy 2010** espouses sustainability, but **implementation gaps** persist. The lesson is that **planning without enforcement and community participation cannot resolve the environmental paradox**.

7. **Indirect impact:** Supply-chain purchases by direct tourism businesses from other domestic firms.
8. **Induced impact:** Household spending of income earned in direct and indirect tourism activity.
9. **Tourism income multiplier:** The factor by which initial tourist expenditure is multiplied to yield total destination income.
10. **Import leakage:** Tourist expenditure that leaves the economy through purchases of imported goods and services.
11. **Tourism policy:** The regulations, rules, guidelines, and objectives that frame collective and individual decisions affecting tourism.
12. **Tourism planning:** The operational roadmap—land-use designations, infrastructure schedules, budgets—that translates policy into action.
13. **Carrying capacity:** The maximum number of visitors a site can absorb without unacceptable degradation.
14. **Effective Carrying Capacity (ECC):** The permissible visitor number after applying corrective factors and management capacity constraints.
15. **Sustainable tourism development:** Development that balances economic prosperity, social equity, and environmental conservation without compromising future generations.
16. **Ecotourism:** Responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains local wellbeing, and involves interpretation and education.
