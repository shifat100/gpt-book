## 5.1 Tourism and Hospitality Marketing Fundamentals

### 5.1.1 Defining Tourism Marketing

**Tourism marketing** is the **managerial and societal process** by which destinations, hospitality firms, and transport providers **identify, anticipate, and satisfy visitor needs and wants**—profitably where appropriate, and sustainably in all cases—through the **creation, communication, and delivery of valued experiences**. The **managerial** dimension focuses on the firm: understanding customer needs, designing services, pricing them, distributing them, and communicating their value. The **societal** dimension focuses on the destination and the community: ensuring that marketing does not **manufacture demand** that exceeds carrying capacity, **commodify culture** without consent, or **externalise environmental costs** onto host communities (Chapters 3 and 4). A marketing campaign that fills a destination beyond its **effective carrying capacity** is a **managerial success and a societal failure**; sustainable tourism marketing must optimise both.

Kotler et al. remind us that hospitality and tourism marketing is **service-dominant**: the customer does not buy a tangible object but a **promise of experience**, delivered through **people, processes, and physical cues**. This is why the **7Ps** framework, rather than the traditional 4Ps, is the appropriate analytical structure.

### 5.1.2 The Extended Services Marketing Mix (The 7Ps)

**Table 5.1: The Traditional 4Ps versus the Extended Services 7Ps of Hospitality Marketing**

| **Traditional 4Ps** | **Extended 7Ps (Services)** | **Tourism/Hospitality Application** |
|---|---|---|
| **Product** | **Product** | Service bundles, destination experiences, branding, packaging (e.g., all-inclusive packages, heritage circuits) |
| **Price** | **Price** | Dynamic pricing, yield/revenue management, seasonal discounts, OTA rate parity |
| **Place** | **Place** | Multi-channel distribution: GDS, OTAs, direct web booking, physical travel agencies |
| **Promotion** | **Promotion** | Integrated marketing communications, digital storytelling, social media influencers, travel expos, FAM tours |
| — | **People** | Frontline staff, tour guides, concierge, internal marketing, service culture |
| — | **Process** | Customer journey blueprinting, check-in/check-out simplicity, boarding procedures, complaint handling |
| — | **Physical Evidence** | Hotel architecture, interior ambiance, clean aircraft cabins, uniform grooming, brand collateral, signage |

**Product.** In tourism, the “product” is a **bundle of tangible and intangible elements** assembled into an experience: transport, accommodation, attractions, and services. **Branding** differentiates the bundle (a Ritz-Carlton stay promises a different experience from a Holiday Inn stay), and **packaging** combines components into purchasable itineraries (a “7-night Sundarbans and tea-garden package”). Kotler et al. emphasise that the **core product** (the benefit sought—rest, adventure, learning) must be distinguished from the **actual product** (the hotel, the flight, the tour) and the **augmented product** (the extras—loyalty points, concierge service, free cancellation).

**Price.** Tourism pricing is **dynamic and yield-oriented**. **Yield management** adjusts prices in real time to match supply and demand, filling seats and rooms at prices the market will bear while maximising **RevPAR** (Chapter 2). **Seasonal discounts** and **shoulder-season packages** smooth demand (Chapter 4). Price is also a **quality signal**: in the absence of pre-purchase inspection (intangibility), travellers often infer quality from price, which is why **discounting can erode brand equity**.

**Place.** Distribution is **multi-channel**: **GDSs** for corporate and complex itineraries, **OTAs** for reach and price transparency, **direct web booking** for margin control and customer data, and **physical agencies** for advice and high-touch service (Chapter 2). **Channel conflict** arises when direct and indirect channels compete on price; **channel managers** synchronise rates and inventory to manage this conflict.

**Promotion.** **Integrated marketing communications (IMC)** ensure that advertising, public relations, social media, content marketing, and personal selling deliver a **consistent message**. **Digital storytelling** and **influencer marketing** are particularly powerful in tourism because the product is **experiential and visual**: travellers rely on **user-generated content and peer reviews** to reduce the **perceived risk** of an intangible purchase. **Travel expos and trade shows** remain important for B2B relationships, and **familiarisation (FAM) tours** for journalists and influencers generate earned media.

**People.** In services, **the employee is the product**. A rude receptionist or an uninformed guide can destroy a meticulously designed experience. **Internal marketing**—treating employees as internal customers, training them, and rewarding service excellence—is therefore a **strategic marketing function**, not merely an HR function. A **service culture** in which frontline staff are empowered to resolve problems is a source of **competitive advantage** that is difficult to imitate.

**Process.** **Service blueprinting** maps the customer journey—search, booking, arrival, check-in, stay, checkout, post-stay—and identifies **pain points and failure points**. **Check-in/check-out simplicity**, **flight boarding procedures**, and **complaint-handling protocols** are process designs that determine **satisfaction and loyalty**. Process standardisation improves **consistency** but must leave room for **personalisation**, which is increasingly expected by modern travellers.

**Physical Evidence.** Because services are intangible, customers seek **tangible cues** to judge quality: **hotel architecture, interior ambiance, cleanliness of aircraft cabins, uniform grooming, signage, and brand collateral**. Physical evidence **reduces perceived risk** and **communicates positioning**: a minimalist lobby signals modernity; a heritage property signals tradition. In digital environments, the **website and app interface** function as physical evidence, shaping first impressions before any human contact.

### 5.1.3 Unique Characteristics of Services: The IHIP Framework

The **IHIP framework**—**Intangibility, Heterogeneity (Variability), Inseparability, and Perishability**—describes the distinctive challenges of marketing services.

**Table 5.2: Tourism Service Challenges (IHIP) and Strategic Marketing Solutions**

| **IHIP Characteristic** | **Challenge** | **Strategic Marketing Solution** |
|---|---|---|
| **Intangibility** | Services cannot be seen, touched, or tested before purchase; perceived risk is high | Use **physical evidence** (photos, virtual tours, brochures); offer **guarantees** and **free cancellation**; build **social proof** through reviews and influencer content; provide **trial experiences** (VR previews, sample stays) |
| **Inseparability** | Production and consumption occur simultaneously; the customer co-creates the experience | Train and empower **frontline staff**; design **service blueprints** that manage customer participation; use **self-service technologies** (kiosks, apps) to reduce dependence on staff availability |
| **Variability (Heterogeneity)** | Service quality varies by employee, time, and customer; consistency is difficult | Standardise **processes** and **training**; implement **quality assurance** and **mystery shopping**; use **scripts** for routine interactions while allowing **empowerment** for personalisation |
| **Perishability** | Services cannot be stored; unsold inventory (rooms, seats) is lost forever | Use **yield management** and **dynamic pricing**; **overbook** strategically; develop **off-peak demand** through pricing and product diversification; **shift demand** to shoulder seasons |

The IHIP framework is not a counsel of despair but a **design agenda**: each characteristic points to a **specific marketing response**. The most successful tourism firms are those that have **institutionalised** these responses—turning intangibility into **brand trust**, variability into **reliable standards**, inseparability into **employee engagement**, and perishability into **revenue management**.

## 5.2 Market Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning (STP) & Marketing Planning

### 5.2.1 Market Segmentation

**Segmentation** divides a heterogeneous market into **homogeneous subgroups** that respond similarly to marketing stimuli. Tourism uses four principal bases:

**Geographic segmentation** divides markets by **country, region, climate, or distance**. Source-market segmentation is fundamental to destination marketing: the needs and expectations of a Japanese traveller differ from those of an Indian or a British traveller, and marketing budgets must be allocated across **source markets** accordingly.

**Demographic segmentation** divides markets by **age, income, education, gender, family life-cycle stage, and occupation** (Chapter 3). Demographic variables are **easy to measure** but **weak predictors** of behaviour on their own—two 40-year-olds with the same income may seek radically different holidays.

**Psychographic segmentation** divides markets by **values, interests, personality, and lifestyle**. The **VALS framework** (Values, Attitudes, and Lifestyles) classifies consumers into groups such as **Innovators, Thinkers, Achievers, Experiencers, Believers, Strivers, Makers, and Survivors**, each with distinctive travel preferences. **Plog’s allocentric–psychocentric continuum** (Chapter 3) is a tourism-specific psychographic segmentation tool.

**Behavioural segmentation** divides markets by **purpose of visit** (leisure, business, VFR, MICE), **booking channel** (OTA, direct, agency), **spend level** (budget, mid-market, luxury), **usage rate**, **loyalty status**, and **benefits sought** (rest, adventure, learning, status).

### 5.2.2 Selecting Target Markets

Once segments are identified, the destination or firm must choose a **targeting strategy**.

**Undifferentiated (mass) marketing** treats the whole market as a single segment and offers one offer to all. It is **cost-efficient** but vulnerable to **specialised competitors** and increasingly ill-suited to a fragmenting tourism market.

**Differentiated marketing** targets **several segments** with **distinct offers** for each—a destination may market **cultural circuits** to European travellers, **beach holidays** to domestic families, and **MICE facilities** to corporate clients. It **maximises reach** but **raises costs** and risks **brand dilution**.

**Concentrated (niche) marketing** targets **a single segment** with a **specialised offer**—a **wellness retreat**, a **birdwatching lodge**, or a **Buddhist pilgrimage circuit**. It is **efficient** for resource-constrained destinations and builds **deep expertise**, but it is **vulnerable to segment-specific shocks** (a health crisis that suppresses wellness travel, a political crisis in a source market).

The choice depends on **resources, market maturity, competitive intensity, and risk tolerance**. Most destinations adopt a **hybrid strategy**: a **core target market** for volume, plus **niche segments** for yield and differentiation.

### 5.2.3 Brand Positioning

**Positioning** is the **act of designing the destination’s offer and image** so that it occupies a **distinctive and valued place** in the target customer’s mind. A positioning statement answers three questions: **Who is the target? What is the point of difference? Why should the customer believe it?** (“For culturally curious European travellers, Bangladesh offers an authentic, uncrowded South Asian experience—the world’s longest beach, the Sundarbans, and living Buddhist and Islamic heritage—delivered with genuine hospitality.”)

**Perceptual mapping** is the visual tool of positioning. It plots the destination against **two or more dimensions** that matter to the target market—for example, **“cultural depth” versus “ease of access,”** or **“authenticity” versus “comfort.”** A destination’s position is determined by **customer perceptions**, not by the destination’s own claims; repositioning requires **changing the offer** (product, price, place, people, process, physical evidence) and **communicating the change** consistently over time.

### 5.2.4 The Master Tourism Marketing Plan

A **master tourism marketing plan** is the **operational document** that translates strategy into action. Its standard structure is:

1. **Executive summary** — a one-page synthesis of the plan’s rationale, objectives, and budget.
2. **Situational audit** — internal analysis (resources, capabilities, brand equity) and external analysis (PESTLE, competitor analysis, market trends).
3. **Market analysis** — segmentation, target market profiles, demand forecasts, and customer insights.
4. **Marketing objectives** — **SMART** (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) targets for arrivals, expenditure, market share, brand awareness, and satisfaction.
5. **Marketing mix strategies** — the **7Ps** deployed to achieve the objectives, with **channel strategy**, **pricing architecture**, **communication plan**, and **service design**.
6. **Action implementation schedules** — timelines, responsibilities, and milestones.
7. **Budget allocation** — resources by market, channel, and activity, with **expected return on marketing investment**.
8. **Control and KPI tracking** — the metrics (arrivals, visitor-nights, RevPAR, conversion rates, Net Promoter Score, cost per acquisition) and the **review cadence** through which the plan is monitored and revised.

The plan is **cyclical, not linear**: control data feed back into the situational audit and market analysis, and the plan is **updated annually or when structural conditions change**.

### 5.2.5 Joint and Collaborative Marketing Efforts

Tourism marketing is often **collaborative** because no single firm or agency can promote a destination alone.

**Public–private partnerships (PPPs)** pool government resources (brand campaigns, infrastructure, regulatory facilitation) with private-sector resources (product development, distribution, customer relationships). **Regional Destination Marketing Organisations (DMOs)** coordinate marketing across multiple local government areas, achieving **scale and coherence** that individual destinations cannot. **Co-op advertising campaigns**—for example, an airline and a hotel chain jointly promoting a destination—**share costs and leverage complementary assets**: the airline has **reach and booking data**, the hotel has **product and loyalty relationships**.

The governance challenge is **free-riding**: firms benefit from destination promotion without contributing to its cost. **Mandatory membership levies, transparent benefit-sharing, and measurable KPIs** are mechanisms for sustaining collaboration.

## 5.3 The Future of Tourism in the Third Millennium

### 5.3.1 Tourism in the Third Millennium: From Mass Consumption to Transformative Travel

Twentieth-century tourism was dominated by **mass industrial consumption**: standardised packages, resort enclaves, and volume-driven economics. Third-millennium tourism is shifting toward **customised, meaningful, and transformative travel**. The WEF’s 2025 insight report projects travel and tourism to contribute **$16 trillion to global GDP by 2034**—more than **11% of the world economy**—and anticipates **30 billion tourist visits worldwide**. But the report emphasises that this growth comes with **profound responsibilities**: the sector must move beyond **impact mitigation** to **regenerative practices** that actively restore natural systems. The **World Tourism Cities Federation** observes that since 2025 the global tourism economy has entered a stage of **structural adjustment and quality enhancement**, with **resilient growth coexisting with structural risks**.

**Table 5.3: Old Realities of 20th-Century Tourism versus New Realities of 21st-Century Third-Millennium Tourism**

| Dimension | Old Realities (20th Century) | New Realities (21st Century) |
|---|---|---|
| **Dominant model** | Mass industrial consumption; standardised packages | Customised, meaningful, transformative travel |
| **Success metric** | Visitor headcount; gross arrivals | Sustainable, regenerative, inclusive value; net benefit |
| **Environmental stance** | Externalised costs; “sun, sea, sand” at scale | Decarbonisation, carbon accounting, regenerative practices |
| **Technology** | GDS, early internet, call-centre booking | AI concierges, predictive analytics, biometrics, IoT, blockchain |
| **Consumer values** | Convenience, value for money, status | Authenticity, ethics, wellness, social sharing, personalisation |
| **Work–leisure boundary** | Separate; fixed holiday periods | Blurred; remote work, digital nomadism, bleisure |
| **Risk environment** | Localised, episodic | Systemic: pandemics, climate extremes, geopolitical volatility |

### 5.3.2 The World of Tourism in 2021 and Beyond: Structural Disruption and Resilient Recovery

The COVID-19 pandemic was the **most severe shock** in tourism history, collapsing international arrivals and exposing the **fragility of over-specialised economies** (Chapter 1). Recovery has been **resilient but uneven**: the Asia-Pacific region continues to account for a major share of the increase in global tourism activity, supported by **air capacity recovery, visa facilitation, and the spending power of the emerging middle class**, while **geopolitical disruptions** in the Middle East have temporarily slowed recovery in that region. The pandemic accelerated three structural shifts: **health security** (sanitation protocols, health certifications, flexible cancellation), **contactless service delivery** (mobile check-in, biometric boarding, digital keys), and **domestic tourism** as a **stabiliser** during international disruptions. The lesson is not that tourism is **doomed to fragility**, but that **resilience must be designed**—through **diversified source markets, flexible capacity, digital infrastructure, and crisis-management protocols**.

### 5.3.3 Nature of Future Growth: Sustainable, Regenerative, and Inclusive

Future growth is increasingly measured **not by raw visitor numbers** but by **sustainable, regenerative, and inclusive value**. The WEF report calls for **regenerative practices** that **restore natural systems** rather than merely reducing harm, and for **local capacity-building and SME support** so that tourism benefits **host communities** rather than extracting value from them. **Inclusivity** encompasses **accessibility** (travel for persons with disabilities), **affordability** (social tourism, Chapter 3), and **gender and youth participation** in tourism employment and entrepreneurship. The strategic implication is that **destination marketing must be integrated with destination management**: promotion that fills capacity beyond **carrying limits** (Chapter 4) is **value-destroying** in the long run, even if it maximises short-term arrivals.

### 5.3.4 Leisure, Tourism, and Society in the Third Millennium

Changing **leisure values** are reshaping demand. **Four-day workweek experiments** in several countries are creating **longer weekends and more frequent short breaks**, altering the traditional **annual holiday** pattern. **Remote work and digital nomadism** have decoupled **work from place**, creating **long-stay, shoulder-season demand** in destinations with good connectivity and affordable living costs. The search for **wellness and mindfulness** has moved from niche to mainstream: travellers increasingly seek **restoration, digital detox, and transformational experiences** rather than passive consumption. The **“Vitamin T”** concept—travel as a source of **time, tranquillity, and transformation**—captures this shift. Destinations that can supply **wellness infrastructure, nature access, and slow-travel experiences** are well positioned for this segment.

## 5.4 Forces Impacting the Future of Tourism

### 5.4.1 Technological Disruption

**Artificial intelligence** has evolved from a **localised tool** into a **core force reshaping the tourism value chain**. **Generative-AI itinerary planning**, **real-time multilingual interaction**, and **immersive virtual experiences** are fundamentally changing **travellers’ decision journeys and consumption experiences**. **AI travel concierges** provide **personalised recommendations** at scale; **predictive analytics** improve **demand forecasting and revenue management**; **smart airport facial biometrics** accelerate **security and boarding**; **IoT in smart hotel rooms** enables **energy efficiency and personalised ambiance**; and **blockchain** offers **secure identity, loyalty interoperability, and transparent supply chains**. The WEF calls for **ethical frameworks** to ensure **equitable access and data privacy**, and for **human verification** in safety-critical AI applications.

### 5.4.2 Demographic Transitions

**Ageing populations** in Europe and East Asia—the **“Silver Tsunami”** —are generating **stable, sustained demand** for **wellness tourism, immersive slow travel, and accessible travel**. The **explosion of the middle class in India and China** is reshaping **global travel patterns and expectations**, creating **new source markets** with distinctive preferences. **Generation Z and Millennials** demand **ethical, eco-conscious travel**, prioritise **social sharing and personalised experiences**, and are **less tolerant of greenwashing**. The **divergence between younger and older travellers** places **greater demands on the precision of tourism product supply**: a destination cannot serve both with a single generic offer.

### 5.4.3 Environmental Realities and Climate Change

**Rising sea levels** threaten **coastal resorts**, and **extreme weather events** are becoming **more frequent**, elevating **tourism safety from a localised concern to a primary factor in destination choice**. The **decarbonisation of aviation** depends on **Sustainable Aviation Fuels (SAF)**, which face **land-use trade-offs** (palm-oil-derived SAF could displace tropical rainforest). **Eco-taxation** and **consumer flight-shame movements** are creating **reputational and cost pressures** on carbon-intensive travel. **Carbon accounting and ecological carrying capacity assessments** are increasingly becoming **baseline considerations for destination access and development**.

### 5.4.4 Safety, Security, and Health Emergencies

Tourism is entering a period of **structurally higher volatility**, driven by the **convergence of geopolitical instability, climate change, health crises, and technological risks**. **Crisis-management protocols**, **biological safety certifications**, and **geopolitical resilience** are no longer **peripheral concerns** but **core components of destination competitiveness**. Travellers increasingly **factor safety into destination choice**, and destinations that **communicate safety credibly**—through **transparent advisories, health infrastructure, and crisis preparedness**—enjoy a **competitive advantage**.

**Table 5.4: Forces Impacting Future Tourism**

| **Force** | **Driving Factors** | **Future Manifestation in Travel** |
|---|---|---|
| **Technological disruption** | AI, biometrics, IoT, blockchain, VR/AR | AI concierges, predictive pricing, seamless biometric journeys, immersive previews, smart rooms |
| **Demographic transitions** | Ageing societies; emerging middle classes in India and China; Gen Z/Millennial values | Silver travel and wellness; new source-market flows; ethical and personalised demand |
| **Environmental realities** | Climate change, sea-level rise, decarbonisation, SAF, eco-taxation | Climate-resilient destinations; low-carbon travel; carbon labelling; flight-shame pressure |
| **Safety and security** | Geopolitical volatility, health emergencies, extreme weather | Crisis protocols, health certifications, safety-based destination choice |

## 5.5 The Changing Nature of Tourism Products, New Realities & New Horizons

### 5.5.1 Evolving Tourism Products

**Space tourism.** **Sub-orbital space tourism** has moved from **conceptual demonstrations** to **regular commercial operations**, with **reusable launch systems and hybrid spaceplanes** lowering operational complexity and enabling **higher flight cadence**. The global space tourism market was estimated at **$1.03 billion in 2025**, growing to **$1.32 billion in 2026** at a **28.6% CAGR**, with **sub-orbital flights accounting for 76.8% of revenue** in 2025. Space tourism remains a **niche, high-cost segment**, but its **technological spillovers** (materials, life-support, remote operations) and **brand value** for destinations and operators are significant.

**Virtual and augmented reality (VR/AR) tourism.** **VR previews** allow travellers to **“try before they book”** hotel and destination experiences; **AR guides** provide **in-destination recommendations and interpretation**. The **metaverse** offers **immersive virtual travel** that can **substitute for physical travel** in some contexts (accessibility, pre-visit familiarisation) and **stimulate demand** in others (previewing destinations that travellers then visit physically). The strategic question is whether VR/AR **cannibalises** or **complements** physical travel; evidence suggests it is primarily **complementary**, reducing **perceived risk** and **enhancing anticipation**.

**Wellness, medical, and transformational tourism.** Wellness tourism—**spa, yoga, detox, mindfulness, nature immersion**—has moved from niche to mainstream, driven by **ageing populations, stress, and the search for meaning**. **Medical tourism** (procedures combined with recovery travel) is a **high-yield segment** with **quality and regulatory requirements**. **Transformational tourism**—travel explicitly undertaken for **personal growth, perspective change, or spiritual development**—overlaps with **wellness and cultural tourism** and is particularly attractive to **experience-seeking, high-spend travellers**.

**“Bleisure” travel.** The **blending of business and leisure**—extending a business trip into a personal vacation, or working remotely from a leisure destination—has been **consolidated by remote work and digital nomadism**, with **dedicated visas and connected infrastructure** supporting professionals who **work while travelling**. Destinations with **good connectivity, affordable long-stay accommodation, and vibrant lifestyle amenities** are well positioned for this segment.

**Culinary and agro-tourism.** **Food and drink** are increasingly **primary travel motivators** (Chapter 2), and **agro-tourism**—farm stays, harvest experiences, food trails—connects travellers to **rural livelihoods and local supply chains**. These products support **local producers, disperse visitors beyond honeypots, and extend dwell time**, and they align with the **regenerative and inclusive growth** agenda.

### 5.5.2 New Realities and New Horizons

**Overtourism versus undertourism.** The post-pandemic recovery has produced a **divergence**: some destinations face **overcrowding and community backlash**, while others remain **below pre-pandemic visitation** and struggle to **restore livelihoods**. The strategic response is **not to maximise arrivals everywhere**, but to **match demand to capacity** through **dispersal, pricing, timed entry, and product diversification** (Chapter 4).

**Destination economic resilience.** Resilience requires **diversified source markets**, **domestic demand stabilisers**, **flexible capacity**, **digital infrastructure**, and **crisis-management protocols**. Destinations that depend on a **single source market, a single season, or a single product** are **structurally vulnerable** (Chapter 1).

**Strategic imperatives for emerging destinations.** For developing-world destinations—including Bangladesh—the imperatives are: **build a consistent national brand**; **invest in digital marketing and data systems**; **develop **community-based and regenerative products**; **strengthen **air connectivity and visa facilitation**; **professionalise **service standards and workforce skills**; and **integrate **sustainability into destination management** rather than treating it as a marketing slogan.

## 5.6 Bangladesh Strategic Marketing Vignette: “Beautiful Bangladesh”

**Origins and branding logic.** The **“Beautiful Bangladesh”** campaign was launched in **2008** as a **national destination brand**, and it was **activated globally** through the **“School of Life” television commercial**, produced for the **Bangladesh Tourism Board (BTB)** and aired during the **2011 ICC Cricket World Cup**, which Bangladesh co-hosted. The commercial was **the first of its kind in Bangladesh** and **won the award for the third-best television commercial at the Zagreb Film Festival**. The campaign’s **branding logic** was to **reframe Bangladesh** from a **“problem” destination** (flood, poverty, political instability) to a **“beautiful, authentic, and welcoming”** destination—a **counter-narrative** built on **natural beauty, cultural richness, and human warmth**.

**Digital marketing and content strategy.** BTB has produced **more than 500 audio-visual contents**—including **28 thematic documentaries** of **three to six minutes**—disseminated through **Facebook, YouTube, X, LinkedIn, and Instagram**, alongside the **Beautiful Bangladesh website** showcasing destinations and products. The campaign has also used **photography and videography competitions** to generate **user-generated content**, and **familiarisation (FAM) tours** for **foreign diplomats, travel writers, influencers, bloggers, and journalists**. BTB participates in **international tourism fairs**, organises **roadshows and B2B sessions**, and conducts **digital campaigns in key source markets with support from Bangladeshi missions abroad**.

**Target source markets.** The campaign’s international targeting has been **broad and undifferentiated**, but industry stakeholders have urged **focus on four to five established source markets**, with **Japan** cited as a priority because of its **existing visitor flows**. **Regional markets**—**India, Sri Lanka, Nepal, and Bhutan**—have been engaged through **B2B meetings** involving tour operators, hotels, resorts, airlines, and tourist vessel operators. The **Bangladesh diaspora** and **expatriate communities** are also engaged as **promotional channels**.

**Strategic assessment.** The campaign has **achieved visibility** but has been criticised for **weak positioning**: “Beautiful Bangladesh” is **not as prominent as neighbouring country brands**, and earlier initiatives **failed to establish consistent positioning**. Stakeholders identify **poor presence at global tourism events**, **negative airport first impressions**, and **travel advisories** as **constraints**, and recommend **separate tourist desks at immigration, a national tourism data system, and a tax rebate for tourism businesses**.

**Opportunities: The Sundarbans, Sylhet, and Buddhist heritage.** The **Sundarbans**—the **world’s largest mangrove forest and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, home to the Royal Bengal Tiger**—is an **iconic natural asset** with **global recognisability**. **Sylhet’s tea gardens and hilly landscapes** are **perfect for eco-tourism**, and the **52 tea gardens** offer a **distinctive cultural landscape**. **Buddhist heritage circuits**—linking **Somapura Mahavihara (Paharpur)**, **Chittagong, and Cox’s Bazar**—can be developed in **partnership with Sri Lanka**, whose **Ceylon tea reputation** offers a **natural synergy** with **Bangladesh’s tea estates**. These assets align with **high-growth segments**: **eco-tourism, cultural tourism, wellness, and slow travel**. The strategic imperative is to **move from generic “beauty” branding** to **specific, experience-led propositions**: “**Sundarbans: the world’s largest mangrove wilderness**”; “**Sylhet: tea country and living traditions**”; “**Buddhist Bengal: a living heritage circuit**.”

## 5.7 Strategic Marketing Plan Template: Six-Step Destination Marketing Action Plan

The following **fillable template** is designed for an **emerging tourist destination**.

| **Step** | **Component** | **Guiding Questions** | **Output** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Situational and market audit** | What are our resources, capabilities, and current brand perceptions? Who are our competitors? What are the PESTLE trends? | SWOT summary; competitor map; market trend brief |
| **2** | **Segmentation and targeting** | Which segments exist? Which are growing, profitable, and aligned with our values? Which can we serve authentically? | Segment profiles; target market selection (core + niche) |
| **3** | **Positioning and value proposition** | What is our distinctive point of difference? Why should the target believe it? How will we prove it? | Positioning statement; perceptual map; brand promise |
| **4** | **Marketing mix (7Ps) design** | What product, price, place, promotion, people, process, and physical evidence will deliver the promise? | 7Ps action plan; channel strategy; communication plan |
| **5** | **Budget, timeline, and responsibilities** | What will it cost? Who does what, and when? What is the expected return? | Budget table; Gantt-style timeline; RACI matrix |
| **6** | **Control and KPI tracking** | Which metrics will tell us whether we are succeeding? How often will we review and revise? | KPI dashboard (arrivals, visitor-nights, spend, awareness, NPS); review calendar |

The template is **iterative**: control data from Step 6 feed back into Step 1, and the plan is **revised annually** or when **structural conditions change**.

### 5.8.1 Short-Answer Conceptual Questions (with Model Answers)

**Question 1. Define tourism marketing and explain why the 7Ps framework is more appropriate than the 4Ps for hospitality and tourism.**

**Model Answer.** **Tourism marketing** is the **managerial and societal process** of identifying, anticipating, and satisfying visitor needs and wants through the **creation, communication, and delivery of valued experiences**, profitably where appropriate and sustainably in all cases. The **7Ps** framework is more appropriate than the 4Ps because tourism products are **services**, and services require **three additional marketing variables**: **People** (frontline staff are inseparable from the experience and determine quality), **Process** (the customer journey—booking, check-in, boarding—shapes satisfaction), and **Physical Evidence** (architecture, ambiance, and cleanliness reduce the perceived risk of an intangible purchase). The 4Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion) capture only the **tangible and transactional** dimensions; the 7Ps capture the **experiential and relational** dimensions that are decisive in hospitality and tourism.

---

**Question 2. Explain the IHIP characteristics of services and provide one marketing solution for each.**

**Model Answer.** **IHIP** stands for **Intangibility, Heterogeneity (Variability), Inseparability, and Perishability**. **Intangibility**: services cannot be inspected before purchase; the solution is to provide **physical evidence** (photos, virtual tours) and **social proof** (reviews, influencer content) to reduce perceived risk. **Heterogeneity**: service quality varies by employee and occasion; the solution is **standardised processes and training**, supplemented by **empowerment** for personalisation. **Inseparability**: production and consumption occur simultaneously, and the customer co-creates the experience; the solution is to **train and engage frontline staff** and design **service blueprints** that manage customer participation. **Perishability**: unsold inventory (rooms, seats) cannot be stored; the solution is **yield management, dynamic pricing, strategic overbooking, and off-peak demand development**.

---

**Question 3. Distinguish between undifferentiated, differentiated, and concentrated targeting strategies, and give a tourism example of each.**

**Model Answer.** **Undifferentiated (mass) marketing** treats the whole market as one segment and offers a **single proposition** to all—for example, a **beach resort** that markets “sun, sea, and sand” to every traveller. **Differentiated marketing** targets **several segments** with **distinct offers**—for example, a **destination** that markets **cultural circuits** to European travellers, **family beach holidays** to domestic visitors, and **MICE facilities** to corporate clients. **Concentrated (niche) marketing** targets **one segment** with a **specialised offer**—for example, a **birdwatching lodge** that serves only **ornithology enthusiasts**. The choice depends on **resources, competitive intensity, and risk tolerance**: undifferentiated is **efficient but vulnerable**; differentiated **maximises reach but raises costs**; concentrated **builds deep expertise but is exposed to segment-specific shocks**.

---

**Question 4. What is a perceptual map, and how is it used in destination positioning?**

**Model Answer.** A **perceptual map** is a **visual tool** that plots a destination (or brand) against **two or more dimensions** that are **salient to the target market**—for example, **“cultural depth” versus “ease of access,”** or **“authenticity” versus “comfort.”** It is used in positioning to **(i) diagnose** the destination’s current perceived position relative to competitors; **(ii) identify gaps**—positions that are **valued by customers but unoccupied** by competitors; and **(iii) guide repositioning**—changing the **offer (7Ps)** and **communication** to move the destination toward a **more favourable position**. Perceptual maps are based on **customer perceptions, not objective attributes**, which is why **market research** is essential to construct them.

---

**Question 5. Outline the structure of a master tourism marketing plan.**

**Model Answer.** A master tourism marketing plan typically contains: **(1) an executive summary**; **(2) a situational audit** (internal and external analysis, including PESTLE and competitor analysis); **(3) a market analysis** (segmentation, target profiles, demand forecasts); **(4) marketing objectives** (SMART targets for arrivals, expenditure, awareness, and satisfaction); **(5) marketing mix strategies** (the 7Ps, including channel, pricing, and communication plans); **(6) action implementation schedules** (timelines and responsibilities); **(7) a budget allocation** (resources by market, channel, and activity); and **(8) control and KPI tracking** (metrics, review cadence, and revision mechanisms). The plan is **cyclical**: control data feed back into the audit and market analysis, and the plan is updated as conditions change.

---

**Question 6. Explain the difference between “sustainable” and “regenerative” tourism, and why the distinction matters for marketing.**

**Model Answer.** **Sustainable tourism** seeks to **minimise negative impacts** and **balance economic, social, and environmental objectives**—it is about **doing less harm**. **Regenerative tourism** goes further: it seeks to **actively restore and enhance** natural and social systems—it is about **doing more good**. The distinction matters for marketing because **consumers are increasingly sceptical of “sustainable” claims** that amount to **greenwashing**. **Regenerative** propositions—**reforestation-linked stays, community-owned enterprises, biodiversity-positive experiences**—offer **verifiable, story-rich differentiation** that aligns with **Gen Z and Millennial values** and with the **WEF’s call for regenerative practices** that **restore natural systems** rather than merely mitigate impact. Marketing that claims regeneration must be **backed by measurable outcomes**, or it will face the same credibility crisis as earlier sustainability claims.

### 5.8.2 Broad/Analytical Essay Questions (with Full Structural Outlines)

**Essay Question 1. “The 7Ps framework is not merely an extension of the 4Ps; it is a different way of thinking about marketing.” Critically evaluate this statement with reference to hospitality and tourism services.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** State the thesis: the 7Ps **adds variables** (People, Process, Physical Evidence) but also **shifts the logic** of marketing from **transaction** to **experience** and from **product-centric** to **service-dominant**.
- **Section 1: The 4Ps and their limits.** The 4Ps were developed for **tangible goods**; they assume that the **product can be standardised, inspected, and stored**, and that the **customer is a passive recipient**. These assumptions fail in services.
- **Section 2: The additional Ps.** **People** makes **employee behaviour** a marketing variable; **Process** makes the **customer journey** a design object; **Physical Evidence** makes the **environment** a communicator. These are not **add-ons** but **constitutive** of the service experience.
- **Section 3: IHIP and the service-dominant logic.** Intangibility, inseparability, variability, and perishability require **different marketing capabilities**—**trust-building, co-creation, standardisation-with-personalisation, and revenue management**—that the 4Ps do not address.
- **Section 4: Implications for tourism and hospitality.** A hotel that manages only the 4Ps (room, price, channel, advertising) but neglects **staff engagement (People), check-in design (Process), and lobby ambiance (Physical Evidence)** will fail to deliver the promised experience.
- **Conclusion.** The 7Ps is **not a longer list** but a **different paradigm**: it locates **value creation** in the **encounter** between customer and provider, not in the **object** exchanged. This is the appropriate paradigm for tourism and hospitality.

---

**Essay Question 2. “The forces reshaping tourism—technology, demography, environment, and security—are not separate; they interact.” Discuss with examples.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** State the thesis: the four forces are **interdependent**, and their **interaction** produces **non-linear, compounding effects** that destinations must anticipate.
- **Section 1: Technology × demography.** **AI and personalisation** are most valuable for **fragmented, value-diverse markets** (Gen Z vs. Silver travellers); **digital nomadism** is a **technology-enabled demographic shift** that creates **new demand for long-stay, connected accommodation**.
- **Section 2: Environment × security.** **Climate change** produces **extreme weather events** that are **security and safety crises**; **decarbonisation policies** (eco-taxation, SAF mandates) **raise costs** and **reshape competitiveness**.
- **Section 3: Technology × environment.** **AI and IoT** enable **energy efficiency and carbon accounting**; **blockchain** can support **transparent sustainability certification**; **VR/AR** can **substitute for some physical travel**, reducing emissions, but may also **stimulate demand** for the physical experience.
- **Section 4: Demography × security.** **Ageing populations** are **more vulnerable to health risks**, raising the **safety bar** for destinations; **younger travellers** are **more sensitive to geopolitical and ethical issues**, making **safety and values** part of the **destination brand**.
- **Section 5: Implications for strategy.** Destinations must adopt **integrated, scenario-based planning** rather than **siloed responses**; **resilience** is the **capacity to absorb shocks across all four domains simultaneously**.
- **Conclusion.** The forces are **a system, not a list**. The most resilient destinations are those that **treat them as interconnected** and build **adaptive capacity** accordingly.

---

**Essay Question 3. “Mass tourism is dead; long live mass tourism.” Critically evaluate the claim that tourism has fundamentally transformed in the third millennium.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** Acknowledge the **transformative rhetoric** (customisation, regeneration, bleisure) but interrogate the **evidence**: has mass tourism **disappeared**, or has it **evolved**?
- **Section 1: The case for transformation.** **Customisation and personalisation**; **regenerative and sustainable imperatives**; **AI and digital distribution**; **remote work and bleisure**; **experience-seeking and wellness**; **the WEF’s projection of 30 billion tourist visits by 2034**—a **scale that implies mass tourism persists**.
- **Section 2: The case for continuity.** **Volume growth** continues; **standardised packages** remain dominant in many markets; **resort enclaves** and **cruise mega-ships** are **mass products**; **price-sensitive domestic tourism** in emerging markets is **mass tourism by another name**.
- **Section 3: The synthesis.** Mass tourism has **not died**; it has **fragmented and been re-engineered**. The **mass market** is now **segmented into micro-markets**, and **sustainability** has become a **condition of competitiveness** rather than a **niche preference**.
- **Section 4: Implications for destinations.** The strategic task is **not to choose between mass and niche** but to **manage volume within carrying capacity**, **diversify products**, and **use technology to personalise at scale**.
- **Conclusion.** The transformation is **real but uneven**: **consumption patterns** have changed more than **volume**, and **sustainability rhetoric** has outpaced **sustainability practice**. The third millennium is **not post-mass tourism**; it is **mass tourism under new constraints**.

---

**Essay Question 4. “Bangladesh has a brand, but not a positioning.” Evaluate the ‘Beautiful Bangladesh’ campaign and propose a repositioning strategy for the Sundarbans, Sylhet, and Buddhist heritage circuits.**

**Structural Outline.**

- **Introduction.** Distinguish **brand** (a name and symbol) from **positioning** (a distinctive place in the customer’s mind). The campaign has **visibility** but **weak positioning**.
- **Section 1: Achievements of the campaign.** **“School of Life” commercial**, **500+ audio-visual contents**, **social media presence**, **FAM tours**, **B2B sessions**, and **international fair participation**. It has **put Bangladesh on the map** for **authenticity and natural beauty**.
- **Section 2: Weaknesses.** **Generic “beauty” positioning** is **undifferentiated**; **“Beautiful Bangladesh” is not as prominent as neighbouring brands**; **poor presence at global events**; **negative airport experience**; **travel advisories**; **lack of a national tourism data system**.
- **Section 3: Repositioning logic.** Move from **generic beauty** to **specific, experience-led propositions** anchored in **distinctive assets**: **Sundarbans** (wilderness, tiger, mangrove ecology), **Sylhet** (tea country, living traditions, eco-tourism), **Buddhist Bengal** (living heritage circuit, partnership with Sri Lanka).
- **Section 4: Marketing mix implications.** **Product**: community-based eco-lodges, tea-trail experiences, Buddhist circuit itineraries. **Price**: premium, fair-trade pricing with **community benefit components**. **Place**: **digital-first distribution**, **OTA partnerships**, **embassy-based promotion**. **Promotion**: **storytelling**, **influencer and journalist FAM tours**, **roadshows in Japan and regional markets**. **People**: **training and professionalisation**. **Process**: **seamless visa and airport arrival**. **Physical evidence**: **interpretive centres, signage, and heritage conservation**.
- **Conclusion.** Bangladesh does not need a **new slogan**; it needs a **focused positioning** and **consistent delivery** across the 7Ps. The assets are **globally distinctive**; the task is to **make them legible** to the right travellers.

### 5.8.4 Applied Marketing Planning Scenarios (with Model Solutions)

**Scenario 1: The Emerging Eco-Destination’s Positioning Dilemma**

*Green Delta* is a district in southern Bangladesh with **mangrove forests, riverine islands, and traditional fishing communities**. It receives **50,000 domestic visitors** per year, mostly **day-trippers**, and **fewer than 1,000 international visitors**. The local government wants to **attract higher-yield international eco-tourists** but has **limited infrastructure**, **no branded accommodation**, and **weak digital presence**.

**Questions:**
(a) Using the STP framework, propose a **segmentation and targeting strategy**.
(b) Draft a **positioning statement** for Green Delta.
(c) Identify the **three most critical 7Ps actions** for the first year.

**Model Solution.**

(a) **Segmentation and targeting.** Segment the international market **psychographically and behaviourally**: **allocentric/experiential travellers** (Chapter 3) who seek **authenticity, nature, and community engagement**; **special-interest segments**—**birdwatchers, photographers, mangrove ecologists, and cultural travellers**; and **regional source markets** (India, Sri Lanka, Nepal) for **short-haul eco-trips**. **Target strategy**: **concentrated (niche) marketing** on **special-interest eco-travellers** from **regional and select long-haul markets**, rather than undifferentiated mass marketing, because infrastructure is limited and the product is **specialised**. The **domestic day-tripper market** should be **retained** as a **volume base** while the **international niche** is developed.

(b) **Positioning statement.** “For **special-interest travellers seeking authentic wilderness and living riverine culture**, **Green Delta** offers **uncrowded mangrove landscapes, community-run eco-lodges, and guided nature immersion**—**a genuine alternative to commercialised eco-resorts**, delivered by **local hosts who are stewards of the ecosystem**.”

(c) **Three critical 7Ps actions.** **(1) Product and Physical Evidence**: develop **two or three community-run eco-lodges** with **basic but high-quality interpretive infrastructure** (trails, signage, birdwatching hides). **(2) People and Process**: train **local guides and homestay hosts** in **interpretation, safety, and hospitality**, and design a **seamless booking-to-arrival process** (online booking, clear meeting points, pre-arrival information). **(3) Promotion and Place**: build a **digital presence** (website, Instagram, birdwatching forums) and **partner with specialist tour operators** and **OTAs** to reach the niche market; use **FAM trips** for **birdwatching journalists and influencers**.

---

**Scenario 2: The Hotel’s Channel and Pricing Strategy**

*Bay View Hotel* is a **150-room, four-star** property in Cox’s Bazar. It currently sells **60% of rooms through OTAs at 20% commission**, **25% direct**, and **15% through corporate accounts**. Occupancy is **70%** and **ADR is $120**. The hotel wants to **increase net RevPAR** and **reduce OTA dependence**.

**Questions:**
(a) Calculate **gross RevPAR** and **net RevPAR** (after OTA commission).
(b) Propose a **channel and pricing strategy** to increase net RevPAR, identifying **risks**.
(c) Explain how **digital marketing** can support the strategy.

**Model Solution.**

(a) **Gross RevPAR** = ADR × Occupancy = $120 × 0.70 = **$84.00**. **OTA commission** = 60% of rooms × $120 × 0.20 = **$14.40 per available room** (0.60 × 120 × 0.20). **Net RevPAR** = $84.00 − $14.40 = **$69.60**.

(b) **Strategy.** **(1) Direct-booking incentives**: offer **loyalty benefits, room upgrades, or flexible cancellation** for direct bookings, shifting **10–15 percentage points** of OTA volume to direct. **(2) Rate parity management**: ensure direct rates are **at least as favourable** as OTA rates, while respecting **contractual parity clauses**. **(3) Corporate account growth**: expand **corporate negotiated rates** (no commission) to raise the **corporate share** from 15% to 20–25%. **(4) Metasearch and retargeting**: invest in **Google Hotel Ads** and **retargeting** to capture **high-intent demand** at **lower acquisition cost** than OTA commission. **Risks**: **reduced OTA visibility** and **search ranking demotion** if direct-booking push is aggressive; **rate-parity disputes**; **campaign costs** that may offset commission savings.

(c) **Digital marketing support.** **Content marketing** (destination guides, virtual tours) drives **organic traffic** to the direct booking engine. **Email marketing** to past guests promotes **repeat direct bookings**. **Social media and influencer partnerships** build **brand preference** that reduces **price comparison** behaviour. **Analytics** track **channel attribution** and **cost per acquisition**, enabling **continuous optimisation** of the channel mix.

## 5.9 Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined tourism marketing and the forces shaping its future.

**Marketing fundamentals.** Tourism marketing is the **managerial and societal process** of creating, communicating, and delivering valued experiences. The **7Ps**—Product, Price, Place, Promotion, People, Process, and Physical Evidence—capture the **experiential and relational** dimensions that the 4Ps miss. The **IHIP characteristics**—Intangibility, Heterogeneity, Inseparability, and Perishability—pose **specific challenges** that require **specific marketing solutions**.

**STP and planning.** Segmentation uses **geographic, demographic, psychographic (VALS), and behavioural** bases. Targeting may be **undifferentiated, differentiated, or concentrated**. **Positioning** is the **distinctive place** a destination occupies in the customer’s mind, visualised through **perceptual maps**. The **master marketing plan** provides a **cyclical structure** from audit to KPI control, and **collaborative marketing** (PPPs, DMOs, co-op campaigns) pools resources across stakeholders.

**Future horizons.** Tourism is shifting from **mass consumption** to **customised, meaningful, and regenerative travel**. The **WEF projects $16 trillion in GDP and 30 billion visits by 2034**, with **regenerative practices** as a condition of sustainable growth. **Technological, demographic, environmental, and security forces** interact to reshape demand and supply. **New products**—space tourism, VR/AR, wellness, bleisure, culinary and agro-tourism—are **expanding the frontiers** of the industry.

**Bangladesh.** The **“Beautiful Bangladesh”** campaign has **achieved visibility** through **digital content, FAM tours, and international events**, but it **lacks focused positioning**. The **Sundarbans, Sylhet tea estates, and Buddhist heritage circuits** offer **globally distinctive assets** that can anchor a **repositioned, experience-led strategy**—provided that **delivery across the 7Ps** matches the promise.

## 5.10 Key Terms Glossary

1. **Tourism marketing:** The managerial and societal process of identifying, anticipating, and satisfying visitor needs through the creation, communication, and delivery of valued experiences.
2. **7Ps:** The extended services marketing mix—Product, Price, Place, Promotion, People, Process, and Physical Evidence.
3. **IHIP:** The four characteristics of services—Intangibility, Heterogeneity (Variability), Inseparability, and Perishability.
4. **Physical evidence:** Tangible cues—architecture, ambiance, cleanliness, uniforms, signage—that communicate service quality and reduce perceived risk.
5. **Service blueprint:** A map of the customer journey that identifies pain points, failure points, and opportunities for process improvement.
6. **Segmentation:** Dividing a heterogeneous market into homogeneous subgroups that respond similarly to marketing stimuli.
7. **VALS:** A psychographic segmentation framework based on Values, Attitudes, and Lifestyles.
8. **Targeting:** The selection of the segment(s) that a destination or firm will serve.
9. **Positioning:** The act of designing the offer and image so that it occupies a distinctive and valued place in the target customer’s mind.
10. **Perceptual map:** A visual tool that plots a destination or brand against dimensions salient to the target market.
11. **Marketing plan:** The operational document that translates strategy into objectives, mix strategies, budgets, and KPI-based control.
12. **Yield management:** Dynamic pricing that adjusts rates in real time to match supply and demand and maximise revenue per available capacity.
13. **Bleisure:** The blending of business travel and personal leisure.
14. **Regenerative tourism:** Tourism that actively restores and enhances natural and social systems, beyond merely minimising harm.
15. **Delphi Technique (in forecasting):** A qualitative method using anonymous, iterative expert rounds to reach consensus (Chapter 4).
16. **Co-op advertising:** Joint promotional campaigns in which suppliers (e.g., airlines and hotels) share costs and leverage complementary assets.
